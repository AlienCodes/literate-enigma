**THE HEALING FOREST**

For most of human history, people lived surrounded by greenery. The modern **shift** in lifestyle—from greenery to concrete jungles—has severed this **organic** connection, with **grave** harm to many people's **mental** health. Could reconnecting with nature reverse this damage? The question may sound novel, but the **theme**—that nature heals the mind—has deep roots, buried in our collective subconsciousness since the dawn of humanity. It has long been a matter of common **intuition** that natural **scenery** helps people **relax**. Yet since these **prior** claims lacked rigorous scientific evidence, they did not **warrant** serious attention. Now, however, that is changing. Key findings on this topic have been **bundle**d together, **compel**ling a fresh look at the issue. Some new **scholarship** suggests that restoring contact with nature is indeed an effective **therapy** for mental health issues. And this approach is already being put into practice: an expert **panel** is working to **implement** nature-based treatments.


A case study from Japan illustrates just how powerful this effect can be. Researchers measured stress hormones in office workers before and after walks through forests rich in **timber**. The results were striking: cortisol levels dropped significantly—changes detectable at the **cell**ular level—and subjects reported fewer **episode**s of anxiety in the following weeks. So impressive were these findings that this practice, known as "forest bathing," has since spread to healthcare **institution**s worldwide. But what explains nature's healing power? Why does it work in this **manner**? The answer, scientists believe, lies in how **exterior** environments rich in greenery **prompt** changes in brain chemistry. The **grace**ful interplay of dappled light and birdsong activates circuits linked to calm—something urban environments simply cannot replicate. City noise, by contrast, can **intrude** on peace, **distort** neural signals, and **preclude** recovery.

Of all groups, **adolescent** populations may see the greatest gains. Young people spend excessive time in front of **screen**s and **struggle** to **cope** with piles of schoolwork—they desperately need relief. To address this, many schools have **supplement**ed playgrounds with greenery and added more PE classes to help **safeguard** students' wellbeing. Some have even introduced additional courses to teach **meditation** and breathing techniques. Yet not everyone benefits equally. Critics point to a wide **gulf** between what evidence shows and what policies deliver: **generous** swathes of green space are more likely to be found in wealthy neighbourhoods, while poorer areas fill with **litter** and concrete. When it comes to closing this gap, the private sector is moving faster—**corporation**s increasingly **lean** toward biophilic office design and are willing to pay real **estate** developers more for it. But public **reform** has yet to catch up: much **outdated** infrastructure remains unrenovated. A clear **blueprint** for greening cities has long existed, yet urban development has not been **orient**ed toward these goals, and many policies have never been properly **implement**ed.

Policy may be stalling, but medicine is forging ahead. Health **maintenance** no longer **overlook**s the healing power that forests provide. **Personalise**d care increasingly includes nature prescriptions, and **on occasion**, a walk beneath the canopy achieves what pills cannot. Those who once sought **vitamin** D from bottles may now find it beneath open sky. None of this means abandoning modern medicine—the question is no longer nature **versus** technology, but how to balance both. But balance does not happen by itself—policymakers must **tilt** investment toward green infrastructure. Perhaps the strongest case for doing so lies not in data, but in instinct. After all, we did not evolve in concrete—and deep down, we have never stopped longing for the green.
