**THE DNA REVOLUTION IN CRIMINAL JUSTICE**

Findings from a recent **inquiry** published in a leading **journal** reveal a stark **dimension** of **judicial** failure: the wrongly **convicted** served an average of 14 years of unjust **imprisonment** before their **sentences** were ruled **invalid**. The Innocence Project—a pioneering legal **initiative**—was created to fight precisely this kind of injustice. When the organisation launched in 1992, its core **notion** was simple: DNA evidence could help **overturn** such **erroneous** verdicts. Three decades on, the results have been **dramatic**—the **figures** speak volumes: the organization has secured the **release** of more than 375 prisoners, ending years of wrongful incarceration.

Modern laboratories can now extract DNA from evidence that older methods could not analyse—a capability that rests on strict safeguards. Laboratory **staff** follow **exceedingly** **rigorous**, **prescribed** protocols **compatible** with legal proceedings, which are designed to prevent **contamination** entirely, ensuring that results **relate** directly and reliably to a suspect's guilt or innocence. Such reliability has attracted **sponsors** including universities, and the effort has proved **fruitful**, revealing **innumerable** cases where authorities had **prosecuted** the wrong person.

The case of Ronald Cotton **exemplifies** the system's tragic flaws. In 1984, Jennifer Thompson, misled by traumatic memory, picked Cotton out of a lineup as her rapist. Her certainty was so absolute that an **attorney**'s defense proved futile, and **deceived** by her unwavering account, the court convicted an innocent man. **Bound** by the wrongful verdict, Cotton spent eleven years in prison, until DNA testing finally revealed the **incredible** truth: another man had committed the crime.

Cotton's case is hardly an isolated one. This **scandal** has echoed worldwide, and **correspondents** have documented the **bitterness** of exonerees and the **adverse** effects on families. In response, **federations** of legal-aid groups across Asia and Europe now **weigh** evidence more carefully, and courts have followed suit, **forging** new partnerships with innocence projects. In some cases, even the true **criminals** have come forward to confess after DNA exposed their guilt—an unexpected act of **mercy** that helped free the wrongly convicted.

Yet the path to DNA-based exoneration is far from smooth, and obstacles still **block** progress: **confidential** files stay sealed, and old evidence often cannot be **retrieved**—samples disappear, storage facilities close, records are lost. Even so, some truths do emerge—not just in America, but across **mainland** Europe and beyond. Ronald Cotton has spoken of **trembling** at the thought that justice might never come, and of the **privilege** of vindication when it finally did. His case **ranks** among the most celebrated, not only for the exoneration itself, but for what followed: Thompson, who wrongly identified him, later became his friend and co-advocate, and their life trajectories have **merged** in pursuit of a common cause—exonerating the innocent.

To dismiss such stories as **ridiculous** outliers ignores the revolution in forensic science—a **benign** force that promises to spare the innocent and condemn the guilty. In **summary**, history has spoken: justice delayed is not justice denied.
