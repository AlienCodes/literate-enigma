# EN: Where They Died, What They Drank

Cholera **erupted** in London's Soho on 31 August 1854. In ten days it **claimed** **upwards of** 500 lives within a 250-yard **radius** of one street corner. **Prevailing** medical opinion **attributed** it to miasma, **foul** air rising from **rotting** **filth**. John Snow, a local **physician** who had **contended** since 1849 that cholera was **swallowed**, not **inhaled**, **instantly** suspected the Broad Street **pump**.

Yet on 3 September its water **harboured** so few **organic** **impurities** that he **hesitated**. **Mortality** records and **residents**' **testimony** proved more **illuminating**: **virtually** all the dead had lived in the pump's **vicinity**. Most of the **exceptions** had **nonetheless** drunk from it.

At his **urging**, the parish's Board of Guardians removed the handle on 8 September, when daily **fatal** cases had already **declined** from 143 to 12. Edmund Parkes, a professor of **clinical** medicine, **interpreted** the same **cluster** as **toxic** **vapour** **diffusing** through the air. Pumps were so **abundant**, he **observed**, that any **outbreak** would probably have had one nearby.

A workhouse for the **needy**, **amid** the dying but **equipped** with its own well, lost a **mere** five of 535 inmates. A **brewery** beside the pump, whose 70-odd **labourers** drew an **allowance** of malt liquor, suffered no **casualties**.

**Conversely**, a **widow** in **distant** Hampstead, who had not **ventured** near Soho for months, had the pump's water **carted** to her daily. She **perished**, as did a visiting **niece** who **sampled** it, back home in "a high and healthy part of Islington". Neither **neighbourhood** had cholera.

Snow's famous map was not **unveiled** until December. It could **chart** **precisely** where people died, but the **verdict** on the pump **hinged** on what they drank.
