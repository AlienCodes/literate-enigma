# EN: Where They Died, What They Drank

Within ten days of 31 August 1854, cholera **claimed** **upwards of** 500 lives in one **overcrowded** corner of Soho. **Orthodox** medicine **blamed** miasma, **foul** air from **filth**; John Snow, a local **physician**, had **maintained** since 1849 that cholera was **swallowed**, not **inhaled**. He **instantly** **suspected** the Broad Street **pump**. Its water looked **innocent**, so he started asking.

From the official **register** he **catalogued** the dead, then **quizzed** their **households**. **Virtually** all had died in the pump's **vicinity**, and most had **consumed** its water. At his **urging** the **handle** was removed on 8 September, when **fatal** cases had **plunged** from 143 a day to 12.

The famous map came **afterwards**, in December. Edmund Parkes, a professor of **clinical** medicine, **observed** that pumps were so **abundant** there that any **outbreak** would have had one nearby. He **interpreted** the same **cluster** as **poison** **drifting** through the air.

**Nevertheless**, a workhouse for the **needy**, almost **surrounded** by **afflicted** **dwellings**, lost a **mere** five of its 535 **residents**; it **boasted** its own well. A **brewery** **adjacent** to the pump lost none of its 70-odd **labourers**, whose **employer** **reckoned** they drank only their **ration** of malt **liquor**.

**Conversely**, a **widow** in **distant** Hampstead, who **favoured** the pump's water and had not visited Soho for months, had it **carted** to her daily and died. A visiting **niece** drank it, went home to "a high and healthy part of Islington" and died too.

A government **inquiry** in 1855 **declined** to **condemn** the pump: no one had **demonstrated** that its **drinkers** suffered **disproportionately**. A map could **chart** where people died, but learning what they drank meant asking the living.
