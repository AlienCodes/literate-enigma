# EN: Where They Died, What They Drank

Cholera **erupted** in Soho on the night of 31 August 1854. In ten days it **claimed** more than 500 lives within 250 yards of one street corner. **Orthodox** medicine **attributed** such **epidemics** to miasma, **foul** air rising from **filth**. John Snow, a local **physician**, had **maintained** since 1849 that cholera was **swallowed**, not **inhaled**. He **suspected** the much-**frequented** Broad Street **pump** at once, though its water had a **reputation** for **purity**.

So he **consulted** the **register** of deaths, and then the living. **Virtually** all the dead had lived in the pump's **vicinity**, and most **households** told him they drank from it. At his **urging**, the handle was removed on 8 September, by which time **fatal** cases had **declined** from a **peak** of 143 a day to 12.

Snow's famous map, which **depicted** each death as a black bar, was not **exhibited** until December. Its **cluster** was **compatible** with either theory, for foul air and foul water **overlapped**. In 1855 Edmund Parkes, a professor of **clinical** medicine, **interpreted** it as poison **diffusing** through the air. Pumps, he **observed**, were so **abundant** that any **outbreak** would have had one nearby.

His most **compelling** cases pulled air and water apart. **Amid** the dying, a workhouse for the poor lost five of its 535 inmates and a **brewery** none of its 70-odd **labourers**: each had its own well. **Conversely**, a **widow** in **distant** Hampstead who had not been near Broad Street for months was so **fond** of its water that she had it **carted** to her daily. She died, as did a visiting **niece** who drank it and returned to her **residence** in "a high and healthy part of Islington".

In 1855 an official **inquiry** **rejected** his case, saying nobody had shown that the well's **drinkers** had **fared worse** than their **neighbours**. A map could show where people died, but the argument **hinged** on what they drank.
