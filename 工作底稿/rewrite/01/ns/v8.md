# EN: Where They Died, What They Drank

Cholera **erupted** in **densely populated** Soho on 31 August 1854. In ten days the death **toll** **exceeded** 500. **Orthodox** medicine **attributed** **epidemics** to miasma, **foul** air **arising** from **filth**. John Snow, a local **physician**, had **maintained** since 1849 that cholera was **swallowed**, not **inhaled**. He **instantly** **suspected** the much-**frequented** Broad Street **pump**.

Its water, though, had a **reputation** for **purity**, so he **consulted** the **register** of deaths, then the living. **Virtually** all the dead had lived near the pump, and most had drunk from it. When the handle was removed at his **urging** on 8 September, **fatal** cases had already **declined** from a **peak** of 143 a day to 12.

Snow's famous map was not **exhibited** until December. Its **cluster** was **compatible** with either theory. Edmund Parkes, a professor of **clinical** medicine, **interpreted** it as **poison** **diffusing** through the air. Pumps, he **observed**, were so **abundant** that any **outbreak** would have had one in its **vicinity**.

The **crucial** cases **isolated** water from air. **Amid** the dying, a workhouse for the poor, with its own well, lost five of its 535 **residents**; a **brewery**, whose 70-odd **labourers** had an **allowance** of malt **liquor**, lost none. **Conversely**, a **widow** in **distant** Hampstead, long absent from Soho, had the pump's water **carted** to her daily. She died, as did a visiting **niece** who drank it and went home to "a high and healthy part of Islington".

In 1855 an official **inquiry** **rejected** Snow's **hypothesis**, saying nobody had **demonstrated** that the pump's **drinkers** **fared worse** than their **neighbours**. A map could show where people died, but the **dispute** **hinged** on what they drank.
