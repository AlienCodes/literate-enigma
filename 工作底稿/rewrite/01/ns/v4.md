# EN: Where They Died, What They Drank

Cholera **erupted** in Soho on the night of 31 August 1854. In ten days it **claimed** more than 500 lives within 250 yards of one street corner. **Orthodox** medicine **attributed** such **epidemics** to miasma, **foul** air rising from **filth**. John Snow, a local **physician**, had **maintained** since 1849 that cholera was **swallowed**, not **inhaled**. He **suspected** the much-**frequented** Broad Street **pump** at once, yet its water had a **reputation** for **purity**.

So he **consulted** the **register** of deaths, and then the living. **Virtually** all the dead had lived near the pump; most of the rest had drunk from it **nonetheless**. The parish's Board of Guardians heard his **findings** on 7 September and ordered the handle's **removal** next day. By then **fatal** cases had **declined** from a **peak** of 143 a day to 12.

Snow's famous map, a black bar for each death, was drawn **afterwards** and first **exhibited** in December. Its **cluster** was **compatible** with either theory. Reviewing his book in 1855, Edmund Parkes, a professor of **clinical** medicine, **interpreted** it as poison **diffusing** through the air. Pumps, he **observed**, were so **abundant** that any **outbreak** would have had one in its **vicinity**.

The **decisive** cases pulled air and water apart. **Amid** the dying, a workhouse for the poor lost only five of its 535 inmates, and a **brewery** none of its 70-odd **labourers**; each had its own well. Miles away, a Hampstead **widow** who had not been near Broad Street for months was so **fond** of its water that she had it **carted** to her daily. She died, as did a visiting **niece** who drank it and went home to "a high and healthy part of Islington".

In 1855 an official **inquiry** **rejected** his **verdict**, saying nobody had shown that the well's **drinkers** suffered more than their **neighbours**. A map could show where people died, but the argument **hinged** on what they drank.
