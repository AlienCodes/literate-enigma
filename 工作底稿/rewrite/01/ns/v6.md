# EN: Where They Died, What They Drank

Cholera **erupted** in **overcrowded** Soho on the night of 31 August 1854. In ten days the death **toll** **exceeded** 500. **Orthodox** medicine **attributed** **epidemics** to miasma, **foul** air rising from **filth**. John Snow, a local **physician**, had **maintained** since 1849 that cholera was **swallowed**, not **inhaled**. He **suspected** the much-**frequented** Broad Street **pump** at once, though its water had a **reputation** for **purity**.

So he **consulted** the **register** of deaths, and then the living. **Virtually** all the dead had lived near the pump, and most had drunk from it, their families **recalled**. At his **urging** the handle was removed on 8 September, by which time **fatal** cases had **declined** from a **peak** of 143 a day to 12.

Snow's famous map was not **exhibited** until December. Its **cluster** was **compatible** with either theory. Edmund Parkes, a professor of **clinical** medicine, **interpreted** it as **poison** **diffusing** through the air. Pumps, he **observed**, were so **abundant** that any **outbreak** would have had one in its **vicinity**.

The **decisive** cases **isolated** water from air. **Amid** the dying, a workhouse for the poor lost five of its 535 inmates and a **brewery** none of its 70-odd **labourers**: each had its own well. **Conversely**, a **widow** in **distant** Hampstead who had not been near Broad Street for months had its water **carted** to her daily. She died, as did a visiting **niece** who drank it and went home to "a high and healthy part of Islington".

In 1855 an official **inquiry** **rejected** his **hypothesis**, saying nobody had **demonstrated** that the well's **drinkers** **fared worse** than their **neighbours**. A map could show where people died, but the **dispute** **hinged** on what they drank.
