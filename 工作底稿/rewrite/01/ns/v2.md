# EN: Where They Died, What They Drank

Cholera **erupted** in Soho on the night of 31 August 1854. In ten days more than 500 people died within 250 yards of a single street corner. **Orthodox** medicine blamed miasma, **foul** air rising from **filth**. John Snow, a local **physician**, had **maintained** since 1849 that cholera was **swallowed**, not **inhaled**. He **suspected** the water of the much-**frequented** **pump** in Broad Street at once.

Yet it passed for pure. With a list from the register of deaths, he asked families where the dead had drawn their water. Nearly all had died close to the pump; most who lived nearer another had drunk from it anyway. The parish's Board of Guardians heard him on 7 September and had the handle removed next day. By then fatal cases had fallen from 143 a day to 12.

The famous map, a black bar for each death, came afterwards: it was first shown in December. Its cluster fitted either theory. Reviewing Snow's book in 1855, Edmund Parkes, a professor of medicine, read the pattern as poison radiating through the air, and noted that pumps were so abundant that any outbreak would have had one nearby.

Where air and water parted company, the evidence was sharper. A workhouse for the poor, more than three-quarters surrounded by stricken houses, lost five of its 535 inmates; a brewery beside the pump lost none of its 70-odd men. Each had its own well and never used the pump. Miles away in Hampstead, a widow who had not been near Broad Street for months had its water carted to her daily. She died; so did a visiting niece who drank it and went home to "a high and healthy part of Islington". Snow called these deaths "perhaps the most conclusive of all".

Even the government inquiry that dismissed his case in 1855 argued about drinkers: nobody, it said, had shown that the well's users died in greater numbers than their neighbours. A map could say where Soho died; asking could say what it drank.
