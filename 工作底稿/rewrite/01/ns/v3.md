# EN: Where They Died, What They Drank

Cholera **erupted** in Soho on the night of 31 August 1854. In ten days more than 500 people died within 250 yards of one street corner. **Orthodox** medicine blamed miasma, **foul** air rising from **filth**. John Snow, a local **physician**, had **maintained** since 1849 that cholera was **swallowed**, not **inhaled**. He **suspected** the busy Broad Street **pump** at once.

Yet its water passed for pure. So he consulted the official register of deaths, then asked where the dead had drawn their water. Nearly all had died close to the pump; most of the rest had drunk from it anyway. The parish's Board of Guardians heard him on 7 September and had the handle removed next day. Fatal cases had already fallen from 143 a day to 12.

The famous map, a black bar for each death, came later, and was first shown in December. Its cluster fitted either theory. Edmund Parkes, a professor of medicine, read it in 1855 as poison diffusing through the air, noting that pumps were so abundant that any outbreak would have had one nearby.

Sharper evidence came where air and water parted company. Nearby, a workhouse for the poor lost five of its 535 inmates and a brewery none of its 70-odd men; both had their own wells. Miles away in Hampstead, a widow who had not been near Broad Street for months had its water carted to her daily. She died, as did a visiting niece who drank it and went home to "a high and healthy part of Islington". Snow thought these deaths "perhaps the most conclusive of all".

In 1855 a government inquiry cleared the well, saying nobody had shown that its drinkers suffered more than their neighbours. A map could show where people died, but the argument hinged on what they drank.
