# EN: The Crowded Heavens

On 10 February 2009, nearly 790 kilometres above northern Siberia, two satellites **slammed** together at about 42,000 kilometres an hour. Iridium 33 belonged to an American satellite-phone **fleet**; Kosmos 2251, built for Russian **military** communications, had been **derelict** since about 1995. Both **shattered** into **wreckage**: the first **accidental** **collision** between two intact satellites.

Neither craft **swerved**. The Russian one, **devoid** of **propulsion**, could not. A public screening service had forecast a miss of about 584 metres, not among that week's closest **encounters**; such warnings were **frequent** and **imprecise**. One country's **lifeless** **hulk** **demolished** another's **operational** satellite.

The **aftermath** **persists**. The **impact** **flung** out more than 2,000 fragments large enough to **catalogue**, and a **multitude** too small to **pinpoint**. At that height air **drag** is **feeble**, so many will **linger** for decades. In January 2012 the International Space Station fired its **thrusters** to **dodge** a **splinter** of Iridium 33.

In June 1978 two NASA scientists **envisaged** a **vicious** **spiral**: collisions **scatter** fragments that **raise the odds** of **subsequent** crashes. The 2009 crash **demonstrated** that first step, not the **runaway** **chain reaction** dubbed the Kessler **syndrome**. By the end of 2024 **surveillance** networks tracked about 40,000 objects, only about 11,000 of them **functional** satellites.

A runaway cascade would **unfold** over decades, is not **inevitable** and can be **mitigated**. Operators now **routinely** **manoeuvre** to **evade** **anticipated** encounters. In September 2022 America's Federal Communications Commission, which licenses satellites, **mandated** **disposal** of low-orbit ones within five years of **retirement**, **scrapping** a 25-year **guideline**. For years, wreckage from that crash kept **outsiders**' spacecraft swerving.
