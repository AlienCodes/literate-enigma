# EN: The Crowded Heavens

Nearly 790 kilometres above the Siberian **Arctic**, on 10 February 2009, an **operational** satellite **silently** **smashed** into a **derelict** one at about 42,000 kilometres an hour. Iridium 33 belonged to an American satellite-phone **constellation**; Kosmos 2251 was a Russian **military** communications satellite, **inactive** since about 1995. Both **shattered** in the first **accidental** **collision** between two intact satellites.

Nobody **swerved**. The Russian **hulk** had no **propulsion**. A public screening service had forecast a **narrow** miss of about 584 metres but listed closer **encounters** that week; such **proximity** warnings were **frequent** and **imprecise**. One owner's **discarded** **relic** **ruined** another's working **asset**.

The **wreckage**, though, **endangered** everyone else: more than 2,000 fragments large enough to **catalogue**, and many more too small to **pinpoint**. Thin air up there **exerts** little **drag**, so many would **linger** for decades. In January 2012 the International Space Station fired its **thrusters** to **dodge** a **remnant** of Iridium 33.

In June 1978 two NASA **researchers** **envisaged** a cascade, later called the Kessler syndrome: collisions **generate** fragments that **raise the odds** of further **impacts**. The Siberian crash **merely** **exemplified** the first step, not a **runaway** **chain reaction**. By the end of 2024 **surveillance** networks tracked about 40,000 objects, only about 11,000 of them **functional**.

**Nonetheless**, the cascade is a decades-long **scenario**, not a **certainty**; operators **routinely** **manoeuvre** **out of harm's way**. In September 2022 America's Federal Communications Commission **mandated** **disposal** of low-orbit satellites within five years of **retirement**, replacing a 25-year **guideline**. Spacecraft whose owners had no part in that crash still swerve around its **scattered** **splinters**.
