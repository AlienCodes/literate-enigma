# EN: The Crowded Heavens

On 10 February 2009, nearly 790 kilometres above Siberia, two satellites **slammed** together at about 42,000 kilometres an hour. Iridium 33 belonged to an American satellite-phone **constellation**; Kosmos 2251, a Russian **military** satellite, had been **derelict** since about 1995. Both **shattered** in the first **accidental** **collision** between two intact satellites.

Neither craft **swerved**. The Russian **hulk**, **devoid** of **propulsion**, could not. A public screening service had forecast a **narrow** miss of about 584 metres, yet the **encounter** was not among the week's closest; such warnings were **frequent** and **imprecise**. One owner's **discarded** **relic** **demolished** another's working **asset**.

The **wreckage**, however, **endangered** everyone else. The **impact** **flung** out over 2,000 **catalogued** fragments and a **multitude** too small to **pinpoint**. At that height air **drag** is **feeble**, so many would **linger** for decades. In January 2012 the International Space Station fired its **thrusters** to **dodge** an Iridium 33 **remnant**.

In June 1978 two NASA scientists **envisaged** a **vicious** **spiral**, later named the Kessler **syndrome**: each collision would **scatter** fragments **raising the odds** of **subsequent** crashes. The 2009 crash **illustrated** that **premise** without **triggering** a **runaway** **chain reaction**. By the end of 2024 **surveillance** networks tracked about 40,000 objects, only about 11,000 of them **functional**.

Such a cascade would **unfold** over decades, though, and is neither **inevitable** nor beyond **mitigation**. Operators now **routinely** **manoeuvre** **out of harm's way**. In September 2022 America's Federal Communications Commission, which licenses satellites, **mandated** **disposal** of low-orbit ones within five years of **retirement**, **scrapping** a 25-year **guideline**. For years, spacecraft whose owners had no part in that crash kept swerving around its **splinters**.
