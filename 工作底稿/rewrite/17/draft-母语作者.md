# EN: The Crowded Heavens

On 10 February 2009, nearly 790 kilometres above northern Siberia, a **derelict** satellite **slammed** into an **operational** one at about 42,000 kilometres an hour. Kosmos 2251 was a Russian **military** satellite, **inactive** since about 1995; Iridium 33 belonged to an American satellite-phone **fleet**. Both **shattered** in the first **accidental** **collision** between two intact satellites.

Lacking **propulsion**, the Russian **hulk** was **powerless** to **swerve**. A public screening service had forecast a **near-miss** of about 584 metres, but that **encounter** did not rank among the week's closest; such warnings were **commonplace** and **imprecise**. One owner's **discarded** **relic** destroyed another's working **asset**.

The crash **flung** out more than 2,000 **catalogued** fragments and **swarms** of smaller ones. At that height the air is so thin, its **drag** so **feeble**, that many **remnants** would **linger** for decades. In January 2012 the International Space Station fired its **thrusters** to **dodge** a **chunk** of Iridium 33.

In June 1978 two NASA scientists **envisaged** a **chain reaction**, later named the Kessler syndrome: each collision would **scatter** fragments **liable** to cause another. The 2009 crash **illustrated** that **premise** without **triggering** a **runaway** **spiral**. By the end of 2024 **radar** and **telescopes** were tracking about 40,000 objects, only about 11,000 of them **functional** satellites.

Yet a runaway **scenario** would **unfold** over decades and is not **inevitable**. In September 2022 America's Federal Communications Commission **mandated** the **disposal** of low-orbit satellites within five years of **retirement**, replacing a 25-year **guideline**. Operators now **routinely** **manoeuvre** to **avert** collisions. Years after the crash, its **wreckage** was still forcing **orbital** **neighbours** to swerve.
