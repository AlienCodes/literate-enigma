# EN: The Octopus That Escaped

One night in early 2016, after **keepers** at New Zealand's National Aquarium in Napier had left, an octopus named Inky **crept** through an **aperture** in the **mesh** **atop** its tank. The **fugitive** **presumably** dropped to the floor, crossed the room **unseen** and **compressed** its **elastic** body into a drain, whose **hollow** pipe **stretched** some 50 metres to the Pacific.

Staff **reconstructed** that route from the **slime** **residue** it **deposited**. Inky had **resided** in **captivity** since 2014, when a fisherman **retrieved** it from a crayfish **pot** off Napier. The **breakout** went **unreported** for about three months. Made public in April 2016, it soon travelled the **globe**.

Such **agility** is **feasible** because an octopus's **pliable** body has no **skeleton** or shell. Its only hard part is its **beak**, so any **crevice** that **accommodates** the beak will do. About two-thirds of its roughly 500 million **neurons** lie in the **limbs**, which handle **sensory** input and react with some **autonomy**.

The **wits** match the **anatomy**. A giant Pacific octopus at the Seattle Aquarium learned to open screw-top **jars**, taking 15 minutes at the **outset**, later averaging about two. In a 2010 study, octopuses met two strangers, a **feeder** and one who **poked** them with a bristly stick, and came to **discriminate** between them, changing **hue** and **posture** **accordingly**.

In July 2012 the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness listed octopuses among animals **possessing** the **neurological** **substrates** for **conscious** **awareness**. In November 2021 a UK government review recommended treating them as **sentient**, and the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act **duly** **encompassed** them in April 2022. Inky, somewhere beyond the pipe, lay **wholly** outside the Act's **jurisdiction**.
