# EN: The Octopus That Escaped

One night in early 2016, after **keepers** had left New Zealand's National Aquarium in Napier, an **octopus** named Inky made an **audacious** **exit**. Staff believe the **fugitive** squeezed through a gap in its tank's **mesh**, dropped to the floor and crossed to a drain. Its **malleable** body **presumably** followed a **hollow** **outlet** pipe 50 metres to the Pacific.

A **sticky** **smear** of slime, the only **residue**, let staff **reconstruct** the route. Caught in a crayfish pot off Napier in 2014, Inky had lived in **captivity** since. The **brazen** **breakout** became public only in April 2016, about three months later, and the **anecdote** **charmed** **readers** worldwide.

This was **feasible** because an octopus lacks a **skeleton** or shell. Its only hard part is its **beak**, so its **flexible** body can be **compressed** through any **aperture** the beak fits. Of its roughly 500 million **neurons**, two-thirds lie in its arms, whose **sensory** **receptors** let them respond with partial **autonomy**.

**Cognition** matches **agility**. A giant Pacific octopus in Seattle learned to **manipulate** screw-top **jars**, needing 15 minutes at first and about two afterwards. In a 2010 study, octopuses came to **discriminate** between two strangers, a **feeder** and a **handler** who touched them with a bristly stick, showing each a **visibly** different **hue** and **posture**.

In July 2012 the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness listed octopuses among animals **possessing** the **neurological** **substrates** for **consciousness**. A November 2021 UK government review advised treating them as **sentient**, and the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act of April 2022 **duly** covers them. None of this proves a **conscious** **inner** life. The law nonetheless **encompasses** Inky's **lineage**, wherever Inky went.
