# EN: The Octopus That Escaped

One night in early 2016, **keepers** at the National Aquarium of New Zealand in Napier went home. An **octopus** named Inky then **compressed** its **boneless** body through an **aperture** in its tank's **mesh**. Staff believe it dropped to the floor, **crawled** across the room and **flattened** itself into a drain **outlet**, whose **pipe** ran some 50 metres to the Pacific.

The **fugitive** left a **glossy** **smear** of slime, from which staff **reconstructed** its **itinerary**. Its **captivity** had begun in 2014, when a fisherman found it **lodged** in a crayfish pot off Napier. The **breakout** stayed **undisclosed** until April 2016, three months on, then made news across the **globe**.

**Anatomy** makes such **agility** **feasible**. An octopus has no **skeleton** or shell, and its only hard part is its **beak**, so it can **negotiate** any gap that wide. Of some 500 million **neurons**, roughly two-thirds sit in its **limbs**, which **integrate** **sensory** input and react with some **autonomy**.

At the Seattle Aquarium, a **cunning** octopus learned to open screw-top **jars**, needing 15 minutes at first, about two on average **afterwards**. In a 2010 study, octopuses became **acquainted** with two strangers over two weeks, a **feeder** and one who touched them with a bristly stick. They came to **discriminate** between the **duo**, with a **contrasting** **hue** and **posture** for each.

In July 2012 the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness listed octopuses among animals **possessing** the **neural** **substrates** for **consciousness**. A November 2021 **appraisal** **commissioned** by the UK government advised treating them as **sentient**, and the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act **embraced** them in April 2022. Inky's **whereabouts** by then were known only to the **tides**.
