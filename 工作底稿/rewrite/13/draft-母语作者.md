# EN: Y2K: The Crisis That Success Erased

The Y2K problem, or **millennium** **bug**, began as a **shortcut**. When memory **hardware** was **costly**, **programmers** stored years as two **digits**, writing "99" for 1999. That **compact** **code** hid an **ambiguity**: a machine might read "00" as 1900 and get interest, ages or **deadlines** wrong.

**Dreading** **chaos**, **organisations** hired **consultants** to **audit** **vulnerable** systems and **patch** **faulty** programs in banks, **utilities** and **aviation**. Britain had already set up Action 2000 in October 1997; in February 1998 Bill Clinton created a **council** to **coordinate** America's effort. The Commerce Department put American public and private **expenditure** at about $100bn; **global** **projections** reached hundreds of billions.

When 2000 arrived, little went wrong. Only **scattered** glitches cropped up. Radiation-monitoring equipment **faltered** at Japan's Shika nuclear plant, with no **threat** to **reactor** safety, and a Pentagon ground system that processes spy-satellite data failed for a time. In February 2000 a Senate committee **concluded** that the money had been well spent.

Many, **cynical** about the **hype**, judged the danger **exaggerated**, even a **hoax**. This **irony** is known as the preparedness paradox: successful **precautions** make a danger look **imaginary**. **Prevention** keeps its **merit** **invisible**, so a calm that **remedial** work had **largely** **ensured** invited **ridicule**.

Others noted that Italy and South Korea spent far less and fared no worse. That **comparison** is weak: those low figures often excluded what firms spent, and much of the work replaced **obsolete** systems due for upgrading anyway. The **investment** was broadly **worthwhile**, yet the **ledger** stays open, since nobody can tell which fixes **averted** harm and which were **wasteful**.
