# EN: Y2K: The Crisis That Success Erased

The Y2K (year 2000) **bug**, or **millennium** bug, was born of **thrift**. When computer memory was **costly**, **programmers** stored years as two **digits**: "99" meant 1999. The **compact** **shortcut** **sufficed** for decades. But a machine might read "00" as 1900, **corrupting** calculations of interest, ages and **deadlines**, and **decrepit** systems were full of such dates.

Governments **braced** for **chaos**. In February 1998 President Bill Clinton created a **council** to **coordinate** America's **mobilisation**. **Consultants** **audited** **code** and **patched** **defective** dates. The Commerce Department estimated that American governments and **enterprises** spent about $100bn; worldwide **projections** ran into hundreds of billions.

Then **midnight** struck, and little went wrong. Glitches were **sporadic** and mostly **minor**: at Japan's Shika nuclear plant, radiation-monitoring **instrumentation** **faltered** without threatening safety, and a Pentagon ground system that processes **spy**-satellite data failed. In February 2000 a Senate committee **concluded** that the **expenditure** had been **justified**.

Many decided instead that the threat had been **exaggerated**, even a **hoax**, and that its **prophets** deserved **scorn**. Yet in **retrospect**, the repairs largely explain the calm. This is the preparedness paradox: when **precautions** succeed, the danger they **averted** looks **imaginary**. **Prevention** leaves no **wreckage**, so its success stays **invisible**.

**Detractors** noted that Italy and South Korea spent far less yet fared no worse. The **comparison** **misleads**: those low figures often **omitted** what firms spent, and much of the work replaced **obsolete** systems **overdue** for upgrading. Nobody can say which dollars were **essential** and which **excessive**. Prevention that works may leave little behind but an **invoice**, weighed against a **catastrophe** no one saw.
