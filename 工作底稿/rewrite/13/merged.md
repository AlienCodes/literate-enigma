# EN: Y2K: The Crisis That Success Erased

The Y2K **bug**, or **millennium** bug, began as a **shortcut**. When memory was **costly**, **programmers** stored years as two **digits**, writing "99" for 1999. That **compact** **code** hid an **ambiguity**: a machine might read "00" as 1900, **corrupting** calculations of interest, ages or **deadlines**.

Governments and firms **braced** for **chaos**. In February 1998 President Bill Clinton created a **council** to **coordinate** America's effort. **Consultants** **audited** **legacy** systems and **patched** **faulty** dates. The Commerce Department estimated that American governments and **enterprises** spent about $100bn; **projections** of the **global** bill ran to hundreds of billions.

Then **midnight** struck, and little went wrong. The glitches that did **crop up** were **sporadic** and mostly **minor**. Radiation-monitoring equipment **faltered** at Japan's Shika nuclear plant without any threat to safety, and a Pentagon ground system that processes **spy**-satellite data failed. In February 2000 a Senate committee **concluded** that the **expenditure** had been **justified**.

Many decided instead that the threat had been **exaggerated**, even a **hoax**. Yet calm **prevailed** **largely** because so much had been fixed. This is the preparedness paradox: when **precautions** work, the danger they **ward off** never appears, so it looks **imaginary**. **Tedious** repairs earned **mockery** rather than **gratitude**.

Critics noted that Italy and South Korea spent far less yet fared no worse. The **comparison** **misleads**: those low figures often **omitted** what firms spent, and much of the **remedial** work replaced **obsolete** systems **overdue** for upgrading. Nobody can say which dollars were **essential** and which **excessive**. **Prevention** that works may leave little behind but an **invoice**, weighed against a **catastrophe** no one saw.
