# EN: Y2K: The Crisis That Success Erased

In the early decades of computing, when memory was **costly**, **programmers** stored years as two **digits**: "99" meant 1999. That **compact** but **ambiguous** **shortcut** created the **millennium** **bug**, or Y2K (for "year 2000"). After **midnight** on 31 December 1999, a machine might read "00" as 1900, **corrupting** calculations of interest, ages or **deadlines**.

Governments and firms **scrambled**. In February 1998 President Bill Clinton created a **council** to **coordinate** America's effort. Programmers **audited** **legacy** **code** and **patched** **faulty** lines. American governments and businesses spent about $100bn, the Commerce Department estimated; **global** **projections** ran to hundreds of billions.

When 2000 arrived, little went wrong. The glitches that did **crop up** were **sporadic** and mostly **minor**. Radiation-monitoring equipment **faltered** at Japan's Shika **nuclear** plant without **compromising** safety, and a Pentagon ground system that processes spy-satellite data failed. In February 2000 a Senate committee **concluded** that the spending was **worthwhile**.

Many drew the opposite **inference**, **writing off** the threat as **hype**, even a **hoax**. Yet calm **prevailed** **largely** because so much had been fixed. This is the preparedness paradox: when **precautions** work, the danger they **ward off** never appears, so it looks **imaginary**. **Tedious** repairs earned **mockery** rather than **gratitude**.

Critics noted that Italy and South Korea spent far less yet fared no worse. The **comparison** is **questionable**: those low figures often **omitted** **corporate** spending, and much **remedial** work replaced **obsolete** systems **overdue** for upgrading. Nobody can say **precisely** which dollars were **superfluous**. A disaster **averted** leaves no **wreckage**, so prevention's cost can be **tallied** while its value is left to **conjecture**.
