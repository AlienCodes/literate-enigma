# 第58篇（鸟类导航）来源摘录（WebSearch 两轮，2026-10-03）
S1 Boston Public Library blog "The Story of AX6587" ; Auk 70(2) 1953 ; Lockley (Skokholm): In 1952 Rosario Mazzeo (amateur ornithologist, Boston Symphony Orchestra personnel manager) took Manx shearwater AX6587 from Skokholm, an island off Wales, by train to London and by plane to Boston. He released it at Logan Airport at 8:15 a.m. on 3 June 1952. It was found back in its nesting burrow on Skokholm on 16 June: about 3,200 miles of open Atlantic in 12.5 days, then the longest homing flight recorded and the first bird tracked across an ocean. Mazzeo posted a letter from Boston right after the release; Ronald Lockley on Skokholm first thought the bird must have been freed in London. The bird outpaced the letter.
S2 Kramer (1950s), Condor/textbook summaries: Gustav Kramer kept starlings in circular cages; when mirrors shifted the sun's apparent position, the birds shifted their heading accordingly; they used the sun as a compass and compensated for its movement during the day.
S3 Emlen (1960s; 1967): Stephen Emlen kept indigo buntings in a planetarium; by blotting out groups of stars he showed they orient by the constellations around the North Star (the centre of the sky's rotation).
S4 Wiltschko 1972 (Science), Goethe University Frankfurt: European robins orient with Earth's magnetic field; the bird compass is an inclination compass reading the angle (dip) of the field lines, not their polarity.
S5 Schulten 1978; Ritz, Adem & Schulten 2000; Xu et al. 2021 (Nature): Klaus Schulten proposed in 1978 that a light-triggered chemical reaction (radical pair) lets birds sense magnetic fields; in 2000 cryptochrome, a light-sensitive protein in the retina, was proposed as the molecule; in 2021 robin cryptochrome 4 proved magnetically sensitive in the lab, more so than in chickens and pigeons. Still a leading hypothesis, not fully proven in the living bird.
S6 Engels et al. 2014 (Nature), University of Oldenburg (9 scientists, with Peter Hore of Oxford; 7 years of work): robins in unscreened wooden huts on campus could not use their magnetic compass; in electrically grounded, aluminium-screened huts (cutting noise at 50 kHz–5 MHz about a hundredfold) they oriented again; removing the grounding or adding noise inside made them lose it again. Double-blind tests. Noise levels far below WHO safety limits; source: everyday electronic equipment.
S7 General (reviews): how birds know where they are (the "map") is still not fully understood.
