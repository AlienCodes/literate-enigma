In the late summer of 1977, two **spaceships** set **forth** **separately**, Voyager 2 on 20 August and Voyager 1 on 5 September. **Fastened** to each was a **gilded** copper **disc** 30 **centimetres** across, a **compendium** of Earth in sound and image.

Carl Sagan of Cornell **oversaw** the committee that **curated** it for NASA, and the **array** is **eclectic**. Greetings are spoken in 55 languages. **Rumbling** **thunder**, a **gust** of wind, birds and the **moans** of whales sit beside **footsteps**, **laughter** and the **rhythmic** beat of a **heart**. About 90 minutes of music, Bach and Chuck Berry's Johnny B. Goode **alike**, draws on many cultures and eras. There are 115 images **encoded** in analogue form, and printed messages from President Jimmy Carter and the UN secretary-general, Kurt Waldheim. The cover bears instructions for playing the disc and a **diagram** fixing the Sun's position, with pulsars as **galactic** **beacons**.

On 14 February 1990, Voyager 1 was about six billion kilometres away. After years of **advocacy** by Sagan, the craft **pivoted** its **onboard** camera for a **backward** **glance**. Across 60 frames it made a **mosaic**, a family portrait of the solar system. Earth was a **pale**, **bluish** **glimmer** less than a pixel wide.

**Patience** was part of the job. Each radio signal crossed the **gulf** in about five and a half hours, and the frames reached Deep Space Network **antennas** over several passes between March and 1 May.

In his 1994 book, Pale Blue Dot, Sagan read the image as a **perspective** on our small, **ephemeral** place in the **vastness** of the cosmos.

Voyager 1 **glided** into **interstellar** space in August 2012, the first human-made object to do so, and Voyager 2 crossed in 2018. Both are working, the longest-running mission NASA has. Their **longevity** has limits, though. Power from their **generators** is **finite**, and **depletion** is expected within a few years.

Each disc carries a map to the Sun on a craft heading **outwards** from it.
