In the late summer of 1977, two identical **discs** were **bolted** to the sides of two **probes** bound for the outer planets. Voyager 2 **lifted** off on 20 August and Voyager 1 on 5 September. Each carried a 30-centimetre copper record, **plated** with gold.

A **committee** **chaired** by Carl Sagan of Cornell **curated** its contents for NASA. There are spoken **greetings** in 55 languages, and the **rumble** of thunder, the **gust** of wind, **birdsong** and the calls of **whales**. Human life is **represented** by **footsteps**, **laughter** and a **heartbeat**. About 90 minutes of music **spans** many cultures and **eras**, from Bach to Chuck Berry. There are 115 images **encoded** in **analogue** form, and **printed** messages from President Jimmy Carter and the UN **Secretary-General**, Kurt Waldheim. **Engraved** on the cover are **instructions** for playing the record and a **diagram** that **pinpoints** the Sun by **reference** to **pulsars**.

On 14 February 1990, Voyager 1 was about six billion kilometres from home. At the **urging** of Sagan, who had **lobbied** for the shot for years, the **craft** **swivelled** its camera **backwards** and took a family **portrait** of the solar system in 60 **frames**. Earth showed up as a **speck** about 0.12 of a **pixel** across.

The **transmission** was slow. Each radio signal needed about five and a half hours to reach the Deep Space Network, and the frames **trickled** in between March and 1 May.

In his 1994 book, Pale Blue Dot, Sagan **interpreted** the image as a **portrayal** of our **tiny**, **fleeting** place in the **cosmos**.

Voyager 1 **crossed** into **interstellar** space in August 2012, the first **human-made** object to do so, and its **twin** **followed** in 2018. Both are **operational**, making up NASA's longest-running mission, though their power is **expected** to **dwindle** to nothing within a few years.

Each still holds a golden **disc** ready to be played.
