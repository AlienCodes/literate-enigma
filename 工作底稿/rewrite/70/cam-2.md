In the late summer of 1977, two Voyager **spacecraft** **departed** a **fortnight** apart. Each **bore** a **gilded** copper **disc** thirty **centimetres** wide, and the two were **duplicates**.

A **panel** **headed** by Carl Sagan of Cornell had **curated** the contents for NASA. **Greetings** in 55 languages sat beside **thunder**, wind, birds and whales, then **footsteps**, **laughter** and a **heartbeat**. Some ninety minutes of music **embraced** many cultures and **periods**, from Bach to Chuck Berry's Johnny B. Goode. There were also 115 pictures **encoded** in analogue form and **written** **statements** from two **statesmen**, Jimmy Carter and Kurt Waldheim. On the **casing**, **etchings** showed how to play the disc, beside a **diagram** that **fixed** the Sun's **whereabouts** by **reference** to pulsars.

On 14 February 1990, Voyager 1 turned its camera **rearward** from some six billion kilometres away. Sagan had **pressed** for the picture for years, and his **persistence** **prevailed**. Sixty frames formed a family portrait of the solar system, and Earth **filled** about twelve hundredths of one pixel, a **negligible** **smudge** of light.

The images **arrived** **piecemeal** between March and 1 May over several Deep Space Network **sessions**, and each radio signal needed about five and a half hours to **traverse** the **gulf**. In his 1994 book Pale Blue Dot, Sagan **read** that point as a **sign** of humanity's small, **transient** place in the cosmos.

Voyager 1 **slipped** into **interstellar** space in August 2012, the first human-made object to do so, and its **sibling** **followed** in 2018. Both are **functional** today, though their power is **projected** to **give out** within a few years.

The camera has gone dark for good, but the disc **aboard** **sails** **outward** with its thunder and laughter.
