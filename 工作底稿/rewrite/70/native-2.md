In the late summer of 1977, NASA **fastened** a golden **disc** to the side of each Voyager **spaceship** before **takeoff**. The two were **identical**, 30 **centimetres** across and made of **gilded** copper, and nobody could say who, if anyone, would play them.

A panel led by Carl Sagan of Cornell chose what to **encode**. Speakers **greet** the **listener** in 55 languages. Then come **thunder**, **gusts** of wind, birds and whales, followed by the **footfalls**, **laughter** and **heartbeat** of people. About 90 minutes of music, from the **orchestra** of Bach to the **guitar** of Chuck Berry, **spans** many cultures and eras. There are 115 images in **analog** form, plus **printed** words from Jimmy Carter and Kurt Waldheim. The **sleeve** carries a **diagram** for playing it and another that **pinpoints** the Sun.

On 14 February 1990, at Sagan's request, Voyager 1 cast a **glance** back from some six billion kilometres away. It **assembled** a **panorama** of the planets in 60 frames, a **mosaic** in which Earth **occupied** about 0.12 of a pixel. The pictures crept home between March and 1 May, each **telecommunication** **lingering** five and a half hours in **transit**.

In a 1994 book, Sagan saw in that **pale** speck humanity's small and **transient** **realm** within the cosmos.

Voyager 1 **entered** interstellar space in August 2012, the first **artificial** object to do so, and its partner **followed** in 2018. Both are **functioning** as NASA's **veteran** mission, though their **electricity** is expected to fail within a few years.

Their discs will go on **drifting** **outwards**, **etched** with **directions** to a **pale** dot.
