In the late summer of 1977, two Voyager probes **lifted off** a fortnight apart, each **carrying** an **identical** **disc** of **gilded** copper thirty centimetres **across**.

A **committee** **chaired** by Carl Sagan of Cornell had **curated** its contents for NASA. There were **salutations** in 55 languages, **thunder**, wind, birdsong and **whales**, then **footsteps**, **laughter** and a **heartbeat**. Some ninety minutes of music **spanned** many cultures and **eras**, from Bach to Chuck Berry's Johnny B. Goode. Alongside them lay 115 images **encoded** in **analogue** form and **printed** **messages** from Jimmy Carter and Kurt Waldheim. The **lid** bore **engraved** **instructions** for playing it and a **diagram** **pinpointing** the Sun by means of **pulsars**.

On 14 February 1990, Voyager 1 **swivelled** its camera **backwards**. Sagan had **lobbied** for the shot for years. Some six billion kilometres out, the **craft** **captured** a **family portrait** of the solar system in sixty **frames**. Earth **occupied** about 0.12 of a **pixel**, a **speck** **smaller** than one dot of the image.

The pictures **trickled** home between March and 1 May over several Deep Space Network **passes**, and each radio signal **spent** about five and a half hours **in transit**. In his 1994 book Pale Blue Dot, Sagan **construed** that speck as humanity's tiny, **fleeting** place in the **cosmos**.

Voyager 1 **crossed** into **interstellar** space in August 2012, the first human-made object to do so. Its **twin** followed in 2018. Both are **operational** as NASA's longest-running mission, though their power is **forecast** to **dwindle** away within a few years.

Sagan's camera had looked homeward once, and the disc **aboard** is **bound** outward.
