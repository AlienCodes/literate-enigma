In the late summer of 1977, two **identical** discs went into space, one bolted to each Voyager **probe**. Each was a 30-centimetre **gold-plated** **copper** **phonograph** record, and nobody knew who, if anyone, would play it.

A **committee** **chaired** by Carl Sagan of Cornell chose the contents for NASA. There were **spoken** **greetings** in 55 languages, then **thunder**, wind, **birdsong** and **whales**. **Footsteps**, **laughter** and a **heartbeat** stood for people. About 90 minutes of music **spanned** many cultures and **eras**, from Bach to Chuck Berry's Johnny B. Goode. There were 115 images **encoded** in **analogue** form, and **printed** messages from President Jimmy Carter and Kurt Waldheim of the UN. **Engraved** on the cover were **instructions** for playing it and a **diagram** that located the Sun by **pulsars**.

On 14 February 1990, Voyager 1 was some six billion kilometres from Earth. At Sagan's **urging**, after years of **lobbying**, it **swivelled** its camera **backwards** and took a **family** **portrait** of the solar system in 60 **frames**. Earth showed up as a **speck** about 0.12 of a **pixel** across. The pictures **trickled** home between March and 1 May over several Deep Space Network **passes**, each radio signal **travelling** about five and a half hours.

In his 1994 book Pale Blue Dot, Sagan read that **dot** as a picture of our **tiny**, **fleeting** place in the **cosmos**.

Voyager 1 crossed into **interstellar** space in August 2012, the first **human-made** object to do so, and its **twin** followed in 2018. Both are **operating** today as NASA's **longest-running** mission, though their power is expected to **dwindle** to nothing within a few years.

The two discs will carry on **coasting** in **silence**, **etched** with **directions** home.
