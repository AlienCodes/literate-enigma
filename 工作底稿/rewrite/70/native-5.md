In late summer 1977, NASA **fastened** a **disc** **wrought** from **gilded** copper, 30 **centimetres** wide, to each Voyager **spaceship** before **takeoff**. Nobody could **foresee** who, if anyone, would play them.

**Advisers** under Carl Sagan of Cornell chose this **compendium** of our **earthly** **abode**. Voices **greet** the **listener** in 55 languages, followed by the **rumble** of **thunder**, the **whistle** of wind, whales, footsteps, **laughter** and a **rhythmic** **heartbeat**. About 90 minutes of music cover a **spectrum** of cultures and eras, **notably** the **composer** Bach and Chuck Berry's **anthem** Johnny B. Goode. An **array** of 115 images, **encoded** in **analog** form, sits beside printed words from two **statesmen**, Jimmy Carter and Kurt Waldheim. The **sleeve** gives **guidance** on playing it, plus a **diagram** fixing the Sun with pulsars as **beacons**.

On 14 February 1990, at Sagan's request, Voyager 1 **pivoted** its camera for a **glance** **backwards** across an **astronomical** six billion kilometres. Sixty frames formed a **panorama** of the solar system, a **mosaic** in which Earth was a **glimmer** about an eighth of a pixel across. They reached Deep Space Network **receivers** between March and 1 May, each **telecommunication** taking five and a half hours.

In a 1994 book, Sagan saw in that **pale** dot our small and **ephemeral** place amid the **vastness** of the cosmos.

Voyager 1 crossed into interstellar space in August 2012, the first **artifact** to do so, and Voyager 2 followed in 2018. Both are working, with a **longevity** unmatched at NASA, but **depletion** of their **electricity** is expected to silence them within a few years.

Their discs will **glide** on **outwards** through the **void**, carrying a map back to the Sun.
