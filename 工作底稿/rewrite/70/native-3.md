In the late summer of 1977, NASA **fastened** a **disc** to each of its two Voyager **spacecraft** before **takeoff**. The pair were identical, 30 **centimetres** wide and **wrought** in **gilded** copper, and nobody could say who, if anyone, would ever play them.

A panel led by Carl Sagan of Cornell decided what this **compendium** of the planet we **inhabit** should **encode**. **Vocal** greetings **greet** the **listener** in 55 languages. Then come the **rumble** of **thunder**, the **whistle** of wind, birds and whales, and the footsteps, **laughter** and **heartbeat** of human beings. Music with a **duration** of about 90 minutes **encompasses** a wide **spectrum** of cultures and eras, **notably** the **composer** Bach and Chuck Berry's Johnny B. Goode. An **array** of 115 images is stored in **analog** form, beside printed words from two **statesmen**, Jimmy Carter and Kurt Waldheim. The **sleeve** offers **guidance** on playing it, plus a **diagram** fixing the Sun's place with pulsars as **beacons**.

On 14 February 1990, at Sagan's long-argued request, Voyager 1 **pivoted** its camera for a **glance** back across an **astronomical** six billion kilometres or so. Its 60 frames formed a **panorama** of the solar system, a **mosaic** in which Earth filled about an eighth of a pixel. The images reached Deep Space Network **receivers** between March and 1 May, each **telecommunication** spending five and a half hours on the way.

In a 1994 book, Sagan saw in that **pale** dot our small and **ephemeral** place amid the **vastness** of the cosmos.

Voyager 1 crossed into interstellar space in August 2012, the first object made by human hands to do so, and Voyager 2 joined it in 2018. Both are working today, with a **longevity** no other NASA mission can match, but the **depletion** of their **electricity** is expected to silence them within a few years.

Their discs will **glide** on **outwards** through the **void**, carrying a map back to the Sun.
