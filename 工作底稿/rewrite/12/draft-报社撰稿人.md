# EN: The Toad That Conquered a Continent

Greyback cane **beetles**, whose **larvae** **devour** cane roots, had Queensland's sugar **plantations** under **siege**. In June 1935 a sugar-industry scientist **imported** 102 cane toads, large **amphibians** from the Americas, from Hawaii to a station near Gordonvale. In August about 2,400 of their young were **released**. **Biological** control, fighting a **pest** with another living thing, had an **ambitious** new **recruit**.

In November 1935 an insect expert, Walter Froggatt, **persuaded** federal officials to **halt** releases; after **lobbying** by Queensland's government, releases **resumed** in September 1936. The **rash** **scheme** **flopped**: the toads barely **dented** the beetles.

They **thrived** instead. With few **foes** and **fertile** females, they **multiplied** **unchecked**. Their **relentless** **invasion** front **accelerated** from about ten **kilometres** a year at first to about 50 in the **tropical** north. In February 2009 the first known toad **breached** Western Australia's border.

**Toxic** skin **glands** made them an often **deadly** meal. Goannas, large Australian **lizards**, have **slumped** in some areas, as have freshwater **crocodiles** and northern quolls, cat-sized native hunters; local quoll populations were **wiped out**. **Ecological** **impacts** **vary**; some of the hunters' former **prey** have **prospered**. In June 2010 researchers reported that young quolls fed toad meat **laced** with a **nausea**-inducing chemical learned to shun toads and survived better.

Biological control is not the **villain**. From 1926 a **moth**, Cactoblastis, cleared most prickly pear, an **invasive** **cactus**, from Queensland and New South Wales within about a decade. In October 1984 Australia passed a Biological Control Act; recruits now face years of **scrutiny**. The toads' **legacy** is a rule, not a **prohibition**: no recruit should be **unleashed** untested.
