# EN: The Toad That Conquered a Continent

Greyback cane **beetles**, whose **larvae** **devour** cane roots, were **plaguing** Queensland's **lucrative** sugar **plantations**. In June 1935 a scientist **imported** 102 cane toads, **bulky** **amphibians** from the Americas, from Hawaii. That August about 2,400 of their young were **released**. **Biological** control, setting one **species** against a **pest**, had an **ambitious** new **recruit**.

The **hasty** plan met **opposition**. In November 1935 an insect expert, Walter Froggatt, **persuaded** federal officials to **halt** releases; after **lobbying** by Queensland, releases **resumed** in September 1936. The **scheme** **flopped**: the toads barely **dented** the beetles, perhaps because the insects kept high on cane **stalks**.

The toads **thrived** instead. With few **foes** and **fertile** females **spawning** thousands of eggs, they **multiplied** **unchecked**. Their **invasion** **frontier**, creeping about ten kilometres a year at first, **accelerated** to about 50 in the **tropical** north. In February 2009 the first known toad **breached** Western Australia's border.

**Toxin** **glands** in their skin make them a deadly meal. Goannas, large **lizards**, have **slumped** in places, as have freshwater **crocodiles** and northern quolls, cat-sized hunters; local quoll populations were **wiped out**. The **havoc** is **uneven**; some species have **prospered**. In June 2010 researchers reported that young quolls fed toad meat **laced** with a **nausea**-**inducing** chemical learned to avoid toads.

Biological control can **triumph**. From 1926 a **moth**, Cactoblastis, cleared most prickly pear, an **invasive** **cactus**, from Queensland and New South Wales. In October 1984 Australia passed a Biological Control Act; recruits now face years of **stringent** **scrutiny**. The **blunder** of 1935 was **haste**: once loose, an **untested** ally may never be **recalled**.
