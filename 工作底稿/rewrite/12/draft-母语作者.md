# EN: The Toad That Conquered a Continent

Greyback cane beetles, whose grubs eat cane roots, were **plaguing** Queensland's **lucrative** **plantations**, and **biological** control, setting one **species** against a **pest**, promised an **elegant** fix. In June 1935 a scientist **imported** 102 cane toads, **bulky** **amphibians** from the Americas, from Hawaii to northern Queensland. That August about 2,400 of their young were **unleashed**.

The **hasty** plan met **opposition**. In November 1935 an entomologist, Walter Froggatt, **lobbied** federal officials into **suspending** releases; in September 1936, at Queensland's **insistence**, releases **resumed**. The **scheme** flopped: the toads barely **dented** the beetles, which perhaps kept high on the **stalks**, out of reach.

The toads **thrived**. With few **foes** and **fertile** females **spawning** thousands of eggs at once, they **multiplied** **unchecked**. Their **invasion** **frontier**, **creeping** about 10 kilometres a year at first, **accelerated** to about 50 in the **tropical** north. In February 2009 the first known toad **breached** Western Australia's border.

**Toxin** **glands** in their skin make them **deadly** **prey**. In places **reptiles** such as goannas and freshwater crocodiles have **slumped**, and northern quolls, cat-sized hunters, have vanished locally. The **ecological** **havoc** is **uneven**; some species benefit. In June 2010 researchers found that young quolls fed toad meat **laced** with a **nausea**-inducing chemical learned to avoid toads and fared better.

Biological control can **triumph**: from 1926 a **moth**, Cactoblastis, cleared most prickly pear from Queensland and New South Wales within about a decade. In October 1984 Australia **enacted** a Biological Control Act; agents now face years of **stringent** **scrutiny** before release. The **blunder** of 1935 was **haste**: once loose, an **untested** ally may never be **recalled**.
