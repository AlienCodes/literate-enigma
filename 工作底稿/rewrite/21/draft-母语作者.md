# EN: The Song That Swept the Sea

Humpbacks, **colossal** whales travelling between polar feeding grounds and warm breeding waters, are **renowned** **vocalists**. Only males sing, probably in **courtship**. Short sounds **constitute** phrases, which **recur** as themes in an **elaborate** **hierarchy**. A song lasts minutes, its **repetition** hours. A population's males sing an **identical** song and **conform** as it **evolves** **subtly** each year.

From 1995 to 1998, researchers recorded humpbacks on their **seasonal** **migration** along eastern Australia's **coastline**, using hydrophones, or underwater **microphones**. At first, two of 82 singers **debuted** a **distinctive** new **melody**, **utterly** unlike the **reigning** **chorus**. These **newcomers** **heralded** what researchers called a "revolution".

Within about two years, **virtually** every singer had **converted**. By 1998 the old song, **displaced** **wholesale**, was **extinct**. The **outsider** was the song of the Indian Ocean population off western Australia, apparently **ferried** east by a few foreign singers. A study published in Nature in November 2000 called the **takeover** **unparalleled** among animal **vocal** traditions.

Nor was it a **freak**. A study published in Current Biology in April 2011 **compiled** about a decade of **recordings** from six South Pacific populations. Song types usually arose off eastern Australia, then **rippled** **eastward** through **successive** populations to French Polynesia, **generally** crossing the **region** within about two years.

This is **persuasive** evidence of **cultural** **transmission**: whales **acquire** songs from one another. Why males sing and **overhaul** songs remains debated. One idea is that whales have an **appetite** for **novelty**. Some **scholars** **ponder** whether this is culture in the human sense. Somewhere off Australia, a **handful** of strangers had **remade** a whole population's **repertoire**.
