# EN: The Song That Swept the Sea

Only male humpbacks, large migratory baleen whales, sing, **predominantly** on their **seasonal** breeding grounds. Their **haunting** **verse** has an **elaborate** **hierarchy**: units form phrases, whose **repetition** builds themes. One **rendition** lasts minutes and **recurs** for hours, probably for **courtship**. Males in a population sing **uniformly**, and their **anthem** **evolves** **progressively** each year.

From 1995 to 1998, researchers **deployed** hydrophones, or underwater microphones, to **compile** recordings of humpbacks migrating along the eastern Australian **coastline**. In the mid-1990s, two of 82 recorded singers **emitted** a **distinctive** new song. So **marginal** a **minority** **scarcely** seemed **capable** of **swaying** so **unanimous** a **chorus**.

Yet within about two years **virtually** every singer had **converted**, and by 1998 the **rival** song alone was **audible**. A study published in Nature in November 2000 **ascribed** it to the west-coast population in the Indian Ocean, apparently via a few **outsiders**. What researchers called a "revolution" had **overthrown** an **entrenched** **repertoire** **wholesale**.

A study published in Current Biology in April 2011 **broadened** the **lens**. **Analysing** over a decade of recordings from six South Pacific populations, it found song types **rippling** **eastward** through **successive** populations to French Polynesia. Most **arose** off eastern Australia, and each **transfer** **generally** took about two years.

Such **cultural** **transmission** is **persuasive** evidence that whales **acquire** traditions from one another. Why whales sing, and why songs usually change gradually yet **occasionally** **abruptly**, remain **controversial**. One **conjecture** is that **novelty** **stimulates** change. Some researchers **resist** calling it culture in the human sense. Somewhere off eastern Australia, a **handful** of strangers had **reshaped** an entire coast's music.
