# EN: The Song That Swept the Sea

Each winter, male humpback whales, and only males, fill their **breeding grounds** with an **eerie**, **haunting** **melody**. Units form phrases, whose **repetition** **constructs** themes, an **elaborate** **hierarchy**. A song lasts minutes, is sung for hours and is thought to **facilitate** **courtship**. Every male in a population sings an **identical** version, which **evolves** **annually**.

That is the rule. Off eastern Australia, it broke. From 1995 to 1998, **researchers** used hydrophones, or underwater **microphones**, to record whales on their **migration** along the **coastline**. In the mid-1990s, **merely** two of 82 singers **emitted** a **strikingly** **distinctive** new song, **conspicuous** **intruders** in a **consistent** **chorus**.

Within about two years, **virtually** every singer had **abruptly** **converted**; by 1998 the old song was **displaced** **wholesale**. The new song was no local **innovation**. A study published in Nature in November 2000 found it **resembled** that of the west-coast, Indian Ocean population, apparently brought by a **handful** of "foreign" **performers**.

The **transformation** was not **unparalleled**. A study published in Current Biology in April 2011 **analysed** six South Pacific populations over a decade. Song types **primarily** arose off eastern Australia, then **rippled** **eastward** through **adjacent** populations to the **archipelago** of French Polynesia, usually within about two years.

Such speed rules out **genetic** **inheritance**. Whales **evidently** learn by **imitation**, a **persuasive** case of **cultural** **transmission**. Why they sing and **overhaul** their songs remains **controversial**. One idea is that they favour **novelty**. Some researchers are **reluctant** to **define** this as culture in the human sense. Somewhere off eastern Australia, a few **outsiders** once rewrote an entire population's music.
