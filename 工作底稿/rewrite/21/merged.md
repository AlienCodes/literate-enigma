# EN: The Song That Swept the Sea

Each winter, male humpback whales, and only males, fill their **breeding grounds** with a **haunting** **melody**. Units **constitute** phrases, and phrases build themes in an **elaborate** **hierarchy**. Each **rendition** lasts minutes and **recurs** for hours, probably as part of **courtship**. All the males in a population sing an **identical** version, which **evolves** **subtly** each year.

That is the rule. Off eastern Australia, it broke. From 1995 to 1998, **researchers** used hydrophones, or underwater **microphones**, to record humpbacks on their **seasonal** **migration** along the **coastline**. In the mid-1990s, **merely** two of 82 singers **emitted** a **distinctive** new song, **utterly** unlike the **reigning** **chorus**.

Within about two years, **virtually** every singer had **converted**, and by 1998 the old song had been **displaced** **wholesale**. A study published in Nature in November 2000 found that the **newcomer** **resembled** the song of the Indian Ocean population off western Australia, apparently **ferried** east by a **handful** of foreign **performers**.

Nor was it a **freak**. A study published in Current Biology in April 2011 **analysed** over a decade of **recordings** from six South Pacific populations. Song types **primarily** arose off eastern Australia, then **rippled** **eastward** through **successive** populations to French Polynesia, **generally** crossing the **region** within about two years.

Change this fast points to **imitation** rather than **inheritance**, a **persuasive** case of **cultural** **transmission**. Why males sing, and why they **overhaul** their songs, remains **controversial**. One idea is that whales have an **appetite** for **novelty**. Some **scholars** are **reluctant** to **define** this as culture in the human sense. Somewhere off eastern Australia, a few **outsiders** once **reshaped** an entire population's music.
