# 第59篇（光之殇）来源摘录（WebSearch 两轮，2026-10-03）
S1 Fabian et al. 2024, Nature Communications (Imperial College London; Samuel Fabian): high-resolution motion capture in a London lab plus stereo video of wild insects in the cloud forest of Monteverde, Costa Rica. Insects do not steer straight at lights; they tilt their backs (dorsal side) towards the light — the "dorsal light response", a reflex that normally keeps them level by turning the back to the brightest region (the sky). A point light traps them in circling orbits; a moth flying over a UV light flipped upside down. Across 477 field recordings covering ten insect orders, insects almost never flew straight at the light. Resolved a long debate.
S2 Lewis et al. 2020, BioScience (Tufts University, Sara Lewis): survey of firefly experts worldwide — habitat loss the top threat, light pollution second, pesticides third. Many fireflies rely on bioluminescent courtship flashes; when nights are too bright they struggle to see each other's signals.
S3 Tufts University lab studies (Avalon Owens & Sara Lewis), Photinus obscurellus pairs: males normally emit about 10 flash patterns a minute; under artificial light about 5. Females responded about half as often under red and blue light, hardly at all under cool or warm white, and never under amber light. No flashing, no mating.
S4 Boyes et al. 2021, Science Advances (UKCEH & Newcastle University, Douglas Boyes): 400+ hours sampling caterpillars at 55 lit and unlit roadside sites in southern England (Thames Valley). Street lighting cut moth caterpillar numbers by 47% in hedgerows and 33% in grass margins. White LED lights worse (52% fewer in hedgerows) than yellow sodium lamps (41%). A separate experiment showed light disrupted caterpillar feeding; caterpillars at lit sites were typically heavier (faster development).
S5 Knop et al. 2017, Nature (University of Bern; Eva Knop): 14 similar cabbage-thistle meadows in the Swiss Alpine foothills; mobile street lamps set up in 7. Light cut night-time pollinator visits by 62%; 29% fewer species visited lit fields (of nearly 300 night-visiting species). Plants set fewer fruit; daytime pollinators did not make up the loss.
S6 Falchi et al. 2016, Science Advances (World Atlas of artificial night sky brightness): more than 80% of the world's people and more than 99% of people in the US and Europe live under light-polluted skies; the Milky Way is hidden from more than one-third of humanity, including 60% of Europeans and nearly 80% of North Americans.
S7 Kyba et al. 2023, Science (Globe at Night): 51,000+ citizen-scientist star counts, 2011–2022; the night sky brightened by about 9.6% a year (stars visible fell accordingly); at that rate a child born where 250 stars are visible would see about 100 by their 18th birthday.
