In a Thai rainforest, a carpenter ant **abandons** the **orderly** column marching along the **canopy** and **descends**. It **staggers** **aimlessly** over low **foliage**, as if **tipsy**. Around noon it **clamps** its **jaws** onto the **underside** of a leaf, **gripping** a major **vein** about 25 centimetres above the forest floor.

By **dusk** the ant is dead. Its jaw muscles have been **wrecked**, so the **clasp** holds after death. Two or three days later a **stalk** **sprouts** from its head and **sheds** **spores** onto the ground below, where **foraging** **nestmates** can pick them up.

The **culprit** is a **parasitic** fungus, Ophiocordyceps unilateralis. Its choice of **perch** is strikingly **precise**. Most bites land on the north-west side of plants, where **humidity** and temperature suit the fungus. When David Hughes and his **colleagues** moved infected ants higher, the fungus grew **abnormally**. On the ground, the **corpses** **vanished**.

How does a fungus **steer** an insect. A 2017 study in PNAS used three-dimensional **microscopy** to look inside. Fungal cells had formed an **interconnected** **mesh** **encircling** the muscle **fibres** of the legs and jaw. They had not entered the brain at all.

The **partnership** is **ancient**. A 48-million-year-old **fossilised** leaf from Messel, in Germany, bears the same **telltale** bite **scars**.

In 2013 the video game The Last of Us **borrowed** the idea. Its creators at Naughty Dog drew on a **segment** of the BBC series Planet Earth, **narrated** by David Attenborough. Neil Druckmann **pondered** what would happen if the fungus jumped to humans.

The **mark** a dying insect leaves on a leaf has outlasted **continents** drifting, and now reaches **gamers**.
