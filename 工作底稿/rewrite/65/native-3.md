Deep in a Thai **rainforest**, a **carpenter** ant breaks from its **colony**'s **column** in the **lofty** **canopy**. **Swaying** on **unsteady** legs, it **wanders** **downward**, as if drunk, through low **shrubs** and **undergrowth**. Around **midday** it **clenches** its **jaws** on a major **rib** **underneath** a leaf, about 25 **centimetres** above the ground.

By **sunset** the insect is **lifeless**, its jaw **muscles** destroyed and its **posthumous** grip **unyielding**. Two or three days later a **fungal** stalk pushes from its head and **sprinkles** **infectious** spores below. **Unsuspecting** nestmates searching for food pick them up.

The **invader**, Ophiocordyceps unilateralis, is **fussy** about **location**. In 2011 David Hughes and colleagues found most **bites** on the **northwest** side of plants, where **humid**, warm conditions were **favourable** to the fungus. When researchers moved infected ants higher, the fungus grew **abnormally**. On the ground, the bodies **disappeared**, eaten or **flushed** away.

In 2017 a Penn State team, reporting in PNAS, examined infected ants with **three-dimensional** **microscope** imaging. Fungal cells formed an **intertwined** **lattice** around muscle fibres in the legs and jaws, like the **cords** of a **puppet**. Yet the fungus had not **infiltrated** the brain.

The **trick** is old. A 48-million-year-old **fossil** leaf from Messel, Germany, bears the same **characteristic** bite **scars**.

Naughty Dog's 2013 game The Last of Us was **seeded** by a Planet Earth episode that showed such an ant, with David Attenborough's **narration**. Neil Druckmann **mused** on what would happen if the fungus could **hop** to humans.

A **fictional** **storyline** of human **terror** began with one ant clinging to one leaf.
