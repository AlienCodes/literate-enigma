## 英文

In a Thai rainforest, a carpenter ant breaks away from its **colony**'s trail through the **canopy** and heads **downward**. It begins to **wander** over low **shrubs**, **swaying** like a drunk. Around **midday** it **clenches** its **jaw** on a main **vein** **underneath** a leaf, roughly 25 centimetres above the ground.

By **dusk** the ant is dead. Its jaw muscles have been **shredded**, so the grip holds on the **lifeless** body. Two or three days later a stalk pushes out of its head and **sprinkles** spores onto the ground below, where other ants searching for food can pick them up.

The organism responsible is a **fungal** **invader**, Ophiocordyceps unilateralis, and its choice of **site** is **selective**. Most bites land on the **northwest** side of plants, where the air is **humid** and the temperature suits the fungus. When researchers moved infected ants higher, the fungus grew **abnormally**. On the ground, the bodies **disappeared**.

A 2017 study in PNAS looked inside with a three-dimensional **microscope** technique. Fungal cells had formed a linked network **entangled** with the **muscle** fibres of the legs and jaw. The **resemblance** to a **puppet** on strings is hard to miss, yet not a single **cellular** thread had entered the brain.

The behaviour is old. A 48-million-year-old **fossil** leaf from Messel, in Germany, bears the same bite **scars**.

In 2013 the video game The Last of Us built its **storyline** on the idea. Naughty Dog drew on a Planet Earth episode, with David Attenborough as **narrator**, showing an ant killed by the fungus. Neil Druckmann imagined a **scenario** in which it jumped to humans.

Scars pressed into a leaf at Messel show that the bite is far older than the camera that filmed it.
