## 英文
In a Thai **rainforest**, a carpenter ant breaks from its **colony**'s **column** high in the **canopy**. It reels **downwards** into the **undergrowth**, **clumsily**, **dizzy** and **disoriented**, as if **intoxicated**. Around **midday** it **fastens** its **jaws** on the underside of a leaf **blade**, biting **firmly** into a major **vein** about 25 **centimetres** above the ground.

The ant carries Ophiocordyceps unilateralis, a **fungal** **invader**. By **dusk** the insect is **lifeless**, yet the bite holds. The fungus has **shredded** the jaw muscles, so they cannot **loosen**. Researchers call it the death grip.

Two or three days later an **elongated** **shaft** grows from the dead ant's **skull**. This fruiting body drops a **dust** of spores on to the ground below, where foraging ants **tread** and can pick them up.

The **site** matters. Most such leaves lie on the **northwest** side of plants, where **humid** air and temperature are **optimal** for the fungus. When researchers moved infected ants higher, the fungus grew **abnormally**. On the ground, the bodies **disappeared**.

In 2017 a team including David Hughes at Penn State examined infected ants under a **3D** **microscope**. Fungal cells had formed a **lattice** around muscle fibres in the legs and jaw, **entwined** with them. None had **infiltrated** the brain.

The death grip is **archaic**. A 48-million-year-old **fossil** leaf from Messel, in Germany, bears its **hallmark**, the same **characteristic** bite **imprints**.

A BBC Planet Earth film with David Attenborough's **narration** showed one such ant. It **spurred** The Last of Us, a 2013 video game. Neil Druckmann, who made it, **envisioned** the parasite jumping to humans.

That **hypothetical** **scenario** became **fictional** horror for **gamers**. The real parasite does its work **quietly** on a leaf, a hand's breadth above the **topsoil**.
