## 英文

In a Thai rainforest, a carpenter ant leaves its **colony**'s trail through the **canopy** and heads **downward**. It starts to **wander** **randomly** over the **leafy** **undergrowth**, **swaying** like a drunk. Around **midday** it **clenches** its **jaw** **tightly** on a main **vein** **underneath** a leaf, about 25 **centimetres** above the ground.

By **dusk** the ant is dead. Its jaw muscles have been **shredded**, so the grip holds **firmly** on the **lifeless** body. Two or three days later a stalk pushes out of its head and **sprinkles** **infectious** spores onto the ground below. **Unsuspecting** ants searching for food can pick them up there.

The organism responsible is a **fungal** **invader**, Ophiocordyceps unilateralis, and it is **picky** about **location**. Most bites land on the **northwest** side of plants, where the air is **humid** and the temperature suits the fungus. When researchers moved infected ants higher, the fungus grew **abnormally**. On the ground, the bodies **disappeared**.

A 2017 study in PNAS peered inside with three-dimensional **microscope** imaging. Fungal cells had formed a linked **lattice**, **entangled** with the **muscle** fibres of the legs and jaw. The picture **evokes** a **puppet** on strings. Yet the fungus did not **infiltrate** the brain at all.

The habit has deep **geological** roots. A 48-million-year-old **fossil** leaf from Messel, in Germany, bears the same bite **scars**.

In 2013 the video game The Last of Us built its **storyline** on the idea. Naughty Dog drew on a Planet Earth episode, with David Attenborough as **narrator**, showing an ant killed by the fungus. Neil Druckmann imagined a **scenario** in which it jumped to humans.

Marks **imprinted** on a leaf at Messel show the bite is far older than the camera that filmed it.
