## 英文

Deep in a Thai **rainforest**, a **carpenter** ant breaks away from its **colony**'s **column** in the **lofty** canopy. It **wanders** on **unsteady** legs through the low **shrubs** and **foliage**, as if drunk. Around noon it **clenches** its **jaws** on a major **rib** under a leaf, about 25 **centimetres** above the ground.

By **sunset** the insect is **lifeless**. Its jaw **muscles** have been destroyed, so the grip stays **unyielding** after death. Two or three days later a **fungal** stalk pushes out of its head and **sprinkles** spores on the ground below. **Unsuspecting** nestmates searching for food can pick them up there.

The parasite is Ophiocordyceps unilateralis, and it is **fussy** about **location**. In 2011 David Hughes and colleagues reported that most **bites** landed on the **northwest** side of plants, where the **humid** air and warmth were **favourable** to the fungus. When researchers moved infected ants higher, the fungus grew **abnormally**. Left on the ground, the bodies **disappeared**, eaten or **flushed** away.

The mind of the ant is not the target. In 2017 a team studied infected ants with **three-dimensional** **microscope** imaging. Fungal cells had formed an **intertwined** **lattice** around the muscle fibres of the legs and jaws, like the **cords** of a **puppet**. Yet the fungus did not enter the brain at all. It worked on the **peripheral** machinery and **bypassed** the **neural** centre.

The **trick** is old. A 48-million-year-old **fossil** leaf from Messel, Germany, carries the same **characteristic** bite **scars**.

Naughty Dog drew on it for The Last of Us, a 2013 game. The idea came from a Planet Earth episode with David Attenborough's **narration**. Neil Druckmann **mused** on what would happen if it jumped to humans.

A **storyline** of human **terror** began with one ant clinging to one leaf.
