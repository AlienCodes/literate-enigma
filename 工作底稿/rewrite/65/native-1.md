In a Thai rainforest, a carpenter ant **abandons** the **orderly** **procession** of its colony along the **canopy** and **staggers** down into the **undergrowth**. Its **erratic** gait looks almost **drunken**. Around midday it **clamps** its jaws onto the **underside** of a leaf, gripping a major **vein** about 25 centimetres above the forest floor.

By **dusk** the ant is dead. Its jaw muscles have been **wrecked**, so the **grip** holds long after its **demise**. Two or three days later a **stalk** **sprouts** from its head and showers **spores** onto the ground beneath, where **foraging** nestmates can collect them.

The **parasite** is Ophiocordyceps unilateralis, and its **choice** of **perch** is **meticulous**. In a 2011 study, David Hughes and colleagues found that **doomed** ants mostly bit leaves on the north-west side of plants, where **humidity** and temperature suit the fungus. When researchers **shifted** infected ants higher, the fungus grew **abnormally**. On the ground, the **corpses** **vanished**.

How does a fungus **steer** an animal? In 2017 a team **scanning** infected ants with three-dimensional **microscopy** found fungal cells woven into an **interconnected** **mesh** around muscle **fibres** in the legs and jaw. The fungus never entered the brain.

The **tactic** is **ancient**. A 48-million-year-old **fossilised** leaf from Messel, Germany, bears the same **telltale** bite **scars**.

The parasite has also **colonised** popular culture. The Last of Us, a 2013 game by Naughty Dog, was **inspired** by a Planet Earth **sequence** narrated by David Attenborough. Neil Druckmann **pondered** what would happen if it jumped to humans.

Millions of players have since **shuddered** at a horror that began on a single leaf.
