# EN: The Company That Invented Its Own Killer

In 1975 Steven Sasson, a Kodak engineer in Rochester, built the first digital camera. The **cumbersome** **gadget**, an eight-pound **heavyweight**, was **toaster**-sized. Its first **rudimentary** image, of Joy Marshall in December, had 10,000 black-and-white **pixels**. Saving it to a **magnetic** **cassette** took 23 seconds, and **retrieval** used a **playback** unit and television.

The device won **admiration** from technical colleagues, but it used no film. By Sasson's **recollection**, **management** was **dismissive**, finding it cute and urging **secrecy**. Kodak's **revenues** **hinged** on selling film, its **profitable** **cornerstone**. Sasson won a patent in 1978, yet the camera never reached production or **fruition**.

Digital **photography** proved **disruptive** to that **cash-cow**. In June 2009 Kodak chose to **phase** out Kodachrome, blaming falling demand in the digital **era**. Since 2003 it had **slashed** 47,000 jobs through **layoffs** and ordered the **shutdown** of 13 factories and about 130 processing labs. The **downfall** was gathering **momentum**.

In January 2012 the 131-year-old **downsized** firm filed for Chapter 11, burdened by **hefty** **pension** **commitments** and years of **procrastination** over digital technology. It kept 19,000 staff, against 145,000 at its 1980s peak. With the firm in **jeopardy**, Citigroup, as **lender**, supplied $950 million for **restructuring**. Kodak sold or licensed its **portfolio** of 1,100 digital imaging patents to a 12-firm **consortium**.

Sasson's **decoration** came separately. In 2009 Barack Obama gave him the National Medal of Technology and Innovation, a **commendation** of **prestige**, for **innovative** digital camera work. Kodak's **sequel** came in 2013, when it left Chapter 11 **refocused** as a **streamlined** **niche** printer, with cameras **disposed** of. Among the **buyers** of its digital imaging patents were Apple and Google.
