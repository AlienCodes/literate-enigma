# EN: The Company That Invented Its Own Killer

In 1975 Steven Sasson, a Kodak engineer in Rochester, built the first digital camera. The **toaster**-sized **gadget**, a **cumbersome** **heavyweight** of about 8 pounds, was no **handheld**. Its first **primitive** picture, of Joy Marshall, had 100 by 100 **pixels**. A **cassette** took 23 seconds to record it, and **retrieval** needed a **playback** unit and television.

Technical staff liked the camera. **Management** gave a **dismissive** **reaction**, roughly that it was cute but should be kept quiet. Film was the **backbone** of Kodak's **revenues** and **earnings**, and a **mass-market** **franchise** had no **strategic** **scenario** for a filmless camera. A patent followed in 1978. Production never did.

The digital **disruption** reached film. In 2009 Kodak announced the end of Kodachrome, part of its **portfolio**, citing falling digital-age demand. The **timeline** of the **downfall** was long, the **outlook** clear. Since 2003 **redundancies** had **slashed** 47,000 jobs. **Layoffs** came with the **shutdown** of 13 factories and about 130 labs at the **downsized** firm.

On 19 January 2012 the 131-year-old Kodak sought Chapter 11 protection from **creditors**, **saddled** with **pension** **obligations** and years of **procrastination** over digital. Some 19,000 staff were left. Citigroup, a **lender**, provided $950 million. **Restructuring** sold its **intellectual** property, about 1,100 digital patents, for $525 million to a **consortium** of twelve **buyers**.

Sasson's **laurels** came first. In November 2009 Barack Obama **decorated** him with the National Medal of Technology and Innovation for his **innovative** camera. In 2013 a **streamlined**, **modestly** sized Kodak left Chapter 11, **refocused** and **remade** as a **niche** printing firm. Its camera **glory** was over. Apple and Google, two digital **challengers**, were among the patent buyers.
