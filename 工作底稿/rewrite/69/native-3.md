In the **fierce** heat of summer 1858, the curtains of the new Houses of Parliament hung heavy with chloride of **lime**, a **bleach** meant to make the air **tolerable**. Outside, the Thames **festered**, and members **contemplated** an **exodus**.

The river had become the **metropolis**'s **lavatory**. **Antiquated** sewers and **outlets** **discharged** the **untreated** **sewage** of some three million **dwellers** into its **currents**, and in the heat this **excrement** began to **decay**. The **vile** **fumes** grew so **overpowering** that the episode earned a name, the Great Stink.

**Disgust** found a voice on 15 July, when the **Chancellor** of the Exchequer, Benjamin Disraeli, **denounced** the river as a Stygian pool **reeking** of **unbearable** horrors. A bill to pay for new sewers was **fast-tracked** into law in about 18 days.

The engineer Joseph Bazalgette **masterminded** the response. His 82 miles of great intercepting sewers caught the flow before it reached the river and **diverted** it **downstream**, east of the city. Some 1,100 miles of street sewers served as **tributaries**, and the whole **infrastructure** took 318 million **bricks**. Having worked out what **calibre** was **adequate** for each pipe, he doubled it. The **backbone** lies **buried** **underneath** the new Victoria, Albert and Chelsea Embankments. The network was **substantially** complete by 1875, and much of it is in use today.

Yet London's **sanitation** rested on a **fallacy**. The **predominant** **belief**, miasma, blamed disease on foul air and **vapours**, so the sewers were intended **principally** to **suppress** the smell. They worked for another reason, guarding the **purity** of the water Londoners drank.

In 1866 cholera killed about 5,600 people in the East End, the only **locality** whose connection was still **pending**. London has seen no major cholera epidemic since.
