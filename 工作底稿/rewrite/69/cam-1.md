In the **sweltering** summer of 1858, **legislators** in the **newly** finished Houses of Parliament **debated** behind curtains **drenched** in chloride of lime, a **bleach** meant to **mask** the **odour** **wafting** from the Thames.

The river had become the **dumping** ground for the **sewage** of some three million Londoners, **discharged** through **antiquated** sewers and **outfalls** straight into the water. That summer the heat made the **putrefying** **effluent** **unbearable**, and the episode became known as the Great Stink.

Members **contemplated** **decamping** altogether. On 15 July Benjamin Disraeli, the **Chancellor** of the **Exchequer**, likened the river to a **Stygian** pool **reeking** with **intolerable** horrors. The government **hurried** a bill through Parliament to **finance** new sewers, and it became law in about eighteen days.

The engineer Joseph Bazalgette **devised** some 82 miles of **intercepting** sewers that **captured** the **outflow** before it reached the river and **conveyed** it east, **downstream** of the city. **Beneath** the streets ran about 1,100 miles of smaller sewers, **requiring** roughly 318 million **bricks**. He calculated the **diameter** each pipe needed, then doubled it. The main **conduits** lie **buried** under the new Victoria, Albert and Chelsea Embankments. The system was **largely** complete by 1875, and much of it is in use today.

The **irony** is that its **architects** **subscribed** to the **prevailing** miasma theory, which held that disease spread through **noxious** air. The sewers were built **chiefly** to **banish** the smell. They worked because they kept sewage out of the water Londoners drank.

The **verdict** arrived in 1866. Cholera killed about 5,600 people in the East End, the only **district** not yet linked to the **network**. London **suffered** no major cholera **outbreak** afterwards.
