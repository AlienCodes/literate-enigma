In the **oppressive** summer of 1858, the **legislature** met in the new Houses of Parliament behind curtains soaked in chloride of **lime**. The **urgency** of the measure was plain. It was meant to make the **pungent** air rising from the Thames **tolerable**.

For years the river had served as the **outlet** for the **excrement** of some three million Londoners, **discharged** through **antiquated** sewers straight into the water. In that summer's heat the **festering** mass gave off an **overpowering** smell, and the episode passed into history as the Great Stink.

Members **contemplated** an **exodus** from the building. On 15 July Benjamin Disraeli, then **Chancellor** of the Exchequer, **characterised** the river as a **hellish** pool **brimming** with **unbearable** horrors. Ministers rushed through a bill to pay for new sewers, and its **enactment** took about eighteen days.

The engineer Joseph Bazalgette laid out some 82 miles of great intercepting sewers. They **diverted** the **wastewater** before it reached the river and sent it east, **downstream** of the city. **Underneath** the streets ran about 1,100 miles of smaller lines, which **consumed** roughly 318 million **bricks**. He worked out the **breadth** each pipe needed, then doubled it. The main **collector** sewers lie **buried** beneath the new Victoria, Albert and Chelsea Embankments. The system was **substantially** finished by 1875, and much of it is in use today.

The twist lies in its **rationale**. Most experts held the **misguided** **belief**, known as miasma, that disease travelled through **tainted** air. The sewers were designed largely to **purge** the stench. They succeeded for a different reason. They kept sewage out of the water Londoners drank.

The **grimmest** test came in 1866. Cholera killed about 5,600 people in the East End, the only part of the **metropolis** not yet joined to the intercepting system. Cholera did not return to the city on that scale again.
