In the **fierce** heat of summer 1858, the curtains of the new Houses of Parliament hung heavy with chloride of **lime**, a **bleach** meant to make the air **tolerable**. Outside, the Thames **festered**, and members **contemplated** an **exodus**.

The river had become the **metropolis**'s **lavatory**. Old sewers and **outlets** **discharged** the **untreated** **sewage** of some three million city **dwellers** straight into its **currents**. In the hot weather this **excrement** began to **decay**, and the smell grew so **pungent** and **overpowering** that it earned a name, the Great Stink.

**Disgust** found a voice on 15 July, when Benjamin Disraeli, the **Chancellor** of the Exchequer, **denounced** the river as a Stygian pool reeking of **unbearable** horrors. **Consequently**, the government **fast-tracked** a bill to pay for new sewers, and it became law in about 18 days.

The engineer Joseph Bazalgette **masterminded** the response. His 82 miles of great intercepting sewers caught the flow before it reached the river and **diverted** it east, **downstream** of the city. A further 1,100 miles of street sewers acted as **tributaries**, and the whole **infrastructure** took some 318 million **bricks**. Having worked out what **calibre** would be **adequate** for each pipe, he doubled it. The **backbone** of the system lies **buried** **underneath** the new Victoria, Albert and Chelsea Embankments. The work was **substantially** complete by 1875, and much of it is in use today.

Yet London's **sanitation** rested on a **fallacy**. The **dominant** **belief** of the day, miasma, held that disease spread through foul air and **vapours**, so the sewers were intended **principally** to clear the smell. Their **notable** success had another cause. They protected the **purity** of the water Londoners drank.

The **evidence** was **stark**. In 1866 cholera killed about 5,600 people in the East End, the only **locality** whose link to the new system was still **pending**. London has seen no major cholera epidemic since.
