## 英文

In the **oppressive** summer of 1858, **statesmen** sat in the new Houses of Parliament behind curtains soaked in chloride of **lime**, a **desperate** **expedient** to make the **pungent** air off the Thames **tolerable**.

The river was the **outlet** for the **untreated** **sewage** of some three million Londoners, **discharged** through **antiquated** sewers straight into it. In the heat, the **festering** **excrement** **generated** an **overpowering**, **sickening** smell, remembered as the Great Stink.

Members **contemplated** an **exodus**. On 15 July Benjamin Disraeli, the **Chancellor** of the Exchequer, **characterised** the river as a **hellish** pool **brimming** with **unbearable** horrors. With sudden **urgency**, a bill to pay for new sewers became law in about eighteen days.

The engineer Joseph Bazalgette **masterminded** some 82 miles of large intercepting sewers, which **diverted** the **filthy** flow before it reached the river and sent it east, **downstream** of the **populous** city. **Underneath** the streets ran about 1,100 miles of smaller **tributaries**, and roughly 318 million **bricks** went into the **masonry**. He worked out the **breadth** each **sewer** needed, then doubled it. The main **collector** lines lie **buried** under the new Victoria, Albert and Chelsea Embankments. The system was **substantially** complete by 1875, and much of it is in use today.

Medical opinion mostly held the **misguided** **belief**, known as miasma, that disease spread through **tainted** air. The sewers were designed chiefly to **purge** the **stinking** **fumes**. Their gain for **hygiene** came almost **accidentally**, by keeping sewage out of the water Londoners drank.

**Verification** came **starkly** in 1866. Cholera killed about 5,600 people in the East End, the only **locality** not yet joined to the intercepting system. The **metropolis** saw no major cholera epidemic afterwards.
