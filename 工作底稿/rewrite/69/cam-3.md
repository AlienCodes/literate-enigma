## 英文

In the **oppressive** summer of 1858, the **legislature** sat in the new Houses of Parliament behind curtains soaked in chloride of **lime**, a **desperate** **expedient** to make the **pungent** air off the Thames **tolerable**.

The river was the **outlet** for the **untreated** **sewage** of some three million Londoners, **discharged** through **antiquated** sewers straight into the water. In the heat, the **festering** **excrement** **generated** an **overpowering**, **sickening** smell, and the episode passed into history as the Great Stink.

Members **contemplated** an **exodus**. On 15 July Benjamin Disraeli, the **Chancellor** of the Exchequer, **characterised** the river as a **hellish** pool **brimming** with **unbearable** horrors. With sudden **urgency**, the government pushed through a bill to pay for new sewers, and its **enactment** took about eighteen days.

The engineer Joseph Bazalgette **masterminded** some 82 miles of large intercepting sewers. These **diverted** the **filthy** flow before it reached the river and sent it east, **downstream** of the **populous** city. **Underneath** the streets ran about 1,100 miles of smaller lines, and roughly 318 million **bricks** went into the work. He worked out the **breadth** each pipe needed, then doubled it. The main **collector** sewers lie **buried** under the new Victoria, Albert and Chelsea Embankments. The system was **substantially** complete by 1875, and much of it is in use today.

The **twist** concerns its **purpose**. Most doctors held the **misguided** **belief**, known as miasma, that disease spread through **tainted** air. The sewers were designed largely to **purge** the smell. They worked for another reason, keeping sewage out of the water Londoners drank.

The **cruellest** **confirmation** arrived in 1866. Cholera killed about 5,600 people in the East End, the only part of the **metropolis** not yet joined to the intercepting system. The city saw no major cholera epidemic afterwards.
