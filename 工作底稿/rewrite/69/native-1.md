## 英文

In the **sweltering** summer of 1858, the curtains of the new Houses of Parliament were **drenched** in chloride of lime, a **bleach**, to make the air **bearable**. Outside, the Thames **reeked**.

By then the **sewage** of some three million Londoners **poured** straight into the river through **antiquated** **drains** and **outlets**. In the heat it **rotted**, and the **stench** grew so **overpowering** that it became known as the Great Stink.

Parliament found it **intolerable**. Members **contemplated** moving out, and on 15 July Benjamin Disraeli, the **Chancellor** of the Exchequer, **likened** the river to a Stygian pool full of **unbearable** horrors. The government **hurried** through a **bill** to **finance** new sewers, and it became law in about 18 days.

The task fell to the **engineer** Joseph Bazalgette. He laid some 82 miles of great **intercepting** sewers that **trapped** the **outflow** before it reached the river and **conveyed** it east, **downstream** of the city. Beneath the streets ran a further 1,100 miles of smaller sewers, made from roughly 318 million **bricks**. Having worked out the **diameter** each pipe needed, he **doubled** it. The main **channels** were **buried** beneath the new Victoria, Albert and Chelsea **Embankments**. The network was largely finished by 1875, and much of it is in use today.

The **prevailing** theory held that disease spread through foul air, or miasma, so the sewers were meant above all to **banish** the smell. They worked for another reason. They kept sewage out of the water Londoners drank.

In 1866 cholera killed about 5,600 people in the East End, the only **district** not yet **linked** to the new system. London saw no major **outbreak** of the disease again.
