# EN: The Man Who Gave Machines a Mind

In May 1936 Alan Turing, a **youthful** **mathematical** talent born in June 1912, **submitted** a paper of **remarkable** **ambition**. Using **logic**, it imagined a **hypothetical** machine that could perform any **computation** if given the right instructions. **Abstract** and **theoretical**, the **vision** became the **blueprint** for modern computers.

On 4 September 1939 he joined the **wartime** codebreakers at Bletchley Park, leading work on **naval** Enigma, the German cipher machine. He designed a **device** that searched **intercepted**, **encrypted** signals for each day's **rotor** **settings**; the first became **operational** in March 1940. Polish mathematicians had **cracked** Enigma in 1932 and shared their **insight** in July 1939.

In a paper published in Mind in October 1950, he asked "Can machines think?" Leaving the **definition** of **consciousness** to **philosophers**, he **proposed** a **practical** **criterion**. A judge exchanges typed messages with two **anonymous** **correspondents**, one human, one machine. If the judge cannot reliably **distinguish** them, the machine passes. His **provocative** game is now the Turing test.

On 31 March 1952 he was **convicted** of "gross indecency", the **offence** then used against gay men, for an **intimate** relationship with a man. To **avert** prison he accepted **hormone** **injections** under **probation**; his security **clearance** was **revoked**. He died of cyanide poisoning on 7 June 1954. A **coroner**'s inquest ruled **suicide**, though some doubt it.

**Recognition** and **remorse** came **slowly**. In September 2009 the **prime** minister **publicly** **apologised**; a **posthumous** royal pardon was **granted** in December 2013. On 23 June 2021, his birthday, a £50 note bearing his **portrait** entered **circulation**. Britain prosecuted him in 1952 and put his **likeness** on its money in 2021.
