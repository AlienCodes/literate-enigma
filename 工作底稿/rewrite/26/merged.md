# EN: The Man Who Gave Machines a Mind

Born in June 1912, Alan Turing was a **formidable** **intellect** by his early twenties. In a paper read in November 1936, he **outlined** a "universal machine": a single **device** able to **execute** any **computation**, given the right instructions. That **abstract** **construct**, built from **mathematical** **logic**, became the **theoretical** **blueprint** of the modern computer.

On 4 September 1939 he joined the **classified** effort at Bletchley Park, leading work on **naval** Enigma, Germany's **encryption** machine. Polish mathematicians had **deciphered** Enigma by late 1932 and shared their **insights** with Britain and France in July 1939. **Exploiting** them, his machines, **operational** from March 1940, **automated** the **laborious** search for each day's **rotor** settings.

In a paper published in Mind in October 1950, he **posed** a **provocative** question: "Can machines think?" He **proposed** a **practical** **substitute**, the imitation game, now **widely** called the Turing test. An **interrogator** exchanges typed messages with a **concealed** human and an **anonymous** machine. If the interrogator cannot **consistently** **distinguish** them, the machine passes.

His **exceptional** **wartime** **contribution** did not protect him. On 31 March 1952 he was **convicted** of "gross indecency", the charge then used against gay men. To **avert** **imprisonment**, he **submitted** to **hormone** **injections** and **forfeited** his security **clearance**. He died of cyanide poisoning on 7 June 1954. An inquest ruled it **suicide**, though some doubt it.

**Recognition** came **slowly**. On 10 September 2009 Britain's prime minister **publicly** **apologised**, and on 24 December 2013 Turing was **posthumously** **granted** a royal pardon. On 23 June 2021, his birthday, a fifty-pound note bearing his **portrait** entered **circulation**. The country that **prosecuted** him in 1952 put his face on its money.
