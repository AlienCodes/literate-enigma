# EN: The Man Who Gave Machines a Mind

Alan Turing, born in London on 23 June 1912, had a **striking**, **profound** **insight** early. In a paper read in November 1936, he **outlined** a "universal machine": one **versatile** **device** able to **execute** any **computation** given the right instructions. That **abstract** **construct** became the **theoretical** **framework** of the modern computer.

On 4 September 1939 he joined Bletchley Park and **oversaw** work on **naval** Enigma, the German forces' **encryption** machine. The electromechanical device he designed **systematically** searched for each day's **configuration**, the first becoming **operational** in March 1940. Yet the **achievement** was not **wholly** British: it built on a **pivotal** **mathematical** advance made in Poland in 1932.

In a paper published in Mind in October 1950, he **posed** a **provocative** question, "Can machines think?", and **proposed** an **intriguing** **substitute**: the imitation game. Now **widely** called the Turing test, it asks whether a judge, exchanging typed messages with a **concealed** human and machine, can **consistently** **distinguish** the **participants**.

The country he served treated him **harshly**. On 31 March 1952 he was **convicted** of "gross indecency", the charge then used against gay men. To avoid **imprisonment** he **submitted** to **hormone** **injections**, and his security **clearance** was **withdrawn**. He died of cyanide poisoning on 7 June 1954; an inquest ruled it **suicide**, a **conclusion** some consider **questionable**.

**Formal** **acknowledgement** came late. The prime minister **apologised** **publicly** in September 2009, and Turing was **posthumously** **granted** a royal pardon in December 2013. On 23 June 2021, his birthday, a £50 note bearing his **portrait** **officially** entered **circulation**. His **likeness** now passes through the hands of the country that **prosecuted** him.
