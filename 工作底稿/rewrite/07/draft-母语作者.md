# EN: The Mould That Changed the World

In September 1928 Alexander Fleming, a bacteriologist at St Mary's Hospital in London, returned from holiday to find that a **stray** **mould** had **contaminated** a dish of **bacteria**. Around it, no **germs** grew. The open window of legend is a **misconception**; the spores probably **originated** in a laboratory below his.

Fleming **recognised** that the mould's juice could **inhibit** **harmful** bacteria, and published his findings in June 1929. Yet the **substance** was **crude**, **unstable** and **stubbornly** hard to **purify**. He used it **chiefly** to **isolate** other **microbes**, and for a decade it lay **dormant**.

In Oxford, Howard Florey, an Australian pathologist, **revived** the work with Ernst Chain, a German-born **chemist** and Jewish **refugee**. Their colleague Norman Heatley **improvised** an **ingenious** **apparatus** to **extract** penicillin. On 25 May 1940 they infected eight mice with **lethal** bacteria. Four given penicillin **injections** survived; four left **untreated** died. The Lancet, a **prestigious** journal, reported the results that August.

People need far larger **doses** than mice. In February 1941 Albert Alexander, an Oxford **constable** **gravely** ill with an **infection**, made a **fleeting** **recovery** on penicillin. The **scarce** supply ran out; he died in March. The **obstacle** was now **quantity**. From July 1941 a government laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, **boosted** yields about tenfold and a mouldy **melon** **furnished** a more **productive** strain. By D-Day in June 1944, **industrial** **fermentation** could supply **Allied** forces.

In October 1945 Fleming, Florey and Chain **jointly** won the Nobel prize; Heatley was **overlooked**. **Fortune** had put the mould in Fleming's dish; a team, and a decade of **toil**, **transformed** it into a **remedy**.
