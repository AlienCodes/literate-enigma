# EN: The Mould That Changed the World

In September 1928 Alexander Fleming, a bacteriologist at St Mary's Hospital in London, returned from holiday to find a dish of staphylococci **contaminated** by a **mould**. The **intruder** had **inhibited** the **bacteria** around it. Popular **mythology** blames an open window; the spore more probably came from a laboratory downstairs, where moulds were **cultivated**.

Fleming published his findings in June 1929. Yet the **crude** juice was hard to **purify** and **unstable**, and he lacked the chemists to **tame** it. He used it **chiefly** to **isolate** bacteria. For about a decade penicillin lay **dormant**, a **curiosity**, not a **cure**.

Oxford **revived** it. Howard Florey, an Australian pathologist, **collaborated** with Ernst Chain, a German-born **chemist** and Jewish **refugee**. Their colleague Norman Heatley **improvised** **ingenious** **apparatus** to **extract** the **compound**. On 25 May 1940 eight mice received a **lethal** **dose** of bacteria; only the four **injected** with penicillin lived. The Lancet, a **prestigious** medical journal, printed the **outcome** that August.

In February 1941 Albert Alexander, an Oxford **constable** with a **grave** **infection**, **rallied** on the drug. Then **meagre** reserves ran out, and in March he died. **Quantity** was now the **obstacle**. From July 1941 Florey and Heatley **enlisted** American help. At a government laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, corn-steep liquor **multiplied** yields about tenfold, and a mouldy market melon **furnished** a more **prolific** strain. **Pharmaceutical** **manufacturers** **brewed** it in **colossal** **vats**; by D-Day in June 1944 Allied forces had **ample** supplies.

In October 1945 Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel prize. **Fortune** **sowed** the mould; a **tenacious** team, and years of **toil**, **transformed** it into medicine.
