# EN: The Mould That Changed the World

In September 1928 Alexander Fleming, a bacteriologist at St Mary's Hospital in London, returned from holiday to a **contaminated** dish. A **stray** **mould** had **invaded** his staphylococci, and no **bacteria** grew near it. The **culprit** probably came from a laboratory downstairs; the open-window **anecdote** is a **persistent** **misconception**.

Fleming's findings, published in June 1929, attracted **scant** notice. The **crude** juice was hard to **purify** and **unstable**, so he used it **chiefly** to **isolate** bacteria. Without chemical **expertise**, he turned to other **pursuits**, and penicillin lay **dormant** for a decade.

In Oxford, Howard Florey, an Australian pathologist, and Ernst Chain, a German-born **refugee** chemist, **revived** Fleming's **neglected** work; their colleague Norman Heatley **improvised** an **ingenious** **apparatus** to **extract** the drug. In May 1940 the team **infected** eight mice with a **lethal** **dose** of bacteria. The **verdict** was **conclusive**: the four given penicillin survived; the four left **untreated** died. The medical journal The Lancet published the results that August.

In February 1941 Albert Alexander, an Oxford police **constable** **gravely** ill with an **infection**, **briefly** **rallied** on penicillin, then **relapsed** and died in March when the **precious** supply ran out. The **obstacle** was **sheer** **quantity**. From July 1941 Florey and Heatley **enlisted** American help. A government laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, **boosted** yields about tenfold, and a mouldy market **melon** **furnished** a more **prolific** strain. By D-Day, in June 1944, **industrial** **fermentation** met Allied needs.

In October 1945 Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel prize for medicine; Heatley was **omitted**. The mould had been a **stroke** of luck; the medicine was **forged** by **collective** **toil**.
