# EN: The Legend of the Wolves That Moved Rivers

Yellowstone National Park's **iconic** grey wolves had been **exterminated** by 1926. On 12 January 1995 eight **captured** in Alberta, Canada, were **hauled** there in **crates**, six more a week later. Kept in **enclosures** until late March, they then **roamed** the **wilderness**.

A **viral** 2014 video narrated by George Monbiot, a British writer, **spun** a **compelling** **narrative**: **wary** of **predators**, elk **retreated** from valleys, letting **willows** **flourish** and riverbanks **stabilise**. Ecologists call this a trophic **cascade**: predators eat or **deter** plant-eaters, and **vegetation** **rebounds**.

Yet the **census** **contradicted** the story: northern-range elk peaked at about 19,000 in January 1994 and were **dwindling** before wolves arrived, **depleted** by **hunters**, **drought**, bears and cougars. A 2013 study by Arthur Middleton, a University of Wyoming ecologist, found that **collared** elk **rarely** **encountered** wolves and kept **grazing** unless one **strayed** within half a mile.

**Scrutiny** of the trees followed. A 2022 paper by Elaine Brice of Utah State University showed that **sampling** only the tallest **aspens** **overstated** **regrowth** four- to sevenfold. But the deeper **flaw** was water. Since 2001 Tom Hobbs and David Cooper of Colorado State University had fenced out elk and built **artificial** beaver **dams**. In January 2024 they reported that streams **eroded** after beavers left had cut too deep to **nourish** **stunted** willows.

Oregon State University's William Ripple and Robert Beschta, **advocates** of the **hypothesis**, **retorted** in 2025 that a weaker cascade **persists**; the **dispute** is **unresolved**. The rivers proved **messier** than the **fable**. Yet a science willing to **revise** its favourite example in public may deserve more trust than any tale of wolves moving rivers.
