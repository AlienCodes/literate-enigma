# EN: The Wolf and the River

By 1926 the **iconic** grey wolves of Yellowstone National Park had been **exterminated**. On 12 January 1995 eight were **hauled** in from Alberta, Canada, in **crates**; six more followed. **Confined** to **enclosures** until late March, they were freed to **roam** the **wilderness**.

A **viral** 2014 video, How Wolves Change Rivers, voiced by the British writer George Monbiot, **propagated** a **compelling** **narrative**. The **predators**, he says, made elk too **wary** to **graze** and **browse** by streams, so **willows** **sprouted** and riverbanks **steadied**. Scientists call this a trophic **cascade**: when **prey** grow **scarce** or **timid**, **vegetation** **rebounds**.

The **census** showed the first crack: northern-range elk peaked at about 19,000 in January 1994 and were **dwindling** before wolves arrived, **buffeted** by hunters, **drought**, bears and cougars. Arthur Middleton, a University of Wyoming ecologist, **collared** a nearby **herd** and reported in 2013 that its elk rarely **encountered** wolves.

**Scrutiny** deepened: in 2022 Elaine Brice of Utah State University showed that **sampling** only the tallest **aspens** **overstated** regrowth up to sevenfold. Tom Hobbs and David Cooper of Colorado State University had **excluded** elk from plots and **dammed** streams since 2001. In January 2024 they **concluded** that, without beavers, **eroded** channels had left willows high and dry. Oregon State University's William Ripple, who **proposed** the **hypothesis**, **retorted** in 2025 that a weaker cascade is real.

Defenders of Wildlife, a **conservation** group, **appeased** **hostile** ranchers with **compensation** for wolf-killed **livestock**. The scientific **dispute** awaits a **verdict**. The rivers proved **messier** than the **fable**, but a science willing to **revise** its favourite story may deserve more faith than a tale that never changes.
