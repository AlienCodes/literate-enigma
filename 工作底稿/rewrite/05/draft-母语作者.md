# EN: The Wolf and the River

Yellowstone National Park's grey wolves were **wiped** out by 1926. Since 1987 Defenders of Wildlife, a **conservation** charity, had **compensated** ranchers for **livestock** lost to wolves. On 12 January 1995 eight wolves **captured** in Alberta, Canada, arrived in **crates**, six more a week later; all were **released** in late March to **roam** the **wilderness**.

A **viral** 2014 video by George Monbiot, a British **environmental** writer, **spun** a **compelling** story: **wary** elk **retreated** from the valleys, letting **willows** **flourish** and riverbanks **stabilise**. Ecologists call this a trophic **cascade**: **predators** eat or **deter** plant-eaters, and **vegetation** **rebounds**.

The elk **census** was less **obliging**. The northern-range **herd** peaked at about 19,000 in January 1994 and was **dwindling** before the wolves came: **hunters**, **drought**, bears and cougars took their **toll**. A 2013 study by Arthur Middleton, a University of Wyoming ecologist, **concluded** that **collared** elk kept **grazing** unless a wolf **strayed** within half a mile.

In 2022 Elaine Brice's team at Utah State University **demonstrated** that measuring only the tallest **aspens** had **overstated** **regrowth** four- to sevenfold; random **samples** **detected** a weaker cascade. Colorado State University ecologists Tom Hobbs and David Cooper had **excluded** elk from plots since 2001 and built **artificial** beaver **dams**. Their January 2024 **verdict**: with beavers long **vanished**, streams had **eroded** their beds, so fences alone could not **revive** **stunted** willows.

Oregon State University ecologists William Ripple and Robert Beschta replied in 2025 that a weaker cascade **persists**. The streams proved **messier** than the fable, yet a science that **revises** its favourite story may be more **credible** than any tale of wolves **reshaping** rivers.
