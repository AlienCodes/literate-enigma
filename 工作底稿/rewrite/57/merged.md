# EN: The Burrs That Became Velcro

In 1941 George de Mestral, a Swiss engineer, returned from hunting in the Alps with his **hound**, an Irish Pointer. **Prickly** burdock **seed-heads** had **hitched** themselves to his clothes and lay **tangled** in the dog's **fur**. Under a microscope he saw thousands of tiny **barbs**, each a **hook** **grasping** the small **loops** in cloth.

Few people took the idea **seriously** at first. In Lyon, then a **weaving** centre, a cotton version proved too **frail** to survive **abrasion**. Working with a **textiles** firm, he turned to **nylon**, whose hooks were **stiff** enough to hold yet able to pull apart. The **trial-and-error** at the **looms** and **mills** lasted about ten years.

The name **merged** two French words, velours, meaning **velvet**, and crochet, meaning hook. A US patent **duly** followed in 1955. In 1959 a New York fashion show sent **apparel** **stitched** with the **fasteners** down the **runway**. The public was less **charmed** than expected, **mostly** because of the **aesthetic**.

The **underdog** proved its **utility** in **outer** space. NASA used it in the Apollo programme to keep **gear** **anchored** in weightlessness, so that nothing would **hover** in **mid-air**. Apollo 11 was **equipped** with a **sizeable** amount, about 3,300 square inches. NASA had not invented the nylon **clasp**.

When the patent ran out in 1978, **cut-price** **knock-offs** from Taiwan, China and South Korea **overran** the market, and a **niche** product went **mainstream**. In 2017 the company asked people to call such **mimics** hook and loop fasteners, to stop its **trademark** **melting** into a **generic** **noun**. The firm ended up asking the public to stop using its own name for rivals' products.
