# EN: The Burrs That Became Velcro

In 1941 George de Mestral, a Swiss engineer, returned from hunting in the Alps with his Irish Pointer. Burdock burrs had **hitched** a ride on his trousers and **tangled** in the dog's **fur**. The **enquiring** **sportsman** put the **prickly** **seed-heads** under a microscope and found **countless** tiny **hooks** **seizing** **loops** in cloth and hair.

Few took the idea seriously at first. He carried it to Lyon, then a **weaving** centre, and consulted its **textiles** **mills**. Cotton proved too **frail** to survive **abrasion**, so he turned to **nylon**, woven on **looms** into hooks **stiff** enough to hold yet able to pull apart. The **trial-and-error** took about ten years.

The name **merged** two French words, velours, or **velvet**, and crochet, or hook. An American patent secured his **ownership** in 1955. In 1959 a New York fashion show sent **attire** with the **fasteners** down the **runway**. Public reaction to the **apparel** was more **subdued** than expected, largely because many found its **aesthetics** **unsightly**.

Space flight gave the **clasp** its most famous work. NASA used it in the Apollo programme to **anchor** **gear** in **weightless** **compartments**, where anything loose goes **wandering** or **hovering**. Apollo 11 carried about 3,300 square inches, an **ample** supply for **zero-gravity** living. Many people **wrongly** believe NASA invented it.

Once the patent lapsed in 1978, a **torrent** of **cut-price** **knock-offs** arrived from Taiwan, China and South Korea, and a **niche** item went **mainstream**. In 2017 the company asked buyers to call copies hook and loop fasteners, lest its **trademark** **dilute** into a **generic** term. It was **politely** requesting the return of a name **hijacked** as an everyday **noun**.
