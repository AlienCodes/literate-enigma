# EN: The Burrs That Became Velcro

In 1941 the Swiss engineer George de Mestral returned from a hunting trip in the Alps with his Irish **pointer**. Burdock burrs had **tangled** in the **hound**'s **fur**. He put the **prickly** **seeds** under a microscope. He saw thousands of tiny **barbs**, each a **hook** able to **grasp** the **loops** in cloth and fur.

Most people were **doubtful** at first. In Lyon, then a **weaving** centre, a cotton version suffered **abrasion** and wore out quickly. With a **textiles** firm, he turned to **nylon**, whose hooks could bend enough to **disengage** yet stay **stiff** enough to hold. **Trial-and-error** at the **looms** and **mills** lasted about ten years.

The name **merged** two French words, velours, meaning **velvet**, and crochet, meaning hook. A US patent followed in 1955. In 1959 a New York fashion show sent **apparel** **stitched** with the **fasteners** down the **runway**. The public greeted it **coolly**, largely finding its **aesthetic** **unflattering** and hardly **fashionable**.

Space travel revealed its **utility**. In the Apollo programme, NASA used it to keep objects **anchored** in **weightless** conditions, where loose items would go **hovering** in **mid-air**. Apollo 11 carried a **sizeable** quantity, about 3,300 square inches. Despite a popular **belief**, NASA did not invent this nylon **clasp**, which **conveniently** already existed.

When the patent ended in 1978, **inexpensive** **knock-offs** from Taiwan, China and South Korea **saturated** a once **niche** market. In September 2017 the company asked people to call them "hook and loop" fasteners, lest its **trademark** turn into a **generic** **noun**. Its **proprietary** name had entered the **vernacular** far enough for the owner to ask buyers to withhold it from rivals.
