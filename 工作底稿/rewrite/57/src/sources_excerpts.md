# 第57篇 来源摘录（两轮 WebSearch，2026-10-02）
S1 Wikipedia "George de Mestral"; History.com; invent.org (National Inventors Hall of Fame); LiveScience: in 1941 Swiss engineer George de Mestral returned from a hunting trip in the Alps with his dog (an Irish Pointer) and found burdock burrs stuck to his clothes and the dog's fur. Curious, he examined them under a microscope and saw thousands of tiny hooks that caught on the small loops in fabric and fur.
S2 History.com; JSTOR Daily; invent.org: few people took the idea seriously at first. He took it to Lyon, then a weaving centre; a cotton prototype was made but cotton wore out too quickly. Working with a textile manufacturer/weavers, he turned to nylon, which could form hooks flexible enough to pull apart yet firm enough to hold. The development took about ten years.
S3 Wikipedia "Velcro"/"George de Mestral"; Velcro company: the name combines the French words velours (velvet) and crochet (hook). A Swiss patent was issued in 1954/1955 and a US patent in 1955 (write "patented in the mid-1950s" or "patented in 1955" for the US).
S4 Velcro company history / cableorganizer: in 1959 a New York fashion show displayed clothing with Velcro fasteners; the public reaction was less enthusiastic than expected, largely because of the product's appearance.
S5 Velcro company / astronautrheaseddon / LiveScience: NASA used Velcro fasteners in the Apollo programme to keep objects in place in weightlessness; Apollo 11 used about 3,300 square inches of them (avoid claiming NASA invented it — it did not).
S6 Smithsonian (2017); Wikipedia: de Mestral's patent expired in 1978, after which cheap imitations flooded the market from Taiwan, China and South Korea; before then it was a niche product.
S7 Velcro Companies (Sept 2017); designboom: the company launched a campaign ("Don't Say Velcro") asking the public to call imitations "hook and loop" fasteners, to protect its trademark from becoming a generic word.
