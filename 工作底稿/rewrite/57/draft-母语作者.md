# EN: The Burrs That Became Velcro

In 1941 George de Mestral, a Swiss engineer, returned from hunting in the Alps with his **hound**, an Irish Pointer. **Prickly** burdock **seed-heads** had **fastened** themselves to his trousers and lay **tangled** in the dog's **matted** **fur**. Under a microscope he saw thousands of tiny **hooks** **grasping** the small **loops** in cloth and fur.

Few people took the idea **seriously** at first. In Lyon, then a centre of **weaving** and **textiles**, a cotton version proved too **frail** and soon wore out. Working with **weavers** at their **looms**, he turned to **nylon**, whose hooks could pull apart, hold **securely** and **withstand** wear. The **trial-and-error** lasted about ten years.

The name **welded** two French words, velours, meaning **velvet**, and crochet, meaning hook. A US patent **duly** followed in 1955. In 1959 a New York fashion show sent **apparel** with the new **fasteners** down the **runway**. The public was less **charmed** than expected, **mostly** by the **unflattering** **aesthetic**.

The **underdog** found its moment in **outer** space. NASA used it in the Apollo programme to keep **gear** **anchored** in **weightless** **cockpits**, where anything loose would **hover** away. Apollo 11 was **equipped** with about 3,300 square inches. Popular **belief** **erroneously** credits the agency with inventing it.

When the patent ran out in 1978, cheap **knock-offs** from **vendors** in Taiwan, China and South Korea **overran** the market, and a **niche** product went **mainstream**. In 2017 the company asked people to call such **mimics** hook and loop fasteners, to stop its **trademark** **melting** into **generic** **vernacular**. The firm ended up asking the world to stop using its own name.
