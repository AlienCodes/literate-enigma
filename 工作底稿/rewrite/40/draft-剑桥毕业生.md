# EN: Move 37

Go reportedly allows more **configurations** than the universe has **atoms**, a **daunting** **computational** challenge. In October 2015 DeepMind's AlphaGo beat the European champion 5–0 without a **handicap**, a first against a professional. That **milestone** brought a high-**stakes**, **televised** **showdown** in Seoul in March 2016 against Lee Sedol, a South Korean **opponent** with 18 international titles.

In game two, on 10 March 2016, AlphaGo's 37th move, an **unconventional** shoulder hit on the fifth line of the **grid**, **defied** **orthodox** principles. Commentators found the **unexpected** **deviation** **bizarre**; one thought it a mistake. Lee left the room, taking more than ten minutes of **deliberation** over a **scenario** he had not **foreseen**.

By AlphaGo's own estimate, the **probability** of a human playing that stone was one in 10,000, long **odds**. The program had first learned to **emulate** strong but **fallible** amateurs online, then **sharpened** its **intuition** over tens of millions of games against itself, **iterations** of **reinforcement** no human could **rehearse**. Lee later **acknowledged** the move as creative and beautiful.

Game four, on 13 March 2016, brought **symmetry**. Lee's 78th move, a **pivotal** **wedge** many professionals called divine, ended AlphaGo's **unbeaten** run. DeepMind's team put a professional's **likelihood** of finding it at about one in 10,000. The **comeback**, showing **resilience** after three **unanswered** losses, was Lee's only win, a **consolation** in AlphaGo's **triumphant** 4–1.

In November 2019 Lee **withdrew** from the game, explaining that AI, an **invincible** **entity**, had put the **pinnacle** beyond him. His **farewell** was a **candid** **reflection** on machine **dominance**, not a **lament**. Yet the one game he won **hinged** on a stone judged as unlikely as AlphaGo's own.
