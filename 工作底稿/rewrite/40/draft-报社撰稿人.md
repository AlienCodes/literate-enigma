# EN: Move 37

Go is said to allow more **configurations** than the universe has **atoms**. In October 2015 DeepMind's **algorithm** AlphaGo reached a **computational** **milestone**, beating the European champion 5–0 on a full **grid** without a **handicap**. In March 2016 it faced an **opponent** in a **televised** Seoul **showdown**, Lee Sedol, a South Korean **strategist** with 18 international titles.

In the second game, on 10 March 2016, AlphaGo's 37th move, an **eccentric** shoulder hit on the fifth line, seemed to **defy** **orthodox** play. Commentators found the **unconventional** **placement** **bizarre**, and one suspected a mistake. Few **viewers** could **anticipate** it. Lee left the room and **deliberated** over the **unexpected** **scenario** for more than ten minutes.

The **odds** were remarkable. AlphaGo itself put the **probability** of a human playing it at one in 10,000. Having learned to **mimic** strong **amateurs**, it played tens of millions of **simulated** games against itself, **sharpening** **neural** networks through **reinforcement**. Those **iterations** led beyond human **intuition**. Lee later called the move creative and beautiful.

Three games down, Lee's **comeback** came in the fourth, on 13 March 2016. His 78th move proved **pivotal**; many professionals called it divine. DeepMind's team put its **likelihood** for a human professional at about one in 10,000, a **rarity** in **reversed** **symmetry**. That **consolation** win showed **dignity** and **resilience**, but AlphaGo, no longer **unbeaten**, took the match 4–1.

In November 2019 Lee chose to **withdraw**. In a **candid** **reflection** on his **farewell**, he **acknowledged** AI as an **entity** that could not be defeated, a **dominant** presence at the game's **summit**. That single win hinged on a stone the machine, too, had failed to **foresee**.
