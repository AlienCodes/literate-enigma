# EN: Move 37

Go is said to allow more **configurations** than there are **atoms** in the universe, a **daunting** **computational** challenge. In October 2015 DeepMind's AlphaGo reached a **milestone**, becoming the first program to beat a professional without a **handicap**. In March 2016 its **opponent** in a **televised** Seoul **showdown** was Lee Sedol, a South Korean **strategist** with 18 international titles.

In game two, on 10 March, AlphaGo's 37th move was a shoulder hit, a **tactic** setting a stone **diagonal** and **adjacent** to an opponent's. On the **grid**'s fifth line, it was far from **orthodox**. Commentators found the **unexpected** **placement** **bizarre**, and one took it for a mistake. Lee left the room, his **pause** for **deliberation** lasting over ten minutes.

DeepMind's **diagnostic** tools showed AlphaGo had put the **odds** of a human playing that stone at one in 10,000, a **staggering** **rarity**. AlphaGo had first learned to **imitate** strong **amateurs** online, then **sharpened** its play over tens of millions of **simulated** games against itself. Lee later **acknowledged** the stone's **originality** and beauty.

The fourth game, on 13 March, **reversed** the roles in **neat** **symmetry**. Lee's 78th move turned the **tide**, and many professionals **heralded** it as **divine**. DeepMind's team **computed** a professional's **likelihood** of finding it: about one in 10,000. It was Lee's only win, a **consolation** amid AlphaGo's **overwhelming** 4–1 victory.

In November 2019 Lee **withdrew** from the professional **circuit**, a **farewell** he tied to AI's **unrivalled** **dominance**. Calling AI an **invincible** **entity**, he said the **summit** was beyond him. Yet his one win came from a stone whose odds, by DeepMind's figures, were as **slim** as those of AlphaGo's **audacious** shoulder hit.
