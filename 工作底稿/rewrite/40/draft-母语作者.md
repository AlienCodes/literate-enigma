# EN: Move 37

Go reportedly allows more **configurations** than there are **atoms** in the universe, so a **superior** **algorithm** seemed remote. In October 2015 DeepMind's AlphaGo beat the European champion 5–0 with no **handicap**, a **milestone** without **precedent**. In March 2016 its **opponent** in a **televised** Seoul **showdown** was Lee Sedol, an **elite** **strategist** with 18 international titles.

The second game, on 10 March 2016, **hinged** on AlphaGo's 37th move, an **eccentric** shoulder hit on the fifth line. Such an **unexpected** **deviation** from **orthodox** **doctrine** seemed to **defy** **intuition**. Commentators found it **bizarre**; one took it for a mistake. Lee left the room and took more than ten minutes to **contemplate** it.

A **diagnostic** check showed AlphaGo had put the **odds** of a human playing that stone at one in 10,000, a **staggering** **rarity**. It learned to **imitate** strong **amateurs** on **virtual** boards, then **sharpened** its play over tens of millions of **iterations** against itself. In **retrospect**, Lee **acknowledged** the stone as **inventive** and beautiful.

The fourth game, on 13 March 2016, **reversed** the roles in neat **symmetry**. Lee's 78th move turned the **tide**, and many professionals **heralded** it as **divine**. DeepMind **calculated** its **likelihood** for a professional at about one in 10,000, an **echo** of game two. It was Lee's only win, a **consolation** amid AlphaGo's **overwhelming** 4–1 victory.

In November 2019 Lee **withdrew** from the professional **circuit**, a **farewell** he tied to AI's **unrivalled** **dominance**. Calling AI an **invincible** **entity**, he said the **summit** was beyond him. Yet his one win came from a stone that, by DeepMind's figures, was as **slim** a chance as AlphaGo's **audacious** shoulder hit.
