# EN: The Letter That Startled Darwin

Charles Darwin **disembarked** from HMS Beagle in October 1836. In September 1838 he began **formulating** the **principle** of natural **selection**: individuals better **adapted** to their **surroundings** survive and pass on their **hereditary** traits. By 1844 he had **composed** a 230-page **manuscript**, yet kept it **unpublished**, **cautiously** **amassing** evidence for some twenty years.

In February 1858 Alfred Russel Wallace, a **naturalist** collecting in the Malay Archipelago and **afflicted** by fever, **independently** **conceived** the same idea and **summarised** it in an essay. It reached Darwin on 18 June 1858. Its argument **paralleled** his own almost **precisely**. He told his friend Charles Lyell he had never seen a more striking **coincidence**. His **priority** seemed **threatened**.

Lyell and the botanist Joseph Hooker **arranged** for both papers to be read **simultaneously** at the Linnean Society, a scientific society in London, on 1 July 1858. Neither **contributor** attended the **proceedings**. Wallace was abroad; Darwin was **grieving** an **infant** son lost to **scarlet** fever on 28 June. The **compromise** may have kept **rivalry** over **precedence** at bay.

The papers' **publication** on 20 August 1858 met a **muted** **reception**. In May 1859 the Society's president, in his **yearly** address, **remarked** the year had been **barren** of **revolutionary** discoveries. **Ultimately**, on 24 November 1859 came Darwin's **treatise**, On the Origin of Species. Booksellers had **subscribed** for the **entirety** of its first **impression**, 1,250 copies.

In October 1858, before the book existed, Wallace **signified** his **acceptance**, **assuring** Hooker in a **cordial** letter of his **satisfaction** with the **arrangement**. In 1889 he published a **lengthy** **exposition** of the **doctrine** and **entitled** it Darwinism. Wallace chose the other man's name.
