# EN: The Letter That Startled Darwin

Charles Darwin returned from HMS Beagle's five-year voyage in October 1836. In September 1838 he began to **formulate** natural **selection**, the **concept** that individuals better **adapted** to their **surroundings** **survive** and pass on their traits. By 1844 he had **drafted** a 230-page **manuscript**, but **deferred** **publication**, **patiently** **amassing** evidence for some twenty years.

In February 1858 Alfred Russel Wallace, collecting specimens in the Malay Archipelago while **afflicted** with fever, **independently** **conceived** the same **principle** and **summarised** it **concisely** in an essay. Darwin received it on 18 June 1858. Its **framework** was **essentially** identical to his, and he **remarked** to Charles Lyell that he had never seen a more **striking** **coincidence**.

Lyell and the botanist Joseph Hooker **arranged** a **diplomatic** **compromise**. Both papers were **formally** read **simultaneously** at the Linnean Society, a scientific society in London, on 1 July 1858. Neither author **attended**. Wallace remained in the **archipelago**, and Darwin was **mourning** his **infant** son, who had died of **scarlet** fever on 28 June.

The papers **duly** appeared in the Society's **proceedings** on 20 August 1858 but **aroused** little **excitement**. In May 1859 its president **declared** in his annual address that the year had **witnessed** no **revolutionary** discoveries. Darwin's **volume** On the Origin of Species was published on 24 November 1859, and booksellers had **subscribed** for all 1,250 copies of the first **impression**.

A **fierce** **rivalry** over **priority** might have **ensued**. Instead, in October 1858, Wallace wrote to Hooker of his **satisfaction** with the **handling** of his paper. In 1889 he **entitled** his own **treatise** on the theory Darwinism. Wallace **willingly** **bestowed** the other man's **surname** on the idea.
