# EN: Tulip Mania: The Bubble and the Legend

In the winter of 1636–37, Dutch **merchants** and **brokers** met in **taverns** to trade **contracts** for **tulip** bulbs still in the ground, a **speculative** business they called windhandel, "trading in the wind". Prices of prized "broken" tulips, with **streaked** **petals**, **soared**. At an **auction** in Alkmaar in early February 1637, one bulb fetched 5,200 guilders, then the Dutch **currency**: more than a decade's **wages** for a skilled craftsman.

The same week, at a sale in Haarlem, the **hub** of the trade, buyers failed to **bid**. Within days the **frenzy** **snapped**: prices **collapsed**, many buyers **defaulted** and **unsettled** contracts **piled** up. Why the market turned so **swiftly** is still **contested**.

Two centuries later, in 1841, Charles Mackay, a Scottish **journalist**, **recounted** the **episode** as national **hysteria** in Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds. In his telling, rich and poor **gambled**, fortunes vanished and Dutch **commerce** **reeled**. His **vivid** account made tulip **mania** **shorthand** for **reckless** **bubbles**.

Yet the **archives** tell a smaller story. Anne Goldgar, a **historian** at King's College London, **scoured** Dutch records and counted only a few hundred traders, mostly merchants and craftsmen. She found no **bankruptcy** caused by tulips, no **drownings** and no **measurable** **harm** to the economy. In May 1638 Haarlem's authorities let buyers **settle** for three and a half per cent of the agreed price. Mackay **relied** largely on **satirical** **pamphlets** of 1637 that **preached** against **greed**.

The **boom** was real but **fleeting**. What spread, and still **circulates** nearly four centuries on, was the **moral**: a **sermon** of 1637, retold by Mackay as history.
