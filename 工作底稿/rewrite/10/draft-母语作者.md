# EN: Tulip Mania: The Bubble and the Legend

In the winter of 1636–37, Dutch **merchants** and craftsmen **flocked** to **taverns** to trade **contracts** for **tulip** bulbs still in the ground, a **speculative** business called windhandel, "trading in the wind". Prices of **coveted** varieties, **notably** "broken" tulips with **streaked** **petals**, **soared**. In early February 1637, at an **auction** in Alkmaar, one bulb fetched 5,200 guilders (then the Dutch **currency**), over a decade's **wages** for a skilled craftsman.

Then the **bubble** **burst**. At a sale in Haarlem, a town near Amsterdam, **bidders** failed to appear. Prices **collapsed**, many **buyers** **defaulted**, and **sellers** faced a **tangle** of **unpaid** contracts. **Plague** was **raging** in Haarlem, but why the market broke is still debated.

In 1841, two centuries later, a Scottish **journalist**, Charles Mackay, told a darker story. His Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds **depicted** a nation **gripped** by **greed**, fortunes lost, **commerce** badly shaken. That **vivid** **chronicle** made tulip mania a **shorthand** for every **reckless** **craze** since.

Yet the **archives** tell a smaller story. Anne Goldgar, a **historian**, identified only a few hundred **traders**, mostly merchants and craftsmen. She found no **bankruptcy** linked to tulips, no one who **drowned** himself, and no **measurable** **harm** to the Dutch **economy**. In May 1638 Haarlem's authorities let buyers **settle** for three and a half per cent of the agreed price. Mackay had **relied** largely on **satirical** **pamphlets** and songs of 1637 that **preached** against greed.

The **speculation** lasted weeks and **involved** hundreds. What spread, and still **circulates** nearly four centuries on, was the **moral**: a **sermon** of 1637, retold by Mackay as history.
