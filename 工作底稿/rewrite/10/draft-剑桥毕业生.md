# EN: Tulip Mania: The Bubble and the Legend

In the winter of 1636–37, Dutch **merchants** met in **taverns** to **bargain** over **contracts** for bulbs still in the ground, a **speculative** trade known as windhandel, "trading in the wind". **Streaked** tulips, the most **coveted**, **soared** in price. At an **auction** in Alkmaar, one bulb fetched 5,200 Dutch guilders, over a decade's **wages** for a craftsman.

But the **craze** was **fleeting**. In early February 1637, buyers failed to appear at a sale in Haarlem, the **hub** of the trade. For reasons still **contested**, **panic** spread and prices **collapsed**; many buyers **defaulted**, leaving **creditors** with **worthless** contracts.

Two centuries later, in 1841, Charles Mackay, a Scottish **journalist**, cast the **episode** as a national **frenzy** in Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds. In his telling, **greed** **gripped** every class, **investors** were **ruined** and Dutch **commerce** was badly shaken. His **vivid** account made tulips **shorthand** for **irrational** markets.

Yet the **archives** tell a smaller story. Anne Goldgar, a **historian**, **unearthed** only a few hundred traders, **predominantly** merchants and craftsmen. She found no **bankruptcy** linked to tulips, no **suicide** in the canals and no **measurable** harm to the economy. In May 1638 Haarlem's authorities let buyers **withdraw** from contracts for a **penalty** of 3.5 per cent of the **negotiated** price. Many of Mackay's **sources** were **satirical** **pamphlets** of 1637 that **preached** against greed.

None of this makes the **bubble** **imaginary**; prices did **surge** and **slump**. But the mania was small and **transient**, while the **moral** **distilled** from it has **circulated** for nearly four centuries. What proved **contagious** was the story, not the trade.
