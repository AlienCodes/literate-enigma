# EN: Tulip Mania: The Bubble and the Legend

In the winter of 1636–37, Dutch **merchants** and **brokers** met in **taverns** to trade **contracts** for **tulip** bulbs still in the ground, a **wager** they called windhandel, "trading in the wind". Prices of the most **coveted** **blooms**, **streaked** "broken" tulips, **soared**. At an **auction** in Alkmaar, one bulb sold for 5,200 guilders, then the Dutch currency: over a decade's **wages** for a skilled worker.

In early February 1637 the **frenzy** **snapped**. At a sale in Haarlem, a town near Amsterdam and the **hub** of the **craze**, buyers failed to **bid**. Prices **collapsed**, many buyers **defaulted** and **unsettled** contracts **piled** up. Why the market turned so **swiftly** remains **contested**.

Two centuries later, in 1841, Charles Mackay, a Scottish **journalist**, **recounted** the **episode** as national **hysteria** in "Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds". Rich and poor, he wrote, **gambled**; families were **ruined**; Dutch **commerce** **reeled**. The **vivid** account **circulated** widely; "tulip **mania**" became **shorthand** for every **speculative** **bubble**.

Yet the **archives** tell a smaller story. Anne Goldgar, a **historian** at King's College London, **combed** Dutch records and found a few hundred traders, mostly merchants and craftsmen. She found no **bankruptcy** caused by tulips, no **drownings** and no **measurable** **harm** to the economy. In May 1638 Haarlem's authorities let buyers escape their **obligations** by paying three and a half per cent of the agreed price. Mackay leaned on **satirical** **pamphlets** of 1637 that **preached** against **greed**.

The **boom** was real but **fleeting**. The **sermon** drawn from it has **persisted** for nearly four centuries; what proved **contagious** was not the trade but the **moral**.
