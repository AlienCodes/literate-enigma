# EN: The Truth About One Marshmallow

Picture a **nursery** school on Stanford's **campus** in the late 1960s and early 1970s. A child of about four or five sits alone with one treat and a **blunt** **bargain**: eat it now, or **resist** **temptation** for about 15 minutes and get two. **Impulsive** children **surrender** **instantly**. **Resolute** ones hold out **grimly**.

Follow-ups made the test **legendary**. A 1990 study in Developmental Psychology found that longer waiters tended, as **adolescents**, to **attain** higher scores on the SAT, a US college-entrance exam. Delay of gratification, the ability to wait for a bigger **reward**, became a **remarkable** **predictor** of success, **arguably** a **virtue** **dictating** **destiny**.

Yet the **foundation** was **precarious**. A **mere** few dozen children entered the SAT analysis, **predominantly** the children of Stanford **faculty** and graduate students. Such a **homogeneous**, **privileged** group was a **questionable** **proxy** for **ordinary**, let alone **impoverished**, families.

Then the **certainty** **crumbled**. A study published in Psychological Science in May 2018 ran a **conceptual** **replication**: the same **underlying** idea, new methods, more than 900 **diverse** four-and-a-half-year-olds, **revisited** at 15. The raw link was roughly half as strong as the original. **Adjusting for** family background and early **cognitive** ability **shrank** it by about two thirds, leaving it **mostly** **statistically** **insignificant**.

The test is not **worthless**; some researchers argue a **marginal** link **persists**. Yet waiting also appears to be a **rational** **judgement** about **context**. A study published in Cognition in January 2013 found that children who had watched an adult break a promise waited **considerably** less. A child who **perseveres** has **frequently** learned that adults are **trustworthy**.
