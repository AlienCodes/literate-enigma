# EN: The Truth About One Marshmallow

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, **psychologists** at a **nursery** school on Stanford's **campus** gave **youngsters** of about four or five a **dilemma**. They could eat one treat at once, or **resist** **temptation**, wait **unattended** for about 15 minutes and **earn** two. The test **assessed** **patience**: **forgoing** an **instant** **reward** for a bigger one.

**Follow-ups** in the late 1980s brought it **fame**. A 1990 study in Developmental Psychology found longer **delays** **correlated** with higher **adolescent** scores on the SAT, a US college-entrance exam. **Self-help** books **seized on** the **remarkable** result, and **hype** **elevated** **restraint** into a **predictor** of **destiny**.

Yet the sample was small, a few dozen children in the SAT analysis, drawn **mainly** from Stanford **faculty** and **postgraduate** families. Results from such a **homogeneous**, **privileged** group could not **readily** be **generalised** to **ordinary** children.

A study published in Psychological Science in May 2018, a **conceptual** **replication**, **probed** the same idea with new **participants** and **modified** **procedures**. Its more than 900 children were more **diverse**, **evaluated** for waiting at four and a half and **achievement** at 15. The raw link was about half as strong as the original. **Adjusting** for family background and early **cognitive** ability shrank it by about two thirds, leaving it mostly **statistically** **insignificant**.

The test is not **worthless**; it may still **reflect** something **substantive**. But a study published in Cognition in January 2013 found that children who first saw an adult break a promise waited **markedly** less than those who saw one kept. Waiting is often a **rational** **wager** on a **trustworthy** world.
