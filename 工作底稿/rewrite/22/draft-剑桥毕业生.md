# EN: The Truth About One Marshmallow

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, at a **nursery** school on Stanford's **campus**, children of about four or five **confronted** a **deliberate** **temptation**. They could **indulge** **promptly** in one marshmallow, or **resist** in **solitude** for about 15 minutes and **earn** two, the **reward** for **patience**.

**Follow-ups** in the late 1980s made the test **legendary**. A 1990 study in *Developmental Psychology* linked longer waiting with higher scores on the SAT, a US college-entrance exam. A **trivial** **snack** became a **glorified** **emblem** of **virtue**: **restraint** at four, **supposedly**, **heralded** **achievement** in **adolescence**.

The **foundation** was **precarious**. The SAT analysis **involved** only a few dozen children, **predominantly** the sons and daughters of Stanford **faculty** and graduate students. Such a **homogeneous**, **privileged** group could **scarcely** **reflect** **ordinary** families, and **prosperous** homes may **foster** patience and grades alike.

A study published in *Psychological Science* in May 2018 ran a **conceptual** **replication**: the same idea, a new sample, **modified** methods. Over 900 **diverse** **youngsters** were **assessed** at four and a half and at 15. The raw link was roughly half as strong as the original. **Adjusted** for family background and early **cognitive** ability, it shrank by about two thirds and was mostly not **statistically** **significant**.

The test may still capture something **authentic**. A study published in *Cognition* in January 2013 adds **context**: children who saw an adult **betray** a promise waited far less than **peers** who saw one kept. Waiting appears to be a **rational** **wager**. A child taught to **distrust** adults **seizes** the sweet sooner than one **accustomed** to kept promises.
