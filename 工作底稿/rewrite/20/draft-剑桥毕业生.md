# EN: The Brain That Learned London

To earn a **licence**, **aspiring** London cabbies must pass "The Knowledge", an **arduous** **examination** on about 25,000 streets within six miles of Charing Cross and thousands of **landmarks**. **Apprentices** typically spend three to four years **committing** this **labyrinth** to memory, and only about half **qualify**.

A study published in PNAS in April 2000 **scanned** 16 male taxi drivers and 50 men outside the **profession**. The drivers' rear, or **posterior**, hippocampus, a seahorse-shaped structure **vital** to memory and **navigation**, was **enlarged**; a front, or **anterior**, **region** was smaller. Longer **tenure** was linked to a larger posterior **segment**.

Yet the link left the cause **uncertain**. Perhaps an **innate** **flair** for **spatial** tasks **attracted** people to the **occupation**; perhaps **prolonged** driving and stress were **responsible**. A study published in December 2006 **eliminated** the second: taxi drivers **possessed** more posterior grey matter than bus drivers of similar **mileage** and stress, **confined** to **predetermined** routes.

A **longitudinal** study published in December 2011 scanned 79 **trainees** and 31 controls on **enrolment** and three to four years later. The 39 who qualified gained grey matter, the tissue **dense** with **nerve** cells, in the posterior hippocampus; the **unsuccessful** and controls did not. The **sequence** strongly suggests that learning **preceded** **expansion**.

Growth appeared to **entail** a **trade-off**: qualified drivers **underperformed** controls at **reproducing** an **intricate** **diagram** after a **delay**, one test of visual memory. The studies were small and **exclusively** male, yet suggest that the healthy adult brain can **reshape** itself under **strenuous** learning. Every street a driver **retained** seemed to **claim** a little room from **elsewhere**.
