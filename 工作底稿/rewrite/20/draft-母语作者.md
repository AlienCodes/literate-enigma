# EN: The Brain That Learned London

For a **licence**, **aspiring** London cabbies must learn a **labyrinth** of about 25,000 streets within six miles of Charing Cross, plus **countless** **landmarks**. This **formidable** exam, "the Knowledge", **typically** **entails** three to four years of **relentless** **revision**, and only about half the **applicants** pass.

A study published in PNAS in April 2000 **scanned** 16 male cab drivers and 50 men outside the trade. It **probed** the hippocampus, a seahorse-shaped structure **vital** for memory and **navigation**. In drivers, its **posterior**, or **rear**, **portion** was **enlarged**, and an **anterior**, or front, region smaller.

Larger rear portions **accompanied** longer **careers**, but that could simply mean the job **attracts** people **endowed** with them, or perhaps **sheer** driving does the work. A study published in December 2006 in Hippocampus **dispelled** the second **notion**. In that rear region, taxi drivers had more grey matter, tissue **dense** with nerve cells, than bus drivers of **equivalent** **tenure** **confined** to **predictable** routes.

A **longitudinal** study published in December 2011 in Current Biology **tackled** the first **suspicion**, scanning 79 **trainees** at the **outset** and three to four years later. The 39 who **qualified** **amassed** grey matter in the rear hippocampus, whereas **unsuccessful** trainees and 31 **outsiders** showed no such **expansion**. The **sequence** strongly suggests learning **reshaped** the brain.

The gain carried a **penalty**: qualified drivers did worse than outsiders on one visual memory test. The studies were small and all-male, yet they **imply** a healthy adult brain can **remodel** its **architecture** through **intensive** learning. Each **alley** a cabbie **conquered** seemed to **occupy** room once **allocated** to other **imagery**.
