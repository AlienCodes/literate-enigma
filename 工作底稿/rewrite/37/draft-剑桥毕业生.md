# EN: The Curse of the Pharaoh

On 4 November 1922, in the Valley of the Kings, **labourers** **hired** by the archaeologist Howard Carter **uncovered** a **staircase** **carved** into the rock. On 26 November, with his **sponsor** Lord Carnarvon present, Carter made a small hole in the **doorway** and glimpsed the **glittering** **riches** of the boy king Tutankhamun. The **spectacular** find **stunned** the world.

It also **bred** a darker story. Carnarvon sold **exclusive** coverage to The Times, and rival reporters, **starved** of news, turned to **rumours** and outside **commentators**. In March 1923 the best-selling novelist Marie Corelli warned publicly that **dire** **retribution** awaited anyone who **intruded** into a sealed **tomb**. Such **morbid** **speculation** soon found its **fodder**.

That month Carnarvon was bitten by a **mosquito**. He **nicked** the bite with a **razor** while **shaving**, and it turned **septic**. Blood poisoning followed, then **pneumonia**, and he died in Cairo on 5 April 1923. It was claimed that the city's lights failed at that **instant** and that his dog in England **howled** and died. Yet **blackouts** were **commonplace** in Cairo then.

No curse **inscription** was ever found in the tomb. In December 2002 a BMJ study **investigated** 44 Westerners then in Egypt. The 25 classed as exposed to the tomb died at an average age of 70, **versus** 75 for the rest, a **disparity** that was not statistically **significant**. The **mortality** figures lent no **tangible** **credibility** to **supernatural** **vengeance**.

Carter, the man who had opened the tomb, died in March 1939, aged 64. The **superstition**, **spurred** by a press **monopoly**, a novelist's **dread** and an **untimely** **demise**, had **seized** the public **imagination**. His **longevity** drew far less **publicity**.
