# EN: Dynamite for the Heart

Around 1847 the Italian chemist Sobrero made nitroglycerin, a **colourless**, **oily** **liquid** of **unpredictable** **temper**. A **mere** **droplet** **dabbed** on the tongue caused hours of violent headache, and he judged it too **risky** to **harness**. On 3 September 1864 a Stockholm shed making it exploded, killing five, including Alfred Nobel's brother Emil.

Nobel **tamed** nitroglycerin by **blending** it with kieselguhr, a **porous** earth, into a **paste**. Shaped into **rods** and patented in 1867 as dynamite, it served mining, **quarrying** and demolition, **enriching** the **industrialist** **enormously**. His 1895 **testament** left more than 31 million kronor as an **endowment** for prizes, first awarded in 1901.

Explosives factory **operatives** suffered **acute** headaches. Their **tolerance** built over the week but **ebbed** during the weekend **respite**, so Mondays brought a **recurrence**, with **dizziness** and **palpitations**, **hence** the name Monday disease. In 1879 William Murrell reported in The Lancet on nitroglycerin as a **medication** for angina pectoris, or chest pain.

Nobel, who had angina, wrote to his **aide** on 25 October 1896 that by an irony of fate doctors had prescribed nitroglycerin for **internal** use. Its **alias**, Trinitrin, was meant not to **frighten** **pharmacists** and the public. He did not **comply**. He died at his San Remo **villa** on 10 December 1896, **partially** **paralysed** by a **cerebral** haemorrhage.

In 1977 Murad **ascertained** that nitroglycerin **generates** nitric oxide, which **widens** blood vessels. In 1980 Furchgott traced an **unidentified** relaxing **factor** to the **vascular** lining, and in 1986 Ignarro **elucidated** its nature: nitric oxide. The **trio** won the 1998 Nobel Prize. Its **presenter**, on the anniversary of Nobel's death, **quoted** his **ironic** letter.
