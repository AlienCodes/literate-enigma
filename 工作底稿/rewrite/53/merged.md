# EN: Dynamite for the Heart

Around 1847 the Italian chemist Sobrero first made nitroglycerin, a **colourless**, **oily** **liquid** of **acute** **instability**. A **mere** **droplet** on the tongue left the head **throbbing** **violently** for hours. On 3 September 1864 a Stockholm shed making it exploded. The **tragic** blast killed five, including Alfred Nobel's younger brother Emil.

Nobel **tamed** the liquid by **blending** it with kieselguhr, a **porous** earth, into a **paste** shaped into **rods**. Patented in 1867 as dynamite, it was **harnessed** for mining, **quarrying** and demolition, **enriching** him **immensely**. His 1895 **testament** left the fortune as an **endowment** for prizes, first awarded in 1901.

Nitroglycerin had **side-effects**. Factory **operatives** suffered **racking** headaches, and their **tolerance**, built over the week, **ebbed** at weekends, so Mondays brought a **recurrence**, with **dizziness** and **palpitations**, **hence** the name Monday disease. In 1879 William Murrell reported in The Lancet on its **therapeutic** use against angina pectoris, attacks of chest pain.

On 25 October 1896 Nobel, who had angina, wrote to his **aide** that by an irony of fate doctors had prescribed nitroglycerin to be taken **internally**. Its **alias**, Trinitrin, was meant not to **frighten** **pharmacists** or the public. He did not **comply**. **Partly** **paralysed** by a **cerebral** haemorrhage, he died at his San Remo **villa** on 10 December 1896.

In 1977 Murad **ascertained** that nitroglycerin releases nitric oxide, which **widens** vessels. Furchgott found an **unidentified** relaxing **factor** in **vascular** **linings** in 1980, and Ignarro showed in 1986 that it was nitric oxide. The **trio** shared the 1998 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Its **presentation** speech, on 10 December, the anniversary of Nobel's death, **quoted** his **ironic** letter.
