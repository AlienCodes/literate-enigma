# EN: Dynamite for the Heart

A **mere** **droplet** on the tongue left the head **throbbing** **violently** for hours, Sobrero reported. The Italian chemist first **concocted** the **colourless**, **oily** **liquid**, nitroglycerin, around 1847 and judged it too **risky**. On 3 September 1864 it blew up a Stockholm shed, killing five, including Alfred Nobel's younger **sibling**, Emil.

Nobel **tamed** the liquid. **Blended** with kieselguhr, a **porous** earth, it became a **paste**, shaped into **rods** and patented in 1867 as dynamite. **Harnessed** for mining, **quarrying**, construction and demolition, it **enriched** him **immensely**. His 1895 **testament** left more than 31 million kronor as an **endowment** for prizes, awarded from 1901.

The explosive had **side-effects**. Factory **operatives** suffered **acute** headaches. **Tolerance** built over the week but **receded** at weekends, so on Mondays headaches **resurfaced**, with **dizziness** and **palpitations**: Monday disease. In 1879 William Murrell reported in The Lancet on nitroglycerin's **therapeutic** use against angina, **intermittent** attacks of chest pain.

Nobel himself developed angina. Doctors prescribed nitroglycerin to be taken **internally**, **disguised** under the **alias** Trinitrin lest it **frighten** **pharmacists** or the public, he wrote to his **aide** on 25 October 1896. He called it an irony of fate. He did not **comply**. **Partly** **paralysed** by a stroke, he died at his San Remo **villa** on 10 December 1896.

In 1977 Murad **ascertained** that nitroglycerin releases nitric oxide, **widening** blood vessels. In 1980 Furchgott found an unknown relaxing **factor** in **vascular** **linings**; in 1986 Ignarro identified it as nitric oxide, a **cellular** **messenger**. The **trio** shared the 1998 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Its presentation speech, on 10 December, the anniversary of Nobel's death, **quoted** his **ironic** letter.
