# EN: The Stamp That Changed the Post

Before 1840, British **postage** was **levied** according to the number of **sheets** in a letter and the **mileage** of its **carriage**. The sum was usually **payable** on **delivery**, so the person receiving the **correspondence**, not the **sender**, settled it. Each letter thus carried a **fluctuating** price.

In 1837 Rowland Hill published a **booklet** urging reform. Parliament **enacted** the **campaigner**'s **proposal** in 1839, and on 10 January 1840 the Uniform Penny Post began. A letter of up to half an **ounce** now cost one penny to any **destination** in Britain, and the fee was **prepaid** by whoever sent it. **Simplicity** replaced **variation**, under one **flat-rate** charge.

The Penny Black, a one-penny **denomination**, was released on 1 May 1840, with **validity** from 6 May. **Engraved** on it was a **youthful** **likeness** of Queen Victoria, the reigning **monarch**. In **aggregate**, 68,808,000 were printed. The sheets had no holes for **separation**, so each stamp was cut out **individually** with **scissors**.

**Mailings** rose from roughly 80 million **items** in 1839 to over 169 million in 1840, about double. The design had a **drawback**. Against black, the red cancelling mark lacked **noticeable** **contrast**, and the ink could be **erased**, leaving stamps **vulnerable** to **reuse**. In February 1841 the Penny Red, cancelled in black ink, arrived to deter **tampering** and **fraud**.

As the **initiator** of postage stamps, Britain set a **precedent** that brought an **exemption**. Its stamps are **nameless**, with no country **inscribed** on them. By international agreement, the **sovereign**'s head **suffices** for **identification**, an **implicit** **designation** that other countries spell out in print.
