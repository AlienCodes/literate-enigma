# EN: The Stamp That Changed the Post

Before 1840, British **postage** followed a **cumbersome** and **complicated** method. The charge was **levied** according to the number of **sheets** in a letter and the **mileage** it travelled, so the sum **fluctuated** with each **item**. **Payment** usually fell on the receiver at **delivery**. Every piece of **correspondence** carried that **obligation**.

In 1837 Rowland Hill, a **reformer**, published a **booklet** with a **proposal**. Parliament **enacted** the scheme in 1839, and the Uniform Penny Post was **implemented** on 10 January 1840. One penny **sufficed** for a letter of up to half an **ounce**, to any **destination** in Britain. The **sender** paid **upfront**, so **prepaid** mail became the **norm**.

The Penny Black appeared on 1 May 1840 for use from 6 May. Its single-penny **denomination** came with an **engraved** **likeness** of the **sovereign**, a **youthful** **depiction** of Queen Victoria in **silhouette**. Total **output** reached 68,808,000. The sheets had no perforations, so each one was cut out **individually** with **scissors**.

Mail rose from roughly 80 million items in 1839 to more than 169 million in 1840, a **twofold** jump. The issue had a **drawback**. Its red **imprint** showed poorly in **contrast** to black, and the **dye** allowed easy **removal**. Such **tampering** **enabled** **reuse**. In February 1841 the **correction** arrived in the Penny Red, **voided** in black ink.

Britain was the **forerunner** in issuing stamps, and that **precedent** earned an **exemption**. Under international agreement, British issues carry no country name, an **anonymity** **uniquely** held by the **initiator**. The **monarch's** head is **adequate** **identification**. Letters leaving Britain show a royal head in place of a **nationality**, and the country goes **unnamed**.
