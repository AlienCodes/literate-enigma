# EN: The Stamp That Changed the Post

Before 1840, British **postage** turned on two things, the number of **sheets** in a letter and the **mileage** it covered to reach its **destination**. Letters travelled **unpaid**. The fee was usually **payable** on **delivery**, at the **doorstep**, so **payment** fell on whoever received the **correspondence**, not whoever wrote it.

In 1837 the **reformer** Rowland Hill published a **booklet** setting out a **proposal**. Parliament **enacted** it in 1839, and on 10 January 1840 the Uniform Penny Post was **implemented**. Its **simplicity** lay in one rule. A penny carried a letter of up to half an **ounce** anywhere in the **realm**, paid **beforehand** by the **sender**.

The Penny Black went on **retail** sale on 1 May 1840 and became usable on 6 May. Each **rectangle** carried an **engraved** **bust** of the young Queen Victoria, a **sovereign** shown in profile. **Altogether**, 68,808,000 were printed. None of the sheets had perforations, so **buyers** and clerks **clipped** each one out **individually** with **scissors**.

Mail rose from roughly 80 million items in 1839 to over 169 million in 1840, a **leap** to double. The red cancelling mark was hard to see on black, and the ink could be **erased**, allowing **reuse**. That **weakness** **spurred** a **revision**. **Consequently**, in February 1841 the Penny Red took over, cancelled in black, **thereby** **curtailing** **fraud**.

Britain issued the first postage stamps, and that **precedent** brought an **exemption**. By international agreement, the UK alone leaves its stamps **nameless**, with no country **designation** **inscribed** on them. **Solely** the ruler's head is held to **suffice** as **identification**. The **monarch**'s portrait makes an **implicit** declaration that other countries make in print.
