# EN: The 79 Days of New Coke

The threat arrived as a free **sip**. Pepsi had set up **booths** in shopping centres across the US, inviting **shoppers** to taste two **unmarked** colas and state a **preference**. Figures varied by region, but **tasters** generally picked Pepsi more often. Ordinary **palates** made priceless **publicity**, and **consumers** and **advertisers** took note.

Coca-Cola's reply was **deliberate**. Nearly 200,000 blind taste tests showed a **concoction** with extra **sweetness** consistently beating both the original and Pepsi. On 23 April 1985 the company announced a **risky** change, replacing a **formula** one year short of its **centenary**. The public called the sweeter **soda** New Coke. Its makers had **misjudged** more than **flavour**.

The **backlash** was swift. Daily calls to the consumer **hotline** rose to about 1,500, against about 400 before, almost all **complaints** from **furious** **callers**. **Grassroots** **protest** groups formed, and **panic-buying** of the original began. In June 1985 one **organiser**, Gay Mullins, filed a **lawsuit** to stop New Coke being sold in original-style packaging. It was dismissed a week later. The **outrage** was not.

Pepsi's chief executive **rejoiced** at Coke's apparent **concession**, giving his staff a day off. The **triumphant** **vindication** proved brief. After 79 days, on 11 July 1985, the original made its **comeback**, **relaunched** as Coca-Cola Classic. The **u-turn** was news. ABC **interrupted** an afternoon soap opera to **broadcast** it, and it led two network news **bulletins**.

The tests had measured a single mouthful, not **loyalty**, **nostalgia**, **defiance**, or the **emotional** **attachment** and **sentiment** that **familiarity** breeds over nearly a century of **sentimental** **heritage**. Each blind cup had hidden the label, and the label was precisely what **loyal** drinkers fought to defend.
