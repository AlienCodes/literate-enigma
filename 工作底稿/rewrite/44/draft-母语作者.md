# EN: The 79 Days of New Coke

Pepsi had set up **booths** in shopping centres across the US, inviting **shoppers** to **sip** two **unmarked** colas and state a **preference**. Figures varied by region, but **tasters** generally picked Pepsi more often. Each stall doubled as **publicity**, letting ordinary **palates** speak to **consumers** where **advertisers** usually did.

Coca-Cola's reply was **deliberate**. Nearly 200,000 blind taste tests showed a sweeter **formula** consistently beating both the original and Pepsi. On 23 April 1985 the company announced it would replace its **closely-guarded** 99-year-old **syrup**, a year short of its **centenary**. The public called the **beverage** New Coke. Its **sweetness** had won on **flavour**, but the **concoction** was **risky**.

The **backlash** was a **grassroots** **outcry**. **Hotline** calls rose from about 400 a day to about 1,500, almost all **complaints**. **Protest** groups were **organised**, and **faithful** drinkers began **panic-buying** the original. In June 1985 one **protester**, Gay Mullins, filed a **lawsuit** to keep New Coke out of packaging like the original's. Its **dismissal** followed a week later.

Pepsi's **triumphant** chief executive gave his **employees** a day off, treating the change as Coke's capitulation. After 79 days, on 11 July 1985, the original recipe was **relaunched** as Coca-Cola Classic. The **u-turn** was a **concession**, and for **loyalists** a **vindication**. ABC **interrupted** a soap opera to **broadcast** the **comeback**, which led two network news **bulletins**.

A blind test captures a **mouthful**, not the **loyalty**, **familiarity** and **sentimental** **attachment** around a brand. Coca-Cola had **misjudged** that **emotional** **heritage** and the **sentiment** and **nostalgia** of **loyal** drinkers. The tests had hidden the label from every taster, and the label was what people rushed to defend.
