# EN: The 79 Days of New Coke

Pepsi had set up **booths** in shopping centres across the US, inviting **shoppers** to **sip** two **unmarked** **beverages** and state a **preference**. Figures varied by region, but **tasters** generally picked Pepsi more often. Each stall, letting **consumers** judge by **palate** alone, doubled as **publicity**.

Coca-Cola's testing was **thorough** and **extensive**. Nearly 200,000 blind taste tests showed a sweeter **formula** consistently beating the original and Pepsi. On 23 April 1985 the company announced it would replace its 99-year-old **syrup**, a year short of its **centenary**. The public called the **soda** New Coke. Its extra **sweetness** had proved a winning **flavour**, but the **concoction** was a **wager**.

The **backlash** was a **grassroots** **outcry**. Consumer **hotline** calls rose from about 400 a day to about 1,500, almost all **furious** **complaints**. **Protest** groups were **organised**, and some **faithful** drinkers stocked up on the original. In June 1985 one protester, Gay Mullins, filed a **lawsuit** to keep New Coke out of packaging like the original's. Its **dismissal** came a week later.

Pepsi's chief executive gave his **employees** a day off, treating the change as Coke's **concession**. After 79 days, on 11 July 1985, the original formula was **relaunched** as Coca-Cola Classic. The **U-turn** was a **vindication** for **loyalists**. ABC **interrupted** a soap opera to **broadcast** the **comeback**, which led two network news **bulletins**.

Coca-Cola had **misjudged** what the tests measured. They captured a **mouthful**, not the **emotional** **attachment** that nearly a century of **familiarity** had built. To many, the old taste carried **nostalgia** and **heritage**. The tests had hidden the brand from every taster, so they could not **quantify** what it meant to **fiercely** **loyal** drinkers.
