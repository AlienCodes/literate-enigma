# EN: The 79 Days of New Coke

Pepsi had set up **booths** in shopping centres across the US, inviting **shoppers** to taste two **unmarked** colas and name a favourite. Each **sip** recorded a **preference**. Although figures varied by region, **tasters** generally picked Pepsi. The **publicity** worried Coca-Cola, whose **clientele**, like other **consumers**, seemed swayed by their **palates**.

Coca-Cola ran nearly 200,000 blind taste tests, and a sweeter **formula** consistently beat both the original and Pepsi. On 23 April 1985 the company announced it was replacing its 99-year-old **concoction**, a **closely-guarded** **syrup** nearing its **centenary**. The public called the **soda** New Coke. Trading a famous **flavour** for **sweetness** was **deliberate** and **risky**.

A **backlash** followed. Calls to the consumer **hotline** rose to about 1,500 a day, against about 400 before, almost all **complaints** from **furious** **callers**. **Grassroots** **protest** groups formed, and **panic-buying** of the original reflected fears of **scarcity**. In June 1985 the **organiser** Gay Mullins filed a class-action **lawsuit** seeking to bar New Coke from original-style packaging, which was dismissed a week later.

Pepsi's **triumphant** chief executive **rejoiced**, giving staff a day off, treating the change as Coke's **concession**. Amid the **outcry**, Coca-Cola **reconsidered**. After 79 days, on 11 July 1985, the original formula was **relaunched** as Coca-Cola Classic. ABC **interrupted** an afternoon soap opera to **broadcast** the **u-turn**, and the **comeback** led two network news **bulletins**.

The tests measured a mouthful. They missed **loyalty**, the **emotional** **attachment** of **nostalgic** drinkers for whom **familiarity** had turned a taste into **heritage**, part **nostalgia**, part **sentiment**. Each of the 200,000 tests hid the name on the cup, and that name was precisely what **loyal** **protesters** defended.
