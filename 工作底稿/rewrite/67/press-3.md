In 1984 Barry Marshall, a 32-year-old doctor at Fremantle Hospital, swallowed a **vile** **brew**. Two Petri dishes' worth of a **bacterium** had been scraped into beef **broth**, and he downed the whole **mixture**.

The **prevalent** **belief** **firmly** blamed ulcers on stress, **spicy** food and an **overly** **acidic** stomach. No **germ**, the **doctrine** held, could **withstand** such **caustic**, **hostile** surroundings.

At Royal Perth Hospital, the pathologist Robin Warren had seen curved, **coiled** bacteria in **biopsies** from patients with gastritis, and Marshall, then a young doctor, joined him. Their attempts to grow the microbe in **cultures** were **invariably** **unsuccessful** until Easter 1982, when the break left plates **unattended** for about five days instead of the **norm** of two. Colonies **bloomed**, and the **creature** was later named Helicobacter pylori.

In 1983 the pair's paper to the Gastroenterological Society of Australia was **snubbed**, its **reviewers** placing it **squarely** in the bottom tenth of that year's **submissions**. Attempts to infect piglets proved **ineffective**, so Marshall made his own **gut** the **testing** ground.

Within days came the **onset** of **vomiting**, **stale** breath and **listless** **weariness**. About ten days later, a **scope** showed the **signs** of gastritis, an **inflammation** of the stomach lining and a **precursor** of ulcers. It was not an ulcer. Samples from his stomach grew the same bacterium.

Urged on by an **insistent** **spouse**, he took **antibiotics**, which **purged** the infection. Ulcers came to be **treated** with such **medication**.

In 2005 Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering Helicobacter pylori and its role in gastritis and peptic ulcer disease.

The piglets had **shrugged** off the germ, and the only **participant** to fall ill was the researcher who had **concocted** the **potion**.
