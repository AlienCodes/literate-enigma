## 英文
In 1984 Barry Marshall, a 32-year-old doctor at Fremantle Hospital, swallowed a **vile** **brew**. Two Petri dishes' worth of a **bacterium** had been scraped into beef broth, and he downed the **potion** **deliberately**.

The medical **establishment** would have called it **pointless**. Ulcers, the **doctrine** held, were caused by stress, **spicy** food and too much **acid**. No **germ**, it was assumed, could **withstand** the stomach's **hostile** juices.

The **trail** began at Royal Perth Hospital, where the pathologist Robin Warren had noticed curved, **coiled** bacteria in stomach samples from patients with gastritis. Marshall, then a young doctor, joined him. **Repeated** attempts to grow the organism in the laboratory came to nothing. Over Easter 1982 the plates sat for about five days instead of the two they were **normally** given, and colonies appeared. The microbe was later named Helicobacter pylori.

In 1983 the pair sent a paper to the Gastroenterological Society of Australia. The society's **judgement** placed it in the bottom tenth of that year's submissions. Attempts to infect piglets proved **unsuccessful**, so Marshall made himself the **subject**.

Within days he was **vomiting**, his breath carried a **stale** **odour**, and **fatigue** set in. About ten days on, a **scope** passed into his stomach showed gastritis, an **irritation** of the stomach lining and a **forerunner** of ulcers. It was not yet an ulcer. Samples from his **gut** grew the same organism.

At the **insistence** of his **spouse**, he took **antibiotics**, which cleared the infection. Ulcers came to be treated with such **medication**.

In 2005 Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for **discovering** Helicobacter pylori and its role in gastritis and peptic ulcer disease.

The piglets had stayed healthy, and the only **volunteer** who fell ill was the researcher who had **mixed** the broth.
