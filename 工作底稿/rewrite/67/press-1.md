In 1984 Barry Marshall, a 32-year-old **physician** at Fremantle Hospital, **gulped** down a **murky** **broth**. Two Petri dishes of **cultured** **microbes** had been **scraped** into beef **stock**, and he swallowed the lot.

His **mischief** had a **rationale**. **Orthodox** **medicine** **attributed** ulcers to **stress**, spicy food and **excess** acid. No bacterium, the **consensus** held, could **survive** in so **corrosive** a **habitat**.

At Royal Perth Hospital the **pathologist** Robin Warren had **spotted** curved, **spiral** bacteria in stomach **biopsies** from patients with **gastritis**. Marshall, then a young **clinician**, joined him. Attempts to **cultivate** the organism kept failing. Over Easter 1982 the plates **lingered** in the **incubator** for about five days instead of the **customary** two, and **colonies** appeared. The **bug** was later named Helicobacter pylori.

In 1983 the pair sent a paper to the Gastroenterological Society of Australia. **Reviewers** **ranked** it in the bottom tenth of that year's **submissions**. **Efforts** to **infect** **piglets** **flopped**, so Marshall offered his own **gut**.

Within days he was **vomiting**, had **foul** breath and felt **drained**. About ten days on, an **endoscopy** **revealed** gastritis, an **inflamed** stomach **lining** that **precedes** ulcers. It was not an ulcer. The **culprit** was **recovered** from his stomach.

At his wife's **urging** he took **antibiotics**, which **eradicated** the **infection**. Ulcers came to be treated the same way.

In 2005 Marshall and Warren **shared** the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for **identifying** Helicobacter pylori and its role in gastritis and **peptic** ulcer disease.

The ten days of sickness that settled the matter were his own.
