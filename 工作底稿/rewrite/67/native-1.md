In 1984 Barry Marshall, a **32-year-old** doctor at Fremantle Hospital, **gulped** down a **murky** beef **broth**. Into it he had **scraped** two Petri dishes' **worth** of a **spiral** **microbe**.

His **reasoning** began with an **orthodoxy**. **Physicians** **blamed** ulcers on **stress**, **spicy** food and **excess** acid, and no **bacterium**, they **assumed**, could **survive** in so **corrosive** a **juice**.

Robin Warren, a **pathologist** at Royal Perth Hospital, had **spotted** curved bacteria in stomach **biopsies** from patients with **gastritis**. Marshall, then a **junior** doctor, **teamed up** with him. Every attempt to **culture** the **organism** failed until Easter 1982, when **plates** **lingered** in the **incubator** for about five days instead of the **customary** two. **Colonies** appeared. The **culprit** was later named Helicobacter pylori.

In 1983 the pair **submitted** a paper to the Gastroenterological Society of Australia. **Reviewers** rated it in the bottom tenth of that year's **entries**. **Efforts** to **infect** **piglets** also came to nothing, so Marshall turned **guinea pig** himself.

Within days he was **vomiting**, his breath **reeked** and he felt **exhausted**. An **endoscopy** about ten days later revealed gastritis, an **inflammation** of the stomach **lining** that **precedes** ulcers, and the bacteria were cultured from his stomach. He had not developed an ulcer, but he had **proved** that the microbe could **thrive** there.

At his wife's **urging** he took **antibiotics**, which **cleared** the **infection**. Ulcers came to be treated the same way.

In 2005 Marshall and Warren shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The **citation** honoured the discovery of Helicobacter pylori and its **role** in gastritis and **peptic** ulcer disease.
