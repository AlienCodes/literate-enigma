## 英文

In 1984 Barry Marshall, a **thirty-two-year-old** doctor at Fremantle Hospital, **scraped** the **bacterial** growth from two Petri dishes into beef **broth** and drank the **mixture**. He was **willingly** swallowing a **potion** **teeming** with the **germ** he blamed for ulcers.

Medical **belief** **upheld** a different **doctrine**. Ulcers were caused by stress, **spicy** food and too much **acid**, and no bacterium could **withstand** the stomach's **caustic** **secretions**.

The **unorthodox** idea began at Royal Perth Hospital, where Robin Warren, a specialist in **pathology**, had seen **curly** bacteria in tissue samples from patients with gastritis. Marshall, a young doctor, began to **collaborate** with him. Attempts to grow the organism were **invariably** **unsuccessful**. Then, over Easter 1982, dishes sat **undisturbed** for about five days instead of the usual two, and **colonies** **blossomed**. The microbe was later named Helicobacter pylori.

**Dismissive** **reviewers** were **unconvinced**. In 1983 the pair's paper to the Gastroenterological Society of Australia was placed in the bottom ten per cent of that year's **submissions**. Attempts to infect young **swine** also failed, so Marshall made himself the **guinea** pig.

Within days he began to **vomit**, his breath had a foul **odour** and he felt **fatigued**. A **tube** passed into his stomach about ten days later showed gastritis, an **inflammation** of its lining and a **precursor** to ulcers, not an ulcer itself. The bacteria were grown from his stomach. His **insistent** **spouse** persuaded him to take an **antibiotic**, which **purged** the infection. Ulcers came to be treated with antibiotics.

In 2005 Marshall and Warren shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering the bacterium and its role in gastritis and ulcer disease.

The experiment had one patient, its own **author**, and his wife had to talk him into the **medication**.
