In 1984 Barry Marshall, a **32-year-old** doctor at Fremantle Hospital, drank a **brew** of beef **broth** into which the **microbial** growth of two Petri dishes had been **scraped**.

The **prevalent** **doctrine** blamed ulcers on stress, **spicy** **cuisine** and excess **acid** **secretion**. No **bacterium**, the **profession** **firmly** held, could **withstand** so **caustic** a **habitat**.

Yet Robin Warren, a pathologist at Royal Perth Hospital, had seen curved, **coiled** bacteria in **gastric** **biopsies** from patients with gastritis. Marshall, a young doctor, began to **collaborate** with him. Attempts to culture the **germ** were **invariably** **unsuccessful** until Easter 1982, when plates sat in the incubator for about five days. They were **ordinarily** **binned** after two. Colonies **blossomed**. The organism was later named Helicobacter pylori.

In 1983 **unimpressed** **assessors** at the Gastroenterological Society of Australia **relegated** the pair's paper to the bottom tenth of that year's **submissions**. Efforts to infect young **swine** were **likewise** **in vain**. Marshall made himself the **guinea pig**.

Within days he was **vomiting**, his breath had a **disagreeable** **odour** and he felt **fatigued**. About ten days later an endoscopy revealed gastritis, an **inflammation** of the stomach's **mucous** **membrane** and a **precursor** to ulcers, **albeit** not an ulcer itself. The germ was cultured **directly** from his stomach.

**Spurred** on by his **insistent** **spouse**, he took **antibiotics**, which **purged** the infection. Ulcers came to be treated with such **medication**.

In 2005 Marshall and Warren shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering Helicobacter pylori and its role in gastritis and peptic ulcer disease. Gastritis, the illness he had **willingly** given himself, is named in the prize citation.
