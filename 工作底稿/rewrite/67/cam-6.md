In 1984 Barry Marshall, a **thirty-two-year-old** doctor at Fremantle Hospital, **scraped** two Petri dishes of **microbial** growth into beef **broth** and drank the **brew**. He was **willingly** swallowing the **germ** he had **indicted** for ulcers.

The **prevalent** **doctrine** blamed stress, **spicy** **cuisine** and acid **overload**, and held that no bacterium could **withstand** the stomach's **hostile**, **caustic** **digestive** **secretions**.

The **heresy** began at Royal Perth Hospital, where the **pathology** specialist Robin Warren had seen **curly** bacteria in **gastric** tissue from patients with gastritis. Marshall, a young doctor, began to **collaborate** with him. Efforts to grow the organism were **invariably** **unsuccessful** until Easter 1982, when dishes sat **undisturbed** for about five days instead of two and colonies **blossomed**. The microbe was later named Helicobacter pylori.

**Doubters** were **unconvinced**. In 1983 the pair's paper for the Gastroenterological Society of Australia was **relegated** to the bottom ten per cent of that year's **submissions**. Attempts to infect young **swine** also **miscarried**, and Marshall became the **guinea** pig.

Within days he began to **vomit**, his breath grew **smelly** and he felt **fatigued**. A **tube** passed into his **gut** about ten days later made gastritis **evident**, an **inflammation** of the stomach's **mucous** lining and a **prelude** to ulcers, not an ulcer. The germ was grown from his stomach. His **spouse** urged an **antibiotic**, which **purged** the infection, and ulcers came to be treated with antibiotics.

In 2005 Marshall and Warren shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering the **bacterium** and its role in gastritis and ulcer disease.

The experiment's only patient was the author of the **contention** it tested.
