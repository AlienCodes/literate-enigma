In 1984 Barry Marshall, a 32-year-old doctor at Fremantle Hospital, swallowed a **vile** **brew**. Two Petri dishes' worth of a **bacterium**, scraped into beef **broth**, made up the **cocktail**.

The **prevalent** **belief** **firmly** blamed ulcers on stress, **spicy** food and an **overly** **acidic** stomach. No **germ**, the **doctrine** held, could **withstand** such **caustic**, **hostile** surroundings.

Robin Warren, who worked in **pathology** at Royal Perth Hospital, had seen curved, **coiled** bacteria in **biopsies** from patients with gastritis. Marshall, a young doctor, worked **alongside** him. Attempts to grow the microbe were **invariably** **unsuccessful** until Easter 1982, when plates sat **unattended** for about five days instead of the **norm** of two, and colonies **bloomed**. The organism was later named Helicobacter pylori.

In 1983 **reviewers** for the Gastroenterological Society of Australia placed the pair's paper **squarely** in the bottom tenth of that year's **submissions**. Infecting piglets **experimentally** proved **ineffective**, so Marshall made himself the **guinea** pig.

**Shortly** afterwards came the **onset** of **vomiting**, **stale** breath and **listless** **weariness**. About ten days later, a **scope** **lowered** into his stomach showed gastritis, an **inflammation** of the **gastric** lining and a **precursor** of ulcers, **albeit** not an ulcer itself. Samples from his **gut** grew the same bacterium.

**Spurred** on by an **insistent** **spouse**, he took **antibiotics**, which **purged** the infection, and ulcers came to be treated with such **medication**. In 2005 Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering Helicobacter pylori and its role in gastritis and peptic ulcer disease.

The piglets had **shrugged** off the germ, and the only **participant** it **sickened** was the researcher who had **concocted** the **potion**.
