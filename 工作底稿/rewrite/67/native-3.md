In 1984 Barry Marshall, a **32-year-old** doctor at Fremantle Hospital, **scraped** the growth from two Petri dishes into beef **broth**. Then, quite **intentionally**, he drank the **mixture**.

The **prevalent** **doctrine** blamed ulcers on stress, **spicy** **cuisine** and the excessive **secretion** of **acid**. No **bacterium**, the **profession** **firmly** held, could **withstand** so **caustic** a **habitat**.

Yet Robin Warren, a pathologist at Royal Perth Hospital, had seen curved, **coiled** bacteria in stomach **biopsies** from patients with gastritis. Marshall, a young doctor, joined him. Attempts to culture the **germ** proved **unsuccessful** until Easter 1982, when plates sat in the incubator for about five days. They were **ordinarily** **binned** after two. Colonies appeared. The organism was later named Helicobacter pylori.

In 1983 the pair submitted their **thesis** to the Gastroenterological Society of Australia. **Unimpressed**, its **assessors** **relegated** the paper to the bottom tenth of that year's **submissions**. Efforts to infect young **swine** were **likewise** **in vain**. Marshall then made himself the **guinea pig**.

Within days he **vomited**, his breath had a **disagreeable** **odour** and **weariness** set in. An endoscopy about ten days later revealed gastritis, an **inflammation** of the stomach's **mucous** **membrane** and a **precursor** to ulcers. **Strictly**, he had not developed an ulcer. The organism was then cultured **directly** from his stomach.

At the **insistent** urging of his **spouse**, he took **antibiotics**, which **banished** the infection. Ulcers, **similarly**, came to be treated with them.

In 2005 Marshall and Warren shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Its citation named Helicobacter pylori and its role in gastritis and peptic ulcer disease. The illness he had **willingly** given himself features there by name.
