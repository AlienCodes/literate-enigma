# EN: Dance of Death: The Fall of Galloping Gertie

Late on the morning of 7 November 1940, a wind of about 40 miles an hour set the Tacoma Narrows Bridge **twisting** **violently**. Its **deck** **tilted** **alternately** from side to side, until the steel **tore apart**, sending sections **toppling** into Puget Sound. There were no human **fatalities**. **Footage** of the **spectacle** reached classrooms worldwide.

It had opened in July 1940 as the third-longest **suspension** **span** in the world at the time, its roadway hung from **cables**. **Shallow** girders eight feet deep made it **slender**, **elegant** and **economical**, but far from **rigid**. Even in **mild** breezes it **heaved** and **rippled**, earning the **nickname** Galloping Gertie.

The popular account, **perpetuated** by **widely** used **textbooks**, **invokes** **resonance**: a **periodic** **impulse** that **coincides** with an object's natural **frequency**, so the **amplitude** grows with every push. The **analogy** is **intuitive** but rests on a **misconception**: a **steady** wind beats in time with nothing.

The **underlying** **mechanism** was flutter: each twist altered the air flowing past the deck, and that air **reinforced** the twist. This **feedback** loop **derived** ever more energy from the wind. In March 1941 a **federally** **commissioned** board blamed the deck's **flexibility** and wing-like forces; in February 1991 two engineers **asserted** in a physics journal that resonance was the wrong **diagnosis**.

The board's **recommendation** **prompted** wind-tunnel tests at the University of Washington, **ultimately** helping make such testing **customary** for suspension bridges. A **sturdier** **successor** opened in October 1950. Gertie may still **instruct**, provided she is read **accurately**: the wind kept no time with the bridge; the bridge fed on the wind.
