# EN: Dance of Death: The Fall of Galloping Gertie

On the morning of 7 November 1940, a **moderate** wind of about 40 miles an hour set the Tacoma Narrows Bridge **twisting**. Its **deck** **tilted** from side to side in ever more **violent** **oscillations** until the steel **tore apart** and **tumbled** into the water. No one died. **Footage** of the **spectacle** would later **fascinate** classrooms worldwide.

The bridge had opened in July 1940 as the third-longest **suspension** **span** in the world at the time, its roadway hung from steel ropes. Girders only eight feet deep **strengthened** the deck, making it **slender**, **elegant** and **economical**, but far from **rigid**. Even **faint** **breezes** made it **heave**, earning the **nickname** Galloping Gertie.

**Textbooks** have long **invoked** **resonance**: a **periodic** push that matches an object's natural **frequency** can **amplify** its motion, as with a child **propelled** on a swing. The **analogy** is **intuitive** but **deceptive**. That morning's wind did not **pulse** in time with the bridge; it blew **steadily**.

The **mechanism** was **subtler**. Each twist **altered** the passing airflow, which **reinforced** the next twist: a **feedback** loop **tapping** ever more energy from the wind. In March 1941 a federal **panel** blamed the deck's **flexibility** and wing-like forces. Later analysis named it flutter; in February 1991 two engineers **asserted** in a physics journal that textbooks had **erred**.

The panel urged **wind-tunnel** tests, and trials on **scaled-down** **replicas** **gradually** became **customary** for suspension bridges. A **sturdier** **replacement** opened in October 1950. Gertie's fall may still **instruct**, if read **accurately**: the wind kept no time with the bridge, but the bridge kept drawing on the wind.
