# EN: Dance of Death: The Fall of Galloping Gertie

On the morning of 7 November 1940 a bridge began to dance. In a **moderate** wind of about 40 miles an hour, the **deck** of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge **twisted** like a **ribbon**, one side rising as the other **descended**. Then it **snapped** and **tumbled** into the water. No one was killed. The **footage** became a **spectacle** in classrooms **across the globe**.

The bridge had opened in July 1940. Its central **span**, the third-longest **suspension** span in the world at the time, hung from two **cables**. To keep it **slender**, **elegant** and **economical**, engineers chose a **shallow** deck only eight feet deep. Even **mild** **breezes** made it **heave**, so drivers **nicknamed** it Galloping Gertie.

**Textbooks** later **invoked** **resonance**. A **periodic** push that matches an object's natural **rhythm** can **amplify** its motion, like a child **pumping** a swing higher. The **analogy** is **intuitive** but **deceptive**. That morning's wind did not **pulse** in time with Gertie; it blew **steadily**.

In March 1941 an official **investigation** **ascribed** the collapse to the deck's **flexibility** and to wind forces like those on a wing. In February 1991 two engineers **asserted** that textbooks had **misjudged** the **mechanism**. Each twist **altered** the airflow, which **reinforced** the next. This **self-feeding** **oscillation**, called **flutter**, kept **tapping** energy from a steady wind.

The investigation **proposed** tests on **miniature** models in a **wind tunnel**, which became **customary** for suspension bridges. A **sturdier** **replacement** opened in October 1950. Gertie may still teach, if read **accurately**: the wind did not keep time with the bridge; the bridge kept **exploiting** the wind.
