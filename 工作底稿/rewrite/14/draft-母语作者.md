# EN: Dance of Death: The Fall of Galloping Gertie

On the morning of 7 November 1940, a **moderate** wind of about 40 miles an hour set the Tacoma Narrows Bridge **twisting**. Its **deck** **heaved** in ever more **violent** **oscillations** until it **tore apart** and fell into the water. No one died. Caught on film, the collapse became a **dramatic** **spectacle** screened **globally**.

Opened in July 1940, it was the third-longest **suspension** **span** in the world at the time, its deck hung from steel **cables**. To save money, it was **reinforced** with girders only eight feet deep, leaving it **slender**, **elegant** and **economical**. Even **breezes** made it **ripple**, earning the **nickname** Galloping Gertie.

**Textbooks** often **depict** the disaster as **resonance**: **periodic** pushes that match an object's natural **frequency** **amplify** each **vibration**, as with a swing. The **analogy** is **intuitive** but **inaccurate**, since a **steady** wind offers no **recurring** **pulses** to follow.

The **mechanism** was **subtler**. Once the deck twisted, it **altered** the air flowing past, and that **distorted** flow drove the twist further: a **self-feeding** cycle now called flutter. In March 1941 a federal **panel** blamed the deck's **flexibility** and **wing-like** wind forces. In February 1991 two engineers **asserted** in a physics journal that textbooks had **erred** in calling it resonance.

The panel had urged **wind-tunnel** tests, and **scaled-down** **replicas** at the University of Washington faced **simulated** gales. Such work shaped the **replacement**, opened in October 1950; wind tests now **routinely** guide suspension designs. Gertie's fall may still **instruct**, if read **accurately**: the wind kept no time with the bridge; the bridge kept **tapping** the wind.
