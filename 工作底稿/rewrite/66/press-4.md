## 英文
In 1925 six **hand-picked** **scrap-metal** **traders** met at the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris. Their host, a **self-styled** **deputy** **director-general** of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs, bound them by an **oath** of **secrecy**, **ostensibly** to spare the public **indignation**. Then he **confidentially** shared his **top-secret** news. The **state-owned** Eiffel Tower was to be sold for scrap.

The **pretext** was less **absurd** than it sounds. Built for the 1889 World's Fair, the **monument** had been meant to come down after twenty years. In a newspaper article that Victor Lustig read in 1925, the authorities **lamented** the **hefty** cost of its **maintenance**.

The **high-ranking** **caller** was Lustig, and his **official-looking** stationery was a **forgery**. Having **concocted** the **scam**, he **politely** asked for **tenders**.

The **unlucky** winner was André Poisson, a **businessman** **impatient** to gain a **foothold** in Paris. Lustig **intimated** that a **low-paid** official needed his salary **supplementing**, so Poisson **sweetened** the **transaction** with a **bribe**. Lustig left for Vienna with the **takings**.

Poisson was too **ashamed** of being **tricked** to go to the police, and the newspapers carried nothing.

About a month later Lustig **reappeared** in Paris and staged an **encore** for another group of dealers. It was **unsuccessful**. The chosen buyer grew **distrustful** and went to the police, the story reached the newspapers, and Lustig made his **getaway**. The second sale, in other words, was **mythical**.

In the United States he was later arrested for making **fake** money, broke out of custody and was **recaptured** 27 days later. He served time on Alcatraz and died of pneumonia in 1947.

Of the two buyers he chose, the one who paid kept **quietly** to himself and the one who kept his money went to the police.
