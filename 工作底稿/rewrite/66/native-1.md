In a **discreet** suite at the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris, in 1925, six **scrap** dealers heard a **startling** **confidence**. Their **host**, a **deputy** **director-general** from the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs, explained that the Eiffel Tower was to be sold for scrap. He **swore** them to **secrecy**, since news of it would cause a public **outcry**, and **invited** **bids**.

The tower had been built for the 1889 World's Fair and was meant to be **dismantled** after twenty years. That **deadline** had passed long before. Then Victor Lustig read a newspaper article in which the authorities **lamented** its **costly** **upkeep**, and an idea took **shape**.

The **official**, of course, was Lustig. His **letterhead** was **forged** and his **title** **invented**. André Poisson, a dealer **eager** to **establish** himself in Paris, **submitted** the **winning** **offer**. Lustig then **hinted** that a **modest** state **salary** needed **supplementing**, and Poisson **obligingly** added a **bribe** on top of the price. The **swindler** left Paris with the money, bound for Vienna.

Poisson was too **embarrassed** to go to the police. Nothing appeared in the papers.

About a month later Lustig **returned** and tried the same **scheme** on a fresh group of dealers. This time the chosen buyer grew **suspicious** and **alerted** the police. The story reached the newspapers, and Lustig **escaped** once more. The second **sale**, so **celebrated** in the **legend**, never **happened**.

He later turned to **counterfeiting** in the United States, where he was **arrested**, broke out of **custody** and was **recaptured** 27 days later. He served time on Alcatraz and died of **pneumonia** in 1947.

The **monument** he had **auctioned** off still stands over Paris.
