## 英文
In 1925 six **scrap-metal** **traders** were invited to the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris for a meeting held in strict **secrecy**. Their host, a **self-styled** **deputy** **director-general** of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs, had **bureaucratic** news. The Eiffel Tower was to be sold for scrap.

The **pretext** was less **absurd** than it sounds. The tower had gone up for the 1889 World's Fair as a **temporary** **monument**, meant to come down after twenty years. That year Victor Lustig had read a newspaper article in which the authorities **lamented** the high cost of its **upkeep**.

The **ministerial** visitor was Lustig, and his **official-looking** stationery was a **forgery**. He asked the dealers for **discretion**, warning that word of the plan could **provoke** a public **uproar**, and then invited **tenders**.

André Poisson, a **businessman** **impatient** to gain a **foothold** in Paris, made the winning offer. Lustig **intimated** that an **ill-paid** official's salary needed **supplementing**, and Poisson took the **hint**, adding a **bribe** to the price. Lustig left for Vienna with the money.

Poisson was too **ashamed** to go to the police, and the papers printed nothing. About a month later Lustig **returned** to stage a **sequel** with another group of dealers. This time the chosen buyer grew **distrustful** and went to the police instead. The story reached the newspapers, and Lustig left Paris in a **hurry**. The second sale, **contrary** to the **legend**, ended **unsuccessfully**.

In the United States he was later arrested for **counterfeiting**, broke out of **jail** and was **recaptured** 27 days later. He served time on Alcatraz and died of pneumonia in 1947.

Of the two buyers he **courted**, the one who paid kept quiet and the one who kept his money called the police.
