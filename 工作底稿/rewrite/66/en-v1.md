## 英文

In 1925 six **scrap-metal** **traders** met **secretly** at the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris. A deputy **director-general** of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs shared the **momentous** news, **confidentially**, that the **world-famous** Eiffel Tower was to be sold for scrap. He invited **tenders** and made each dealer **solemnly** **vow** **secrecy**, **ostensibly** to spare the public **indignation**.

The **pretext** was not **absurd**. Built for the 1889 World's Fair, the **monument** had **originally** been **destined** to come down after twenty years. Victor Lustig had read a newspaper article in which the authorities **lamented** the **hefty** cost of its **upkeep**. The **high-ranking** deputy was Lustig, a **crook** whose **ministerial** stationery was a **forgery**.

André Poisson, a **businessman** **impatient** to gain a **foothold** in Paris, won. Lustig then **intimated** that his modest **civil-service** **remuneration** needed **supplementing**, and Poisson paid an **additional** **bribe**. Lustig left for Vienna with the **takings**.

**Fleeced** and too **ashamed** to go to the police, Poisson swallowed the loss **quietly**. Not a **whisper** of the **deceit** appeared in print.

About a month later Lustig had the **gall** to **reappear** with an **encore** of his **script** for new dealers. This time the buyer grew **distrustful** and went to the police. The **affair** made the papers, and Lustig made his **getaway**. The **much-repeated** second sale is a **fairy-tale**.

Later arrested in the United States for making **fake** money, he broke out of **detention**, was **recaptured** 27 days later and served time on the **penal** island of Alcatraz. He died of pneumonia in 1947.

Of his two buyers, the one who paid kept **mum** and the one who kept his money went to the police.
