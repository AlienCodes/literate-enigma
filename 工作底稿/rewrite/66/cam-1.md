At the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris, in 1925, a **gentleman** posing as a **deputy** director-general of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs **confided** a plan to six scrap-metal **dealers**. The Eiffel Tower, he **revealed**, was to be sold for **scrap**.

The **claim** sounded **plausible**. The tower had been built for the 1889 World's Fair, and the **original** plan was to **dismantle** it after twenty years. That **deadline** had passed, and Victor Lustig had read a newspaper article in which the **authorities** **lamented** its **maintenance** costs.

For the occasion Lustig had **forged** government **stationery**. He **swore** his guests to **secrecy**, explaining that news of the sale would **provoke** a public **outcry**, and **invited** **bids**.

André Poisson, **eager** to **establish** himself among the city's **merchants**, **submitted** the winning offer. When Lustig **hinted** that a **modest** official **salary** needed **supplementing**, Poisson handed over a **bribe** on top of the price. Lustig **departed** for Vienna with the money.

The **victim** was too **embarrassed** to go to the police, and nothing appeared in the papers.

About a month later Lustig **returned** and **attempted** the same **scheme** on another group of dealers. This time the chosen **buyer** grew **suspicious** and **alerted** the police. The story reached the **press**, and Lustig **fled**. Despite the **legend**, the second sale **collapsed** before any money changed hands.

Later, in the United States, he was **arrested** for **counterfeiting**, **escaped**, and was **recaptured** twenty-seven days afterwards. He served time on Alcatraz and died of **pneumonia** in 1947. Of the two sales in his story, only the first had ever **yielded** him a franc.
