## 英文
In 1925 six **hand-picked** **scrap-metal** **traders** met at the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris. Their host, **parading** as a **deputy** **director-general** of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs, **confidentially** shared his news. The **world-famous** Eiffel Tower was to be sold for scrap. He bound his **listeners** by an **oath** of **secrecy**, **ostensibly** to spare the public **indignation**.

The **pretext** was less **absurd** than it sounds. Built for the 1889 World's Fair, the **monument** was meant to come down after twenty years. In 1925 Victor Lustig had read a newspaper article in which the authorities **lamented** the **hefty** cost of its **maintenance**.

That **high-ranking** **caller** was Lustig, and his **official-looking** stationery was a **forgery**. Having **concocted** the **scam**, he asked for **tenders**.

The **unlucky** **victor** was André Poisson, a **businessman** **impatient** to gain a **foothold** in Paris. Lustig **intimated** that a **low-paid** official needed his salary **supplementing**, so Poisson **sweetened** the **transaction** with a **bribe**. Lustig left for Vienna with the **takings**.

**Fleeced** and too **ashamed** to go to the police, Poisson kept **mum**, and the newspapers carried nothing.

About a month later Lustig **reappeared** with an **encore** for another group of dealers. This time the chosen buyer grew **distrustful** and went to the police, the **affair** reached the newspapers, and Lustig made his **getaway**. The **much-repeated** second sale is a **fairy-tale**.

In the United States he was later arrested for making **fake** money, broke out of custody and was **recaptured** 27 days later. He served time on Alcatraz and died of pneumonia in 1947.

Of the two buyers he chose, the one who paid kept quiet and the one who kept his money called a **policeman**.
