## 英文
In 1925 six scrap-metal **dealers** were **summoned** to a **confidential** meeting at the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris. Their **host** introduced himself as **deputy** director-general of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs, and he shared a **secret**. The Eiffel Tower was to be sold for **scrap**.

The **pretext** was not **absurd**. The tower had been built for the 1889 World's Fair and was meant to be **dismantled** after twenty years. That year Victor Lustig had read a newspaper article in which the **authorities** **lamented** its high **maintenance** costs.

The **official** was Lustig. He had **forged** government **stationery**, and he swore the dealers to **secrecy** to avoid a public **outcry**. Then he asked for **bids**.

André Poisson, a dealer **eager** to **establish** himself in Paris, won. Lustig **hinted** that a **modest** official **salary** needed **supplementing**, and Poisson **obligingly** handed over a **bribe** on top of the price. Lustig left for Vienna with the money.

Poisson was too **embarrassed** to go to the **police**, and nothing appeared in the papers. About a month later Lustig **returned** and tried the same **scheme** on another group of dealers. This time the chosen buyer grew **suspicious** and went to the police. The story reached the newspapers, and Lustig **escaped** from Paris. The second "sale", in other words, never **closed**.

He was later arrested in the United States for **counterfeiting**, broke out of **custody** and was **recaptured** 27 days later. He served time on Alcatraz and died of **pneumonia** in 1947.

The **legend** credits him with selling the tower twice. The **paperwork** shows one **buyer**, one **bribe**, and one dealer too **ashamed** to **complain**.
