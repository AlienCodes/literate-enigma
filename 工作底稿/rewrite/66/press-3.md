In 1925 six **scrap-metal** **traders** were invited to the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris. Their host, a **self-styled** **deputy** **director-general** of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs, bound them by an **oath** of **secrecy**, **solemnly** explaining that his news could stir public **indignation**. The Eiffel Tower, he revealed **confidentially**, was to be sold for scrap.

The **pretext** was less **absurd** than it sounds. Built for the 1889 World's Fair, the **monument** had been meant to come down after twenty years. In a newspaper article that Victor Lustig read in 1925, the authorities **lamented** the **hefty** cost of its **upkeep**.

The **ministerial** visitor was Lustig, and his **official-looking** stationery was a **forgery**. Having **concocted** the **scam**, he **politely** asked for **tenders**.

The **unlucky** winner was André Poisson, a **businessman** **impatient** to gain a **foothold** in Paris. Lustig **intimated** that a **low-paid** official needed his salary **supplementing**, so Poisson **sweetened** the **transaction** with a **bribe**. Lustig **promptly** left for Vienna with the money.

Poisson had been **tricked**, but he was too **ashamed** to go to the police, and the newspapers carried nothing.

About a month later Lustig **reappeared** in Paris to stage an **encore** for a fresh group of dealers. This time the chosen buyer grew **distrustful** and went to the police. The story reached the newspapers, and Lustig made his **getaway**. The second sale, **notably**, was **unsuccessful**, whatever the **mythical** version **claims**.

In the United States he was later arrested for counterfeiting, broke out of custody and was **recaptured** 27 days later. He served time on Alcatraz and died of pneumonia in 1947.

Of the two buyers he **courted**, the one who paid kept quiet and the one who kept his money called the police.
