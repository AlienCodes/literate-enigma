In 1925, in a **discreet** **suite** at the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris, six **scrap-metal** dealers heard **solemn** news. A **deputy** **director-general** from the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs told them that the Eiffel Tower was to be sold for scrap. He made them take an **oath** of **secrecy**, to spare the city a **patriotic** **outcry**, and asked each for a **tender**.

The **monument** had been built for the 1889 World's Fair and was meant to come down after twenty years. Victor Lustig had read a newspaper article in which the authorities **lamented** the high cost of its **upkeep**. The rest was **trickery**.

Those **ministerial** **credentials** were **fake**, typed on government stationery that Lustig had **forged**. André Poisson, a dealer **eager** for a **foothold** in Paris, made the best offer. His **host** then let it be understood that a **meagre** state **salary** needed **supplementing**. Poisson **obligingly** added a **bribe** on top of the price, and Lustig left for Vienna with the money.

The **unfortunate** buyer had been **fleeced**, yet he was too **ashamed** to go to the police. Not a line appeared in any **gazette**.

About a month later Lustig returned to stage an **encore** for a fresh **select** group of dealers. This time the chosen buyer felt **uneasy**, and his **distrust** carried him to the police. The **caper** reached the newspapers, and the **slippery** **crook** left Paris in **haste**. The famous **sequel**, in short, ended without a sale.

In the United States he was later arrested for **counterfeiting**, escaped from **detention** and was **recaptured** 27 days later. He served time on Alcatraz and died of **pneumonia** in 1947.

Of the two buyers he chose, only Poisson paid.
