In 1925 six **unsuspecting** scrap-metal dealers met **secretly** at the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris. **Confidentially**, a **deputy** director-general of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs told them that the Eiffel Tower was to be sold for scrap. He invited **tenders**, warning of public **outrage** if word got out, and made each **trader** **vow** **secrecy**.

The deputy was Victor Lustig, a **crook** whose **ministerial** stationery was a **forgery**. His **pretext**, though, borrowed from fact. Built for the 1889 World's Fair, the tower had been **destined** to come down after twenty years, and Lustig had read a newspaper article in which the authorities were **lamenting** the cost of its **upkeep**.

André Poisson, **keenly** seeking a **foothold** in the **mercantile** life of the **metropolis**, emerged **victorious**. **Obliquely**, Lustig suggested that an official's **remuneration** needed **supplementing**, and Poisson added a **bribe** to the price. Lustig **withdrew** to Vienna with the **takings**.

The **fleeced** buyer was too **ashamed** to approach the police, and the newspapers printed not a **whisper** of the **deceit**.

About a month later Lustig was back, **brazenly** staging a **replay** of his **script** for a fresh **clientele**. This time the chosen **contestant** grew **uneasy** and took his **distrust** to the police. The story reached the newspapers, and Lustig made his **getaway**. In other words, the second sale of legend is **mythical**.

Later, in the United States, he was caught for counterfeiting, broke out of **detention** and was **recaptured** twenty-seven days afterwards. After **incarceration** on Alcatraz, he died of pneumonia in 1947. Of the two sales credited to him, only the first ever earned him a franc.
