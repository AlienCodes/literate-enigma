## 英文
In 1925, in a **discreet** **suite** at the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris, six **scrap-metal** **traders** heard **solemn** **tidings**. A **deputy** **director-general** from the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs told them, **confidentially**, that the Eiffel Tower was to be sold for scrap. He demanded an **oath** of **secrecy**, to spare Paris a public **commotion**, and invited each to submit a **quotation**.

The **monument** had been built for the 1889 World's Fair and was due for **removal** after twenty years. Victor Lustig had read a newspaper article in which the authorities **lamented** the cost of its **upkeep**. The rest was **deceit**. His **ministerial** **credentials** were **fake**, down to the **fraudulent** government stationery.

André Poisson, a **businessman** keen to gain a **foothold** in Paris, made the best offer. Lustig then let it be known that a modest state salary needed **supplementing**, and Poisson **greased** his palm with an **additional** **bribe**. The **pretender** left for Vienna with the **takings**.

The **unfortunate** buyer had been **fleeced**, yet **embarrassment** kept him from the police. Not a line appeared in any **gazette**.

About a month later Lustig returned to try the same **trick** on a fresh **select** group of dealers. This time the chosen buyer grew **doubtful**, and his **distrust** carried him to the police. The **caper** made the papers, and the **slippery** **rogue** made his **getaway**. The **sequel**, famous in popular legend as a second sale, ended without one.

In the United States the **forger** was later arrested for counterfeiting. He escaped from **detention**, was **recaptured** 27 days later and served time on Alcatraz. He died of pneumonia in 1947.

Of the two buyers he chose, only Poisson paid.
