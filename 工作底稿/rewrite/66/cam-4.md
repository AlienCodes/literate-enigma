## 英文
In 1925 six **unsuspecting** scrap-metal dealers met **secretly** at the Hôtel de Crillon in Paris. **Confidentially**, a **deputy** director-general of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs told them that the Eiffel Tower was to be sold for scrap. He invited **tenders**, warning that **disclosure** would provoke public **outrage**, and made each **trader** **solemnly** **vow** **secrecy**.

The deputy was Victor Lustig, a **crook** whose **ministerial** stationery was a **forgery**. His **pretext**, **nevertheless**, was **credible**. Built for the 1889 World's Fair, the tower had **originally** been **destined** to come down after twenty years. Lustig had also read a newspaper article in which the authorities were **lamenting** the **hefty** cost of its **upkeep**.

André Poisson, an **entrepreneur** **keenly** seeking a **foothold** in the **mercantile** life of the **metropolis**, emerged **victorious**. **Obliquely**, Lustig suggested that an official's **remuneration** needed **supplementing**, and Poisson paid an **additional** **bribe**. Lustig left for Vienna with the **takings**.

The **fleeced** buyer was too **ashamed** to approach the police, and the newspapers printed not a **whisper** of the **deceit**.

About a month later Lustig was back, **brazenly** staging a **replay** of his **script** for fresh **prey**. This time the chosen **contestant** grew **uneasy** and took his **distrust** to the police. The **affair** reached the newspapers, and Lustig made his **getaway**. In other words, the second **transaction** of legend is **mythical**.

Later, in the United States, Lustig was caught for counterfeiting, broke out of **detention** and was **recaptured** twenty-seven days afterwards. After **incarceration** on the **penal** island of Alcatraz, he died of pneumonia in 1947. Of the two sales credited to him, only the first **actually** earned him a franc.
