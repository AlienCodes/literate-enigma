# EN: Microplastics: Everywhere, but How Harmful?

In May 2004 Richard Thompson, a **marine** **biologist** at the University of Plymouth, and colleagues reported in the journal Science on an **invisible** **pollutant**. **Sifting** **plankton** samples kept since the 1960s, they found that **microscopic** plastic **fragments** and **synthetic** **fibres** had grown more **abundant** in the sea. They **labelled** the **particles** microplastics.

Since then the **durable** **debris** has proved **ubiquitous**, turning up on **shorelines**, in deep-sea **sediment**, in **rainfall** and in Arctic ice. In December 2015 America **prohibited** microbeads, tiny plastic **beads** in face **scrubs** and other **rinse**-off **cosmetics**.

The search then turned **inward**. In March 2022 a small Dutch study found plastic in the blood of about three-quarters of 22 healthy **donors**. In March 2024 a New England Journal of Medicine study linked plastic in **fatty** **plaque** in neck **arteries** to a higher risk of heart attack, **stroke** or death. Its authors stressed that **correlation** is not **causation**.

The most **sensational** figures are the **shakiest**. In June 2019 a report for WWF, a **conservation** group, **reckoned** that people may swallow a credit card's worth of plastic a week; **critics** call that a gross **overestimate**. Two months later the World Health Organization was **reassuring** but **tentative**: on limited evidence, microplastics in drinking water posed no **apparent** risk at current levels. In February 2025 a Nature Medicine study reported plastic in brain **tissue**, popularised as a **spoonful**; others **queried** its **methodology**.

**Detection** has outrun understanding. Microplastics are now far easier to find than to judge. Harm is **plausible** but **unproven**, and until the **verdict** is in, **sober** **measurement** may serve better than **panic**.
