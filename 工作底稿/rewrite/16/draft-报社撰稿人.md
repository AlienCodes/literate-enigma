# EN: The Sound That Shook the World

Shortly after ten in the morning on 27 August 1883, Krakatoa, a **volcanic** island in the Sunda **Strait**, **unleashed** a **colossal** explosion. About four hours later, **islanders** on Rodrigues, nearly 4,800 kilometres away, took the **rumble** for **distant** **gunfire**. **Barometers** worldwide recorded the **atmospheric** pressure wave, some catching seven **successive** passages in about five days.

The **eruption**, begun in May 1883, **culminated** as about two-thirds of the island **crumbled** into the sea. **Tsunamis**, **reportedly** over 30 metres high in places, **swamped** **coastal** **settlements** on Java and Sumatra. Dutch **colonial** **administrators** counted 36,417 dead, mostly **drowned**; the figure may be an **underestimate**.

**Telegrams** from Batavia, **relayed** through Singapore by **submarine** **cable**, reached London within hours. Within a day or two, European and North American **readers** followed what is often called the first global news event. Yet early **dispatches** were **terse**; the **magnitude** of the **devastation** emerged only days later.

**Ash** and **sulphur** gas, **hurled** tens of kilometres high, formed a **haze** that circled the **equator** within two weeks. In November 1883, **dusk** skies over Europe and North America **glowed** **crimson**; in New York State and Connecticut, fire engines were **summoned** to **phantom** **blazes**. Later **reconstructions** **indicate** global temperatures **dipped** a few tenths of a degree Celsius in 1884.

In February 1884, Britain's Royal Society appealed in The Times for dated observations worldwide. A **dissenting** **minority** of experts blamed the skies on **high-altitude** **moisture** or **cosmic** dust. Through air, wire and **twilight**, Krakatoa reached people who had never heard of it, in **scattered** fragments some scientists began to **stitch** together.
