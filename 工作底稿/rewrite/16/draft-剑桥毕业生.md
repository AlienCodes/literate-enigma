# EN: The Sound That Shook the World

On the morning of 27 August 1883, Krakatoa, a **volcanic** island between Java and Sumatra, **unleashed** its **mightiest** explosion. About four hours later, **islanders** on Rodrigues, nearly 4,800 kilometres away, took the **roar** for **distant** **gunfire**. **Barometers** across the **globe** recorded the **ensuing** pressure wave, some of them seven times over about five days.

Krakatoa had **stirred** on 20 May 1883; at the **climax** of the **eruption**, on 26–27 August, about two-thirds of the island collapsed into the sea. **Tsunamis**, **reportedly** over 30 metres high in places, **swamped** **coastal** **settlements** on **neighbouring** Java and Sumatra. Dutch **colonial** **administrators** counted 36,417 dead, mostly **drowned**, perhaps an **underestimate**.

**Telegrams** **relayed** from Batavia along **submarine** **cables** reached London within hours, and within a day or two Western newspapers were **chronicling** what is often called the first global news event. Yet the earliest **dispatches** were **terse**; the **magnitude** of the **devastation** emerged **piecemeal** over the following days.

**Hurled** tens of kilometres high, **ash** and **sulphur** gas formed a **haze** that circled the **equator** in about two weeks. In November 1883, **crimson** sunsets over Europe and North America **lured** **firefighters** in New York and Connecticut to **phantom** **blazes**. By later estimates, global temperatures **dipped** a few tenths of a degree Celsius the following year.

In February 1884 Britain's Royal Society **advertised** in The Times for dated observations from around the world. Meanwhile, some experts **rejected** the volcanic **explanation**, blaming **moisture** at high **altitude** or **cosmic** dust. Through barometers, cables and crimson skies, one island's **ruin** helped some **far-flung** **observers** **perceive** an **interconnected** Earth.
