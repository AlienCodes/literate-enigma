# EN: The Sound That Shook the World

Shortly after ten in the morning on 27 August 1883, Krakatoa, a **volcanic** island in the Sunda **Strait**, **unleashed** its **mightiest** explosion. About four hours later, **islanders** on Rodrigues, nearly 4,800 kilometres away, took the **roar** for **distant** **gunfire**. **Barometers** worldwide recorded the **atmospheric** pressure wave, some of them seven times over about five days.

**Restless** since 20 May, the volcano reached a **catastrophic** **climax** on 26–27 August, when about two-thirds of the island **crumbled** into the sea. **Tsunamis**, **reportedly** over 30 metres high in places, **swamped** **coastal** **settlements** on Java and Sumatra. Dutch **colonial** **administrators** counted 36,417 dead, mostly **drowned**, and may have **underestimated** the toll.

**Telegrams** from Batavia, **relayed** by **submarine** **cable**, reached London within hours. Within a day or two, Western newspapers were **chronicling** what is often called the first global news event. Yet early **dispatches** were **terse**; the **magnitude** of the **devastation** emerged **piecemeal** over several days.

**Ash** and **sulphur** gas, **hurled** tens of kilometres high, formed a **haze** that circled the **equator** in about two weeks. In November 1883, **crimson** sunsets **glowed** over Europe and North America, sending **firefighters** in New York State and Connecticut to **phantom** **blazes**. Later **reconstructions** suggest global temperatures **dipped** a few tenths of a degree Celsius the following year.

In February 1884, Britain's Royal Society **advertised** in The Times for dated observations from around the world. Some experts **rejected** the volcanic **explanation** for the glow, blaming **moisture** at high **altitude** or **cosmic** dust. Through barometers, cables and crimson skies, **far-flung** **observers** began to **glimpse** how **interconnected** the planet already was.
