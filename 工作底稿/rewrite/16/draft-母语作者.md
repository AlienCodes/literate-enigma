# EN: The Sound That Shook the World

**Shortly** after ten in the morning on 27 August 1883, Krakatoa, a **volcanic** island in the Sunda **Strait**, **unleashed** its **mightiest** explosion. About four hours later, **islanders** on Rodrigues, nearly 4,800 kilometres away, took the **roar** for **distant** **gunfire**. **Barometers** worldwide recorded the **atmospheric** pressure wave, some of them seven times over about five days.

**Restless** since May, the volcano reached a **catastrophic** **climax** on 26–27 August. About two-thirds of the island **crumbled** into the sea. **Tsunamis**, **reportedly** over 30 metres high in places, **swamped** **coastal** **settlements** on Java and Sumatra. Dutch **colonial** **administrators** counted 36,417 dead, mostly **drowned**, and may have **underestimated** the toll.

In what is often called the first global news event, **dispatches** **flashed** along **submarine** **cables** to London within hours. European and North American newspapers carried **bulletins** within a day or two. Yet the first **telegrams** were **terse**; the **sheer** **magnitude** of the **devastation** emerged only days later.

**Hurled** tens of kilometres high, **ash** and **sulphur** gas formed a **haze** that circled the **equator** in about two weeks. In November 1883, **crimson** sunsets **glowed** over Europe and North America, sending **firemen** in New York State and Connecticut to **phantom** **blazes**. Modern **reconstructions** suggest global temperatures **dipped** a few tenths of a degree Celsius the following year.

In February 1884, Britain's Royal Society **advertised** in The Times for dated observations from around the world. Some experts **rejected** the volcanic **explanation** for the glow, blaming **moisture** at high **altitude** or **cosmic** dust. Through barometers, cables and **sunsets**, **attentive** **observers** **glimpsed** how **interconnected** the planet already was.
