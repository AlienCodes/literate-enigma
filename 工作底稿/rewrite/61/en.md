# EN: The Language the Children Invented
## 英文

On a **rattling** bus in Managua, deaf pupils kept up a **brisk**, **wordless** **hubbub** across the **aisle**, **deft** hands **darting**, **palms** turning and **eyebrows** **arching**.

Before the late 1970s Nicaragua had no deaf **community**. Most deaf people led **secluded** lives, sharing a **homemade** **repertoire** of gestures with parents and **siblings**. In 1977 a special-education centre opened in Managua's San Judas district with 50 deaf pupils. By 1983 it and a **vocational** school for **teenage** pupils at Villa Libertad held more than 400.

The **curriculum** **hinged** on **oral** Spanish and **lipreading**, and teachers signed only the manual **alphabet**. The **drills** **stumbled**, and most pupils failed to grasp what a Spanish word was. At **recess** and on the buses, a **rough** **jumble** of home signs and gestures **spontaneously** **ripened** into a full language **entirely** their own. The **bewildered** teachers found it **unintelligible**, and in June 1986 the Ministry of Education brought in Judy Kegl, an MIT linguist.

Researchers traced the language's **emergence**. A 2001 study found that pupils who started younger, and later **entrants**, used **grammatical** devices more often and more **uniformly** than older, earlier signers. Each fresh **intake** added **complexity**. In a 2004 study, asked to describe something rolling **downhill**, Spanish-speaking gesturers and the first signers **welded** manner and path into one **undivided** movement. Later signers **unpacked** them into **discrete** **segments** **neatly** **arrayed** in **linear** order, a **framework** found **nowhere** in the gestures around them.

For **theorists**, the case was a **fortunate** chance to watch a language's **genesis**, and evidence of an **instinct** for order. The youngest signers did more than learn it. They built the **scaffolding** their elders had lacked.
