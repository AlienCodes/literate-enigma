# 第61篇（尼加拉瓜手语）来源摘录（WebSearch，2026-10-03）
S1 Wikipedia "Nicaraguan Sign Language"; Nicaraguan Sign Language Projects: Before the late 1970s Nicaragua had no deaf community; deaf people were largely isolated, many using home-sign gestures with their families. In 1977 a centre for special education in Managua's San Judas district started with 50 deaf children; it had about 100 by 1979. In 1980 a vocational school for deaf adolescents opened in Villa Libertad, Managua. By 1983 more than 400 deaf pupils were enrolled in the two schools.
S2 Same: Teaching stressed spoken Spanish and lipreading; teachers' signing was limited to fingerspelling. It had little success; most pupils failed to grasp the concept of Spanish words. Linguistically cut off from their teachers, the children communicated in the schoolyard, on the street and on the school bus; combining gestures and their home signs, a new language rapidly emerged (first a pidgin-like system, then a full language).
S3 Same: Teachers could not understand the children's signing. In June 1986 the Ministry of Education brought in Judy Kegl, an American Sign Language linguist from MIT, to help.
S4 Senghas & Coppola 2001, Psychological Science: children who entered younger, and later cohorts, used grammatical devices (spatial modulations) more often and more consistently than older, earlier signers — the language grew more complex as each new group of young children learned it.
S5 Senghas, Kita & Özyürek 2004, Science: when describing motion (e.g. something rolling down a slope), Spanish-speaking gesturers and the earliest signers expressed manner and path together in one movement; later cohorts split them into separate, discrete signs arranged in sequence — properties of language that children imposed, though they were absent in the gestures around them.
S6 General: linguists regard it as a rare chance to watch a language being born; Nicaraguan Sign Language (Idioma de Señas de Nicaragua, ISN) is now a full language.
