# 第60篇（哈伯）来源摘录（WebSearch，2026-10-03）
S1 Knowino/Fritz Haber; BASF history; KIT: In July 1909 Fritz Haber (Karlsruhe) and his co-worker showed BASF representatives a high-pressure tabletop apparatus that synthesised ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen (about 185–200 atmospheres, roughly 500–600 °C with a catalyst); ammonia began to drip out as a liquid. BASF engineer Carl Bosch led the industrial scale-up; mass production began at the new plant in Oppau in autumn 1913. Bosch later won the 1931 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for high-pressure methods.
S2 Nobel Prize records: Haber received the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (awarded in 1919) for the synthesis of ammonia from its elements. The process was hailed as making "bread from air".
S3 Erisman et al. 2008, Nature Geoscience; Our World in Data: by 2008 nitrogen fertiliser from the Haber-Bosch process sustained about 48% of the world's population (44% in 2000); roughly half the nitrogen in the human body's proteins and DNA has passed through a Haber-Bosch factory.
S4 Smithsonian; Wikipedia (Clara Immerwahr); 1914-1918 Online: In the First World War Haber, made a captain, organised German chemical warfare. On 22 April 1915 at Ypres, Belgium, the German army released about 168 tonnes of chlorine from nearly 6,000 cylinders — the first large-scale gas attack. His wife Clara Immerwahr, the first woman to earn a chemistry doctorate in Germany (Breslau, 1900), opposed his work; on 2 May 1915, after a party in his honour, she shot herself in the garden with his service pistol. He left for the Eastern Front the next day.
S5 History sources: Ammonia also let Germany make nitrates for explosives despite the British blockade of Chilean saltpetre.
S6 New World Encyclopedia; Wikipedia: Haber was of Jewish origin; after Hitler took power he left Germany in 1933 and died in Basel, Switzerland, on 29 January 1934. His institute's work on hydrocyanic pesticides led to Zyklon; a later variant, Zyklon B, was used in Nazi death camps, where relatives of his were among the victims.
