# EN: The Bread Made from Air

In July 1909, **envoys** from BASF stood **watchful** round a **squat** tabletop machine in Fritz Haber's Karlsruhe laboratory. Behind its **bolted** **valves**, nitrogen and hydrogen met at **crushing** pressure and **furnace** heat. **Silently**, a **pungent** liquid began to **drip** from a **tube** in **beads**. **Ammonia** had been **pried** from the air.

Carl Bosch of BASF scaled up the **miracle**, and from autumn 1913 a **titanic** plant at Oppau began to **mass-produce** it. Haber won a Nobel Prize amid **applause** for making bread from air. **Ploughed** into the **soil**, its fertiliser became a **cornerstone** of **sustenance**, feeding about 48 per cent of **mankind** by 2008.

Ammonia also stocked the **arsenal**, supplying nitrates for **ordnance** and **defying** a British **blockade**. Haber, made a captain, **marshalled** chemical warfare with **zeal**. On 22 April 1915 at Ypres, soldiers **vented** about 168 tonnes of chlorine from nearly 6,000 cylinders. A **greenish**, **venomous** **fog** went **creeping** towards the **trenches**, the first large-scale gas **assault** on any **battlefield**.

His wife, Clara Immerwahr, Germany's first woman doctor of chemistry, had **openly** opposed his work. On 2 May 1915, after **festivities** in his honour, she shot herself in the garden with his service **pistol**. The next day he left for the Eastern Front.

**Reputation** offered no **shelter**. Under Hitler, **persecution** drove Haber, a Jew by origin, out of Germany in 1933, and he died in Basel months later. **Pesticide** research at his institute had produced Zyklon. A later version, Zyklon B, served the Nazi death camps, and relatives of his were among the **Holocaust**'s victims.

About half the nitrogen in our **cellular** proteins and DNA has passed through a Haber-Bosch plant. The organiser of Ypres is thus **permanently** **woven** into our **bones** and **veins**.
