# EN: The Bread Made from Air

In July 1909, visitors from BASF gathered round a **squat** tabletop **rig** in Fritz Haber's Karlsruhe laboratory. Behind its **bolted** **valves**, nitrogen and hydrogen met at **crushing** pressure and **furnace** heat. **Slowly**, a **pungent** liquid began to **drip** out in clear **beads**. It was **ammonia**, **pried** from the air itself.

Carl Bosch, a BASF engineer, **enlarged** the process, and in autumn 1913 a **gigantic** plant at Oppau began to **mass-produce** it. The **synthesis** won Haber a Nobel Prize and **applause** for making bread from air. **Ploughed** into the **soil** as fertiliser, it provided the **sustenance** of about 48 per cent of the world's people by 2008, one study estimated.

Ammonia also filled the **arsenal**. With a British **blockade** cutting off Chilean saltpetre, it supplied the nitrates for German **ordnance**. Haber, made a captain, organised chemical warfare with **zeal**. On 22 April 1915 at Ypres, in Belgium, soldiers opened nearly 6,000 cylinders, and about 168 tonnes of chlorine **hissed** out. A **greenish**, **poisonous** **fog** went **creeping** towards the enemy **trenches**. It was the first large-scale gas **assault** on any **battlefield**.

His wife, Clara Immerwahr, the first woman in Germany to earn a doctorate in chemistry, made her **objection** plain. On 2 May 1915, after a party in his honour, she shot herself in the garden with his service **pistol**. The next day he left for the Eastern Front.

**Prestige** offered no **shelter**. After Hitler took power, **persecution** drove Haber, a Jew by origin, out of Germany in 1933. He died in Basel the following January. **Pesticide** research at his institute had produced Zyklon. A later version, Zyklon B, served the Nazi death camps, and relatives of his were among the victims of that **genocide**.

About half the nitrogen in human proteins and DNA has passed through a Haber-Bosch plant. The organiser of Ypres is **incorporated** into our **bones** and our **veins**.
