# EN: The Killer Smog of 1952

On Friday 5 December 1952, cold, **stagnant** air **descended** on London. **Overhead**, warmer air pressed down like a **lid**, holding the **chill** **captive** beneath. Londoners were **accustomed** to **murky**, **dreary** winters. This time, though, the capital's smoke was trapped too, thickening into a **choking** **smog**, a mix of smoke and fog.

That smoke, from coal burned in **domestic** **fireplaces** and power stations, was **laden** with soot and **noxious** sulphur dioxide. In places, **visibility** fell to a few metres. **Ambulance** services were **suspended**, an opera at the Sadler's Wells theatre was abandoned at the **interval**, and **pedigree** **cattle** at the Smithfield **agricultural** show died or had to be **slaughtered**.

The smog **dissipated** on Tuesday 9 December. Officials at the time put the **excess** deaths at about 4,000, but a 1954 Ministry of Health report blamed later deaths on **influenza**. A study published in Environmental Health Perspectives in 2001 **reassessed** the evidence and cast doubt on that **explanation**, estimating about 12,000 excess deaths between December 1952 and February 1953.

For decades Londoners had **tolerated** the smoke as a **nuisance**. One **villain** **flickered** in their own homes, in a source of **warmth** and **comfort** so **habitual** that few thought to **accuse** it. Such **complacency** may help to explain the government's **grudging** **response**. It **withstood** pressure for an inquiry until May 1953.

Reform proved **protracted**, slowed by official **inertia**. The Clean Air Act became law on 5 July 1956, three and a half years after the smog. It **empowered** **municipal** councils to create **smokeless** zones, **combating** smoke at its **source**. The **cosy** **hearth**, **beloved** of British families, had been among the chief **offenders** all along.
