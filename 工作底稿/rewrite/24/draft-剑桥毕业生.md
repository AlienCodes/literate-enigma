# EN: The Fog That Killed a City

On Friday 5 December 1952, cold, **motionless** weather **descended** on London. Warmer air **hovered** above and **imprisoned** the cold air beneath it like a **lid**. Smoke from **countless** **chimneys** could no longer rise, so a **murky** yellow **smog**, a mix of smoke and fog, **gathered** in the **stagnant**, **chilly** air.

Coal smoke from **domestic** **grates** and power stations **choked** the streets with soot and **noxious** sulphur **dioxide**. In places, **visibility** fell to a few metres. **Ambulance** services were **suspended**. At Sadler's Wells, a performance of La Traviata was abandoned at the **interval**. Even prize **cattle** at the Smithfield Show died or had to be **slaughtered**.

The smog **dissipated** on Tuesday 9 December. **Contemporary** officials put the **excess** deaths at about 4,000, and a 1954 Ministry of Health report **blamed** later deaths on an **influenza** **outbreak**. Yet a study published in Environmental Health Perspectives in 2001 **reassessed** the **mortality** figures and estimated about 12,000 excess deaths between December 1952 and February 1953.

Fog was no **rarity** in London, whose **populace** had long **tolerated** **gloomy**, **damp** winters. The true **perpetrator** was the coal fire in every **cosy** home, an **unquestioned** **source** of **warmth** and **comfort**. Few were **inclined** to **accuse** something so **commonplace**, and the government **evaded** calls for an inquiry until May 1953.

**Reform** proved **protracted**. The Clean Air Act received royal assent on 5 July 1956, three and a half years after the smog. It **empowered** **municipal** councils to create **smokeless** zones. The family **hearth** had been the **villain** all along, **faithfully** **stoked** by the very households it harmed.
