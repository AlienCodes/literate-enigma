# EN: The Fog That Killed a City

On Friday 5 December 1952, **chilly**, **motionless** weather **descended** on London. Its people were **accustomed** to fog and **shrugged** it off. **Overhead**, however, a **blanket** of warmer air **pinned** the cold air beneath it like a **lid**. The **stagnant** air could not rise, so the smoke had no **outlet**.

Coal smoke from **domestic** **fireplaces** and power stations formed smog, a mix of smoke and fog **laden** with sulphur dioxide and **grit**. In places, **visibility** fell to a few metres. **Ambulance** services were **suspended**, an opera at Sadler's Wells was abandoned at the **interval**, and **pedigree** **cattle** at the Smithfield Show died or were **slaughtered**.

The **murky** **veil** lifted on Tuesday 9 December, when the weather changed. At the time, officials put the **excess** deaths at about 4,000. A 1954 Ministry of Health report **blamed** the deaths that followed on **influenza**. A study published in Environmental Health Perspectives in 2001 estimated about 12,000 excess deaths between December 1952 and February 1953.

The **villain**, then, was not the fog, a **notorious** **nuisance** that Londoners had long **tolerated**. It was the coal fire, the **cheerful** **companion** of every **cosy** sitting room. Such a **homely** **threat** was **awkward** to **accuse**, and the government **withstood** calls for an inquiry until May 1953. **Complacency**, not **conspiracy**, **underlay** the **delay**.

Change, when it came, **proceeded** at a **leisurely** **pace**. The Clean Air Act received royal assent on 5 July 1956, three and a half years after the smog. It **authorised** councils to create **smokeless** **zones**. The **faithful** **hearth**, **source** of so much **warmth**, had been the **assassin** all along.
