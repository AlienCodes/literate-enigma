# EN: The Fog That Killed a City

On Friday 5 December 1952, cold, **stagnant** air **descended** on London. **Overhead**, warmer air sat like a **lid**, holding the **chill** **captive** beneath. Londoners were **accustomed** to **murky** winters, and the **dreary** **blanket** seemed **commonplace**. This time, though, smoke was **imprisoned** with it, creating **smog**, a mix of smoke and fog.

That smoke, from coal burned in **domestic** **fireplaces** and power stations, was **laden** with **noxious** soot and sulphur dioxide. **Visibility** fell to a few metres in places, and **ambulance** services were **suspended**. At Sadler's Wells, La Traviata was abandoned at the **interval**. At the Smithfield Show, prize **cattle** died or had to be **slaughtered**.

The smog lifted on Tuesday 9 December, when the weather changed. **Contemporary** officials counted about 4,000 **excess** deaths. A 1954 Ministry of Health report **blamed** **ensuing** deaths on **influenza**. A study published in Environmental Health Perspectives in 2001 **reassessed** the **mortality** at about 12,000 excess deaths between December 1952 and February 1953, **triple** the first figure.

Yet fog had long been **tolerated** as a **harmless** **nuisance**. The real **villain** **flickered** in millions of **grates**, a source of **warmth** and **comfort** so **habitual** that few thought to **accuse** it. Such **inertia** may help to explain the **grudging** **response**. The government **withstood** calls for an inquiry until May 1953.

**Parliament** passed the Clean Air Act, which received royal assent on 5 July 1956, three and a half years after the smog. It **empowered** local councils to create **smokeless** zones, **combating** smoke at its **source**. The **cosy** **hearth**, **beloved** in British homes, had been the **perpetrator** all along.
