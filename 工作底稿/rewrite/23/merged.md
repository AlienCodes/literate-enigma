# EN: The Steel Box That Remade the World

On 26 April 1956 a converted Second World War **tanker**, the Ideal X, **slipped** out of Port Newark, New Jersey, for Houston. **Clamped** to her deck were 58 **containers**, **trailer** bodies **detached** from **lorries**. Few **spectators** could have seen so **unremarkable** a **cargo** as a **landmark**.

**Previously**, **freight** travelled **loose**. **Gangs** of twenty or more dockworkers carried **sacks**, **barrels** and crates piece by piece, **strenuous** **drudgery**. Ships could spend up to half their time **berthed**, and **theft** was **rampant**. In 1956 loading loose cargo cost about $5.83 a ton; the Ideal X, by the company's reckoning, about 16 cents a ton.

Yet containers were not new. The **hurdle** was **consensus**: **rival** firms' boxes had **incompatible** **dimensions** and **latches**. An international standards committee formed in 1961. In January 1963 Sea-Land, the company behind the Ideal X, let the committee use its **patents** without **royalties**, including those on corner **fittings** for **hoisting** and locking. A box could then **transfer** **smoothly** between ship, **crane**, lorry and train.

The **efficiency** came at the **expense** of **livelihoods**. Employment on New York's **waterfront** **shrank** by about three quarters between 1963 and 1976, as cargo crossed the harbour to a new **terminal** at nearby Elizabeth, **inaugurated** in August 1962. London's **historic** **docks** closed **successively** from 1967 to 1980, while Felixstowe **expanded**.

The **rewards** proved **substantial**. A study published in the Journal of International Economics in 2016 estimated the **cumulative** rise in trade between **industrialised** countries over 20 years at about 700 per cent. Roughly two thirds of seaborne trade by value now travels in containers. The gangs have given way to **tireless** cranes and **orderly**, **immense** **stacks**.
