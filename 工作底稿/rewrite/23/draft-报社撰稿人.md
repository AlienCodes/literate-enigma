# EN: The Steel Box That Remade the World

On 26 April 1956 a converted Second World War **tanker**, the Ideal X, **slipped** out of Port Newark, New Jersey, for Houston. **Clamped** to her deck sat 58 **containers**, **trailer** bodies hauled by **lorries**. Few **spectators** would have **heralded** this **clumsy**, **unremarkable** **cargo** as a **landmark**.

Cargo then travelled **loose**: **gangs** of twenty or more dockworkers carried **sacks**, **barrels** and crates piece by piece, a **primitive**, **strenuous** **drudgery**. Ships could spend as much as half their time **berthed**, and **theft** was **rampant**. In 1956 loose cargo cost about $5.83 a ton to load; the Ideal X, by the company's reckoning, cost 16 cents a ton.

Containers were not new; **consensus** was the **barrier**, as **rival** firms built a **jumble** of **dimensions** and **incompatible** **clasps**. A standards committee **convened** in 1961. In January 1963 Sea-Land, the company behind the Ideal X, **waived** **royalties** on its **patents**, including the corner **fittings** for **hoisting** and **latching** boxes. One box could pass **smoothly** between ship, **crane**, lorry and train, **undisturbed**.

The **efficiency** had a **ruinous** **downside**. Longshore work in New York **shrank** by about three quarters between 1963 and 1976, as cargo moved to a new **terminal** at Elizabeth, New Jersey, opened in August 1962. London's **antiquated** **docks** closed **successively** from 1967 to 1980, while Felixstowe **mushroomed**.

A study published in the *Journal of International Economics* in 2016 estimated that containers lifted trade between **industrialised** countries by about 700 per cent over 20 years, a **cumulative** **leap**. Roughly two thirds of ocean trade, by value, now travels in containers. The gangs gave way to **relentless** cranes and **orderly**, **colossal** **stacks**.
