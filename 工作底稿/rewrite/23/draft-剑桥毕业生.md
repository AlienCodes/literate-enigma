# EN: The Steel Box That Remade the World

On 26 April 1956, the Ideal X left Port Newark, New Jersey, for Houston. The converted Second World War **tanker** carried 58 **containers** **mounted** on deck, each the metal body of a **lorry** **trailer**. That **unassuming** **shipment** was a **momentous** **landmark**, the **dawn** of an **era** in **freight**.

**Previously**, **gangs** of twenty or more **dockers** **hoisted** **sacks**, **barrels** and crates **piece by piece**. Ships could sit **moored** for as much as half their time, and **theft** was **prevalent**. In 1956 loading **loose** **cargo** cost about $5.83 a ton; by the company's reckoning, loading the Ideal X cost about 16 cents a ton.

Yet containers were not new. **Rival** boxes had **incompatible** **dimensions** and **latches**. An international standards committee formed in 1961. In January 1963 Sea-Land, **successor** to the Ideal X venture, **granted** it **royalty**-free use of its **patents**, including the corner **fittings** for lifting and locking. Boxes could then **transfer** **directly** between ship, **crane**, lorry and **rail**.

The **efficient** system cost **livelihoods**. **Employment** on New York's **waterfront** **shrank** by about three quarters between 1963 and 1976, as cargo **relocated** across the harbour to a new **terminal** at Elizabeth, New Jersey, **inaugurated** in August 1962. London's **riverside** **docks** were **shuttered** one after another from 1967 to 1980, while Felixstowe **expanded**.

A study published in the Journal of International Economics in 2016, using data for 1962–1990, estimated that containers raised trade between **wealthy** nations by a **cumulative** 700 per cent over 20 years. **Nowadays** containers carry about two thirds of sea trade by value. London's dockers once moved such **merchandise** by **sweat** and **muscle**.
