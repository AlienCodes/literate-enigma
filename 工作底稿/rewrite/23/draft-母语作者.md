# EN: The Steel Box That Remade the World

On 26 April 1956, the Ideal X sailed from Port Newark, New Jersey, to Houston. The converted Second World War **tanker** carried 58 metal **containers** **stacked** on deck, **essentially** **detached** **trailer** bodies from **lorries**. The **seemingly** **unremarkable** trip had **enormous** **consequences** for **docks** and **dockers**.

Most **cargo** was then break-bulk, loaded piece by piece. **Gangs** of twenty or more dockworkers **manually** moved **sacks**, **barrels** and crates, and ships could spend as much as half their time **berthed**. **Theft** was **notorious**. In 1956 loading **loose** cargo cost about $5.83 a ton. By the company's reckoning, loading the Ideal X cost about 16 cents a ton.

Containers were not new. The real **hurdle** was **consensus**, as **rival** firms' boxes differed in **dimensions** and **latches**. An international standards committee was set up in 1961. In January 1963 Sea-Land, the Ideal X's **corporate** **successor**, **granted** the standards body its **patents** **royalty-free**, including corner **fittings** for **hoisting** and locking. **Compatible** boxes could then **transfer** between ship, **crane**, lorry and train.

The **savings** brought **hardship**. **Waterfront** work in New York **shrank** by about three quarters between 1963 and 1976, as **freight** **relocated** across the harbour to a new **terminal** at Elizabeth, New Jersey, opened in August 1962. London's **historic** docks closed **successively** from 1967 to 1980, while Felixstowe **expanded**.

The **rewards** were **substantial**. A study published in the *Journal of International Economics* in 2016 used 1962–1990 data. It estimated that containerisation **elevated** trade between **wealthy** nations by about 700 per cent, a **cumulative** figure, over 20 years. **Affordable** shipping brought **riches** that the **labourers** of New York and London never shared.
