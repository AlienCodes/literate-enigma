# EN: The Steel Box That Remade the World

On 26 April 1956 a converted Second World War **tanker**, the Ideal X, **slipped** out of Port Newark, New Jersey, for Houston. **Clamped** to her deck were 58 **containers**, **trailer** bodies **detached** from their wheels and **chassis**. Few **spectators** would have taken so **unremarkable** a **cargo** for a **landmark**.

At the time, **freight** travelled **loose**. **Gangs** of twenty or more dockworkers carried **sacks**, **barrels** and crates piece by piece. It was **strenuous** **drudgery**. Ships could spend up to half their time **berthed**, and **theft** was **rampant**. In 1956 loading loose cargo cost about $5.83 a ton; loading the Ideal X cost, by the company's reckoning, about 16 cents.

Containers were not new. The **hurdle** was **consensus**: **rival** firms' boxes had **incompatible** **dimensions** and **latches**. An international standards committee formed in 1961. In January 1963 Sea-Land, the Ideal X's renamed operator, let the committee use its **patents** free of **royalties**, including those on corner **fittings** for **hoisting**. Lifted by **crane**, a box could **transfer** **smoothly** between ship, **lorry** and train.

This **efficiency** came at the **expense** of **livelihoods**. New York's **waterfront** employment **shrank** by about three quarters between 1963 and 1976. Much of the cargo crossed the harbour to a new **terminal** at Elizabeth, **inaugurated** in August 1962. Most of London's **historic** **docks** closed one after another from 1967 to 1980, while Felixstowe, on the Suffolk coast, **expanded**.

The **rewards** were **substantial**. A study published in the Journal of International Economics in 2016 estimated that containerisation had lifted trade between **industrialised** countries by a **cumulative** 700 per cent over 20 years. **Tireless** cranes now lift identical boxes into **orderly**, **immense** **stacks**.
