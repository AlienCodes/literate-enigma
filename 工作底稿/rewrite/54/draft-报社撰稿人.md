# EN: The Joke That Became OK

On 7 February 1839, the Boston Morning Post printed O.W., meaning 'oll wright'. The **misspelling** was **deliberate**. Since 1838 a **vogue** for **abbreviations** had gripped the city's papers, and the **craze** had turned **comic**: K.Y. meant 'know yuse'. Such **fashionable** **humour** let writers adopt the **persona** of an **uneducated** country bumpkin.

On 23 March 1839, the Post took a **tongue-in-cheek** **swipe** at a **contemporary**, the Providence Journal. The **presumed** author was the editor, Charles Gordon Greene. The **satire** concerned a **mock** society **parodying** a city **ordinance** against dinner-time **bell-ringing**. In it, 'o.k.' was **glossed** as 'all correct'. The letters stood for 'oll korrect'.

The **incumbent** Democratic president, Martin Van Buren, was nicknamed 'Old Kinderhook' after his **birthplace**. In 1840, as he sought **re-election**, his **partisans** in New York formed the Democratic O.K. Club, and O.K., a **double-meaning** **slogan**, spread nationally. In November his **challenger**, William Henry Harrison, **vanquished** him in an **electoral** **landslide**, 234 votes to 60.

Meanwhile, on 30 March 1840, the New York Morning Herald **concocted** a **yarn**. The former president Andrew Jackson, it claimed, was a **dreadful** speller who marked **paperwork** O.K., for 'Ole Kurrek'. The **smear** was **groundless**, as Jackson spelled **reasonably** well. Within about twenty years, however, people adopted the **fictional** **habit** **in earnest**.

OK, reputedly the planet's most spoken or typed word, **attracted** **exotic** **pedigrees**, such as Choctaw 'okeh' and Greek 'ola kala'. Columbia's Allen Walker Read **excavated** its Boston **genesis** from **obscurity** in six **definitive** articles for American Speech in 1963–64. **Authoritative** American dictionaries that had **upheld** the Choctaw **derivation** as late as 1961 now largely print 'oll korrect' instead.
