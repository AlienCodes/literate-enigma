# EN: The Joke That Became OK

OK, often called the planet's most frequently spoken word, began as a newspaper **gag**. From 1838 Boston's papers **embraced** a **vogue** for **abbreviations**, which turned **comic** through **deliberate** **misspellings**: O.W. for 'oll wright', K.Y. for 'know yuse'. The **phonetic** spellings let writers adopt the **persona** of an **uneducated** bumpkin.

On 23 March 1839 the Boston Morning Post printed the first known o.k., in a **satire** **taunting** the Providence Journal. **Glossed** as 'all correct', the letters **encoded** a **knowingly** **illiterate** 'oll korrect'. The piece concerned Boston's **mock** Anti-Bell-Ringing Society, which **parodied** an **ordinance** against ringing dinner bells. Its **presumed** author was the editor, Charles Gordon Greene.

**Partisan** politics then **propagated** the abbreviation nationwide. Martin Van Buren, the **incumbent** president, was nicknamed 'Old Kinderhook' after his **birthplace**. When he sought **re-election** in 1840, his New York **supporters** formed the Democratic O.K. Club, making the letters a **double-meaning** **slogan**. He lost that November by an **electoral** **landslide**, 60 votes to 234.

On 30 March 1840 the New York Morning Herald **concocted** a **red herring**, a **yarn** in which Andrew Jackson, **supposedly** an **inept** speller, marked papers O.K. for 'Ole Kurrek'. It was **bogus**: Jackson spelled **adequately** and never marked documents OK. Within about twenty years, **curiously**, people were marking OK on **paperwork** **in earnest**.

For over a century **exotic** **pedigrees** like Greek ola kala and Choctaw okeh **consigned** OK's Boston **provenance** to **obscurity**. The linguist Allen Walker Read **rigorously** sifted **archival** newspapers, **substantiating** Boston's **paternity** in six articles in American Speech in 1963–64. **Authoritative** dictionaries that had **upheld** Choctaw until 1961 now derive the word from 'oll korrect'.
