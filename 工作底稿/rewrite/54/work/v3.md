# EN: The Joke That Became OK

OK is reputedly the most spoken or typed word on Earth. In 1838 **abbreviations** came into **vogue** in Boston's newspapers, and the **craze** turned **comic**. Writers **condensed** **deliberate** **misspellings** into initials. O.W. meant 'oll wright', or all right. The **humour** lay in adopting the **persona** of an **uneducated** country bumpkin.

On 23 March 1839 the Boston Morning Post printed 'o.k.', **glossed** as 'all correct'. The letters **hinted** at a **phonetic** spelling, 'oll korrect'. The **tongue-in-cheek** piece **teased** the Providence Journal about Boston's **sham** Anti-Bell-Ringing Society, which **mocked** an **ordinance** **outlawing** dinner-bell ringing. The likely **satirist** was the Post's editor, Charles Gordon Greene.

Martin Van Buren, the **incumbent** president, was nicknamed Old Kinderhook after his **birthplace**. **Supporters** of his **candidacy** for **re-election** formed the Democratic O.K. Club in 1840. Its name, a **slogan** with a **dual** meaning, spread nationally. At the **polls** that November he was **unseated**, 60 **electoral** votes to 234. The letters fared better.

On 30 March 1840 the New York Morning Herald printed a **bogus** **yarn**: Van Buren's predecessor, Andrew Jackson, a **supposedly** **hopeless** speller, had marked papers O.K., for 'Ole Kurrek'. Jackson spelt **reasonably** well and never marked documents OK. Yet the **fabricated** story **anticipated** a **clerical** **habit**. Within about twenty years people were marking OK on **paperwork**.

**Nobler** **pedigrees** circulated, from Greek 'ola kala' to the **indigenous** Choctaw 'okeh'. **Authoritative** American dictionaries **plumped** for Choctaw as late as 1961. In 1963–64 the linguist Allen Walker Read **cracked** the case in six **definitive** articles for the **periodical** American Speech. The **dreadful** speller behind the letters was a Boston newspaper in **disguise**.
