# EN: The Joke That Became OK

OK is reputedly the most spoken or typed word on Earth. In 1838 **abbreviations** came into **vogue** in Boston's newspapers, and the **craze** soon took a **comic** turn. Writers **condensed** **deliberate** **misspellings** into initials. O.W. meant 'oll wright', or all right. The **humour** lay in the spelling, which let them adopt the **persona** of an **uneducated** country bumpkin.

On 23 March 1839 the Boston Morning Post printed 'o.k.', **glossed** as 'all correct'. The letters **hinted** at a **phonetic** 'oll korrect'. They appeared in a **tongue-in-cheek** **swipe** at the Providence Journal about Boston's Anti-Bell-Ringing Society, a **sham** body that **mocked** a city **ordinance** against ringing dinner bells. The likely **satirist** was the editor, Charles Gordon Greene.

Martin Van Buren, the **incumbent** president, was nicknamed Old Kinderhook after his **birthplace**. To promote his **candidacy** for **re-election** in 1840, **supporters** formed the Democratic O.K. Club. Its name, a **slogan** with a **dual** meaning, spread nationally. At the **polls** that November he was **unseated**, by 60 **electoral** votes to 234. The letters fared better.

On 30 March 1840 the New York Morning Herald printed a **bogus** **yarn**: Van Buren's predecessor, Andrew Jackson, a **supposedly** **hopeless** speller, had marked papers O.K., for 'Ole Kurrek'. Jackson spelt **reasonably** well and never marked documents OK. Yet the **fabricated** story **anticipated** a real **clerical** **habit**. Within about twenty years people were marking OK on **paperwork**.

**Nobler** **pedigrees** circulated, from Greek 'ola kala' to Choctaw 'okeh'. **Authoritative** American dictionaries **plumped** for Choctaw as late as 1961. In 1963 and 1964 Allen Walker Read, a Columbia linguist, **cracked** the case in six **definitive** articles for the **periodical** American Speech. The **dreadful** speller behind the letters turned out to be a Boston newspaper in **disguise**.
