# EN: The Joke That Became OK

OK is said to be the planet's most frequently spoken or typed word. It began as a **gag**. From 1838, **abbreviations** were the **vogue** in Boston's newspapers, and the **craze** turned **comic**, with **deliberate** **misspellings**: O.W. for 'oll wright', K.Y. for 'know yuse'. The **humour** lay in the **persona** of an **uneducated** country bumpkin.

On 23 March 1839 a Boston Morning Post **satire** took a **swipe** at the Providence Journal over Boston's **mock** Anti-Bell-Ringing Society. That society **poked fun at** a city **ordinance** **outlawing** dinner **bell-ringing**. In passing, the Post printed 'o.k.' with a **straight-faced** **gloss**: 'all correct'. The **presumed** writer was the editor, Charles Gordon Greene.

In 1840 Martin Van Buren, the **incumbent** president, sought **re-election**. He was nicknamed 'Old Kinderhook' after his **birthplace**. His **supporters** in New York formed the Democratic O.K. Club, and O.K. spread nationally as a **double-meaning** **slogan**. Neither meaning **swayed** enough **voters**. That November William Henry Harrison **unseated** him in a **landslide**, 234 **electoral** votes to 60.

On 30 March 1840 the New York Morning Herald printed a **fabricated** **smear**: the **ex-president** Andrew Jackson, a **supposedly** **hopeless** speller, had marked **paperwork** O.K., for 'Ole Kurrek'. Jackson spelled **reasonably** well and never marked documents OK. Within about twenty years people really did. The **bogus** **habit** was merely **premature**.

**Nobler** **pedigrees** **abounded**: Choctaw 'okeh', Greek 'ola kala'. **Authoritative** American dictionaries **upheld** the Choctaw case as late as 1961. In 1963–64 Allen Walker Read of Columbia University **excavated** the **neglected** Boston story in six **conclusive** articles for American Speech. Most dictionaries now **embrace** his **derivation**, a pedigree of bad spelling and dinner bells.
