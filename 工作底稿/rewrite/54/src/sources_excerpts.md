# 第54篇 来源摘录（两轮 WebSearch 核查 + 选题核查员，2026-10-02）
S1 History.com (Klein, 2014); Wikipedia "OK"; wordorigins.org: from 1838 Boston newspapers ran a vogue for abbreviations ("a remarkable vogue", Read), which moved on to comic, deliberately misspelled ones — O.W. ("oll wright"), K.Y. ("know yuse"), N.G. ("no go"), N.C. ("nuff ced"). O.W. appeared in the Boston Morning Post on 7 February 1839. Deliberate misspellings let writers adopt the persona of an uneducated country bumpkin.
S2 Merriam-Webster; Smithsonian; History.com: on 23 March 1839 the Boston Morning Post printed "o.k." glossed as "all correct", in a satirical piece aimed at the Providence Journal about Boston's mock "Anti-Bell-Ringing Society" (formed 1838 to satirise a city ordinance against ringing dinner bells). The likely writer was Charles Gordon Greene, the paper's founder and editor.
S3 Merriam-Webster; etymonline; NPR (2006); Wikipedia 1840 election: Martin Van Buren, the Democratic president, was nicknamed "Old Kinderhook" after his birthplace, Kinderhook, New York. In 1840, running for re-election, his supporters in New York formed the Democratic O.K. Club; O.K. became a campaign slogan with a double meaning and spread nationally. Van Buren lost in November 1840 to William Henry Harrison, 234 electoral votes to 60.
S4 Wikipedia "OK"; worldwidewords; Metcalf (OUP 2010): on 30 March 1840 the New York Morning Herald printed a story that Andrew Jackson, a supposedly terrible speller, had marked papers "O.K." for "Ole Kurrek". It was a hoax: Jackson spelled reasonably well and never marked documents OK. Yet within about twenty years people really did begin marking OK on documents.
S5 Wikipedia "OK" / "List of proposed etymologies of OK"; Chronicle of Higher Education; Smithsonian: rival origin stories included the Choctaw "okeh" (cited as the probable origin by Webster's and other major American dictionaries until as late as 1961) and Greek "ola kala". Allen Walker Read of Columbia University published six articles in the journal American Speech in 1963–64 tracing OK to Boston; his etymology silenced most scepticism and is now given in most dictionaries.
S6 NPR (20 Nov 2010) on Allan Metcalf, "OK: The Improbable Story of America's Greatest Word" (OUP 2010): OK is said to be the most frequently spoken or typed word on the planet (hedge required).
S2b wordorigins.org / History.com: OK made its first (known) appearance in print in the Boston Morning Post on 23 March 1839; Charles Gordon Greene, founder and editor, was among the most prolific users of these initialisms. Merriam-Webster: o.k. = abbreviation of 'oll korrect', a facetious misspelling of 'all correct'.
S4b Britannica: Andrew Jackson was US president 1829–37 (so "former president" in 1840).
S5b worldwidewords / Chronicle: Read's 1963–64 papers drew on extensive period newspaper citations ("archival newspapers").
S5c Wikipedia "List of proposed etymologies of OK": Choctaw 'okeh' glossed as "it is so"; Greek 'ola kala' as "all is good" (glosses for the Chinese notes only).
