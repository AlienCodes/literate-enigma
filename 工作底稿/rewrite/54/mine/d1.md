# EN: The Joke That Became OK

OK is said to be the planet's most frequently spoken or typed word. It began as a **gag**. From 1838, Boston's newspapers had a **vogue** for **abbreviations**, and the **craze** turned **comic** with **deliberate** **misspellings**: O.W. for 'oll wright', K.Y. for 'know yuse'. The **humour** lay in adopting the **persona** of an **uneducated** country bumpkin.

On 23 March 1839 the Boston Morning Post printed 'o.k.' and **glossed** it, **straight-faced**, as 'all correct'. It came in an **editorial** **swipe** at the Providence Journal about Boston's **mock** Anti-Bell-Ringing Society. That society, formed in 1838, set out to **poke fun at** a city **ordinance** that **outlawed** ringing dinner bells. The **presumed** writer was the editor, Charles Gordon Greene.

In 1840 Martin Van Buren, the **incumbent** president, sought **re-election**. He was nicknamed 'Old Kinderhook' after his **birthplace**, Kinderhook. His **supporters** in New York formed the Democratic O.K. Club, and O.K. spread nationally as a **double-meaning** **slogan**. Neither meaning **swayed** enough **voters**. That November William Henry Harrison **unseated** him, 234 **electoral** votes to 60.

On 30 March 1840 the New York Morning Herald printed a **fabricated** **smear**. In it Andrew Jackson, a **supposedly** **hopeless** speller, **approved** **paperwork** by marking it O.K., for 'Ole Kurrek'. Jackson spelled **reasonably** well and never marked documents OK. Within about twenty years people really were marking OK on documents. The **bogus** **habit** was merely **premature**.

**Exotic** **derivations** **abounded**, from Choctaw 'okeh' to Greek 'ola kala'. **Authoritative** American dictionaries **upheld** the Choctaw one as late as 1961. In 1963–64 Allen Walker Read **excavated** the Boston story in six **conclusive** articles for American Speech. Most dictionaries now **embrace** his account, which gives the word a **pedigree** of bad spelling and dinner bells.
