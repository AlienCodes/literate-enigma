# EN: The Prize That Looked Away

On 29 May 1919, as a total **eclipse** **obscured** the sun, two British **expeditions** photographed stars in its **vicinity**. Arthur Eddington **observed** from Príncipe, an island off West Africa; Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson from Sobral, in **north-eastern** Brazil. They were testing Einstein's **bold** **forecast** that the sun's **gravity** would **deflect** passing starlight.

On 6 November, at a **joint** meeting of Britain's Royal Society and Royal Astronomical Society in London, the **findings** were **hailed** as **consistent** with that forecast. Next day The Times **proclaimed** a "Revolution in Science"; on 10 November a New York Times **headline** read "Lights All Askew in the Heavens". **Overnight**, an **eminent** **physicist** became a **global** **celebrity**.

Stockholm proved harder to **dazzle**. The Nobel physics committee valued **experimental** **confirmation**, and Allvar Gullstrand, a Nobel-winning eye **specialist** on the committee, judged the **empirical** support for relativity **insufficient**. In 1921 no **nomination** **fulfilled** Nobel's **criteria**, so, as the **statutes** **permit**, the prize was **reserved** for a year.

On 9 November 1922 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences **conferred** that prize on Einstein, **citing** **chiefly** "his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect". That law, **governing** how light **ejects** **electrons** from metal, would help **underpin** **quantum** theory. A letter the next day made clear that relativity had not been **evaluated**. Einstein, **aboard** a **steamer** **bound** for Japan, missed the December **ceremony**.

On 11 July 1923, in Gothenburg, a Swedish city, he **delivered** his Nobel **lecture**. He **devoted** it to the **fundamental** ideas and problems of relativity. The theory the committee had looked past reached a Nobel **podium** after all, carried there by the man the prize had **honoured**.
