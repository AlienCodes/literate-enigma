# EN: The Prize That Looked Away

On 29 May 1919, during a total **eclipse**, two British **expeditions** turned their **telescopes** on stars beside the **obscured** sun. Arthur Eddington **observed** from Príncipe, an island off West Africa; Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson from **inland** Sobral, in **north-eastern** Brazil. They aimed to **verify** Einstein's **bold** **forecast** that the sun's **gravity** would **bend** passing light.

On 6 November, at a **joint** **gathering** in London, Britain's Royal Society and Royal Astronomical Society **hailed** the **findings** as **consistent** with that forecast. The next day The Times **proclaimed** a "Revolution in Science". **Overnight**, a **physicist** **renowned** **chiefly** within his **discipline** became a **global** **celebrity**.

Stockholm proved harder to **dazzle**. The Nobel physics committee valued **experimental** **confirmation**, and one **prominent** member, the eye **specialist** Allvar Gullstrand, judged the **empirical** support for relativity **insufficient**. In 1921 no **nomination** **fulfilled** Nobel's **criteria**, so, under a **provision** of the **statutes**, the prize was **reserved** for a year.

On 9 November 1922 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences **conferred** that prize on Einstein, citing in particular "his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect". That law, describing how light **ejects** **electrons** from metal, would **subsequently** help **underpin** **quantum** theory. A letter the next day **specified** that relativity had not been judged. Einstein was **aboard** a **steamer** **bound** for Japan and missed the December **ceremony**.

On 11 July 1923, in Gothenburg, Sweden, he finally **delivered** his Nobel **lecture**. He **devoted** it to the **fundamental** ideas and problems of relativity. The theory the committee had spent two years looking past had **ultimately** reached a Nobel **podium**, carried there by the man the prize had **honoured**.
