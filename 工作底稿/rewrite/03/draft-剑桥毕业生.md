# EN: The Prize That Looked Away

On 29 May 1919, as a total **eclipse** **obscured** the sun, two British **expeditions** photographed nearby stars. Arthur Eddington **observed** from Príncipe, an island off West Africa; Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson from Sobral, in Brazil. The **astronomers** were **probing** Einstein's **bold** **hypothesis**: that the sun's **gravity** would **deflect** light from **distant** stars.

On 6 November, at a **joint** meeting of Britain's Royal Society and Royal Astronomical Society, the **findings** were **hailed** as **confirmation** of his theory. The Times of London **proclaimed** a "Revolution in Science"; a New York Times **headline** of 10 November read "Lights All Askew in the Heavens". **Overnight**, an **eminent** **physicist** became a **global** **celebrity**.

The Nobel committee proved harder to **persuade**. It wanted **rigorous** **experimental** **verification**, and Allvar Gullstrand, a Nobel-winning eye specialist on the committee, **maintained** that relativity remained **unproven**. Finding no 1921 **nomination** that **fulfilled** Nobel's **criteria**, the committee, as its **statutes** **permit**, **deferred** that year's prize.

On 9 November 1922 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences **conferred** that prize on Einstein, **citing** **chiefly** his discovery of "the law of the photoelectric effect". The law, which **governs** how light **ejects** **electrons** from metal, would help **underpin** **quantum** theory. A letter the next day made it **explicit** that relativity had been **excluded** from **consideration**. Einstein, then **en route** to Japan, missed the December **ceremony**.

On 11 July 1923, in Gothenburg, a **coastal** Swedish city, he **delivered** his Nobel **lecture** to an **assembly** of Scandinavian **naturalists**. His subject was not light but relativity. The theory the committee had **deliberately** looked past found a Nobel **podium** after all, **courtesy of** the prize's own **recipient**.
