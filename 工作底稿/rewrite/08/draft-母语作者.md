# EN: Saving the Sky

A thin **layer** of **ozone** **shields** the **globe** from much of the sun's **ultraviolet** **radiation**. In June 1974 Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland, chemists at the University of California, Irvine, warned in Nature of a **menace** in fridges and **spray** cans. Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), **inert** enough to reach the stratosphere, could there **liberate** **chlorine**, a **catalyst** that destroys many ozone **molecules**. The pair foresaw **gradual** **depletion**.

In May 1985 Joseph Farman and colleagues at the British Antarctic Survey reported something more **alarming**. At Halley Bay, an Antarctic research station, a long-running, ground-based Dobson spectrophotometer showed ozone **plummeting** each spring in the southern **hemisphere**. Behind it lay a **humble**, **painstaking** **routine** of **manual** **observation**.

Yet NASA's Nimbus-7 **satellite**, **orbiting** with **sophisticated** **sensors**, had recorded the same lows. Its software flagged readings that low as **dubious**, since none had been reliably measured before, and NASA was still **verifying** them when the British published. The **weakness** lay in **expectation**, not **hardware**.

**Remarkably**, **diplomats** listened: in September 1987 they signed the Montreal **Protocol**, a **binding** **accord** to **curb** CFCs. **Amendments** **tightened** it. In London in June 1990 **wealthy** countries **pledged** to **phase** out CFC **manufacture** by 2000. In September 2009 the protocol and the Vienna Convention became the first treaties in UN history to win **universal** ratification.

In January 2023 a UN-backed **assessment** **forecast** **recovery** to 1980 levels around 2066 over Antarctica, 2045 over the Arctic and 2040 elsewhere. Though the sky will take decades to **heal**, its **rescue** began with **diligent** **observers** who refused to write off a **persistent**, **sobering** number as a **glitch**.
