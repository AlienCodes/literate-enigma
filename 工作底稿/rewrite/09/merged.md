# EN: Apollo 13: The Successful Failure

On April 11th 1970 NASA, America's space agency, launched Apollo 13, meant to make the third crewed **lunar** landing. On April 13th, some 200,000 miles out, an **oxygen** tank **exploded** in the service **module**, the ship's store of oxygen and power. The **blast** **crippled** the **spacecraft**; the landing was **aborted**.

The three **astronauts** took **refuge** in the lunar module, Aquarius, built for two men and about two days. Its engine **steered** the **craft** onto a **trajectory** around the Moon, whose gravity swung it home. The crew **rationed** water, **conserved** power and **shivered** in a **bitterly** cold **cabin**.

Then a quieter **hazard** **emerged**: **carbon dioxide** was **accumulating**. The command module, the crew's **capsule**, had square **filter** **cartridges**; Aquarius's **sockets** were round. Engineers on the ground **devised** an **adapter** from plastic bags, **cardboard** and tape on board, and talked the crew through **assembling** it. On April 17th the capsule **splashed** down in the Pacific.

Yet the **culprit** lay on the ground. In June 1970 a NASA review board **traced** the **explosion** to the tank's 28-**volt** **thermostat** switches. In 1965 a new **specification** let the tank's **heaters** run on 65-volt ground power, but nobody **upgraded** the switches; neither the **contractor** nor NASA **detected** the **mismatch**. Weeks before launch, a heater ran for about eight hours to **drain** the tank. The switches probably **malfunctioned**, **scorching** the **insulation** on **wiring** that **ignited** in flight.

NASA came to call Apollo 13 a "successful failure". The **paradox** fits a flight lost to a **paperwork** **oversight** five years before launch and saved within four days by **ingenuity** on the ground.
