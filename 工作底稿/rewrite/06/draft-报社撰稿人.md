# EN: The Myth of the Unsinkable

On 10 April 1912 the Titanic, a **lavish** **liner** of **plush** **cabins**, left Southampton for New York on her **maiden** voyage, **dubbed** "practically unsinkable" by a shipbuilding magazine. At about 11.40pm on 14 April she **collided** with an **iceberg**. By about 2.20am she had **submerged**, and the **catastrophe**'s **toll** would pass 1,500.

The **paradox** is that she was **compliant**. Since 1894 the Board of Trade, the British department that **oversaw** **merchant** shipping, had **stipulated** lifeboats by **gross** tonnage, its **scale** stopping at 16 for ships of 10,000 tons or more. The **roughly** 46,000-ton Titanic carried 20, **exceeding** an **obsolete** **requirement**. Yet at full **capacity** those boats could **accommodate** 1,178, **barely** half the 2,200 or so aboard.

Nearby lay a smaller ship, the Californian. Her **sole** **wireless** **operator**, Cyril Evans, worked for Marconi, which ran most ships' radio rooms. At about 11.30pm he switched off and went to bed; the Titanic's **frantic** **distress** calls fell on **idle** **headphones**. **Hindsight** has branded him **negligent**, yet no rule required a night watch.

American **senators** heard **testimony** from **survivors** in April and May 1912; Lord Mersey, a British judge, **presided** over an **investigation** from May to July. That August, American **legislation** **mandated** a **round-the-clock** radio watch. On 20 January 1914, **delegates** from 13 countries signed the International **Convention** for the Safety of Life at Sea in London, **pledging** lifeboats for all and an ice **patrol**.

At last the rules counted people, not tons. **Folklore** blames a **boast**. The **doomed** liner sank in under three hours; the **comforting** **illusion** that **compliance** meant safety took 21 months to go down.
