# EN: The Myth of the Unsinkable

On 10 April 1912 the Titanic, an **opulent** **liner**, left Southampton for New York on her **maiden** voyage. A shipbuilding journal had **dubbed** her "practically unsinkable". At about 11.40pm on 14 April she **collided** with an **iceberg**; by about 2.20am on 15 April she had **vanished**, and the death **toll** **surpassed** 1,500.

**Arrogance** is the usual **culprit**. In fact the Titanic **exceeded** the legal **requirement**. An 1894 **scale** from the Board of Trade, Britain's **maritime** **regulator**, **pegged** lifeboat numbers to tonnage; its top band, 10,000 tons and **upwards**, required 16 boats. At about 46,000 tons she **boasted** 20, with **capacity** for 1,178: just over half of roughly 2,200 aboard. **Compliant**, yet **inadequate**.

Cyril Evans, **sole** **wireless** **operator** on the nearby steamer Californian, was a Marconi **employee**, as most ships' operators were. Around 11.30pm he switched off his **apparatus** and went to bed. No rule **stipulated** that anyone stay **vigilant** overnight; this was no **negligence**. But the Titanic's **frantic** **distress** calls never reached him.

In August 1912 US **legislation** **obliged** ships to keep **round-the-clock** radio watch. On 20 January 1914, in London, **delegates** of 13 countries signed the first International **Convention** for the Safety of Life at Sea. It **mandated** lifeboats for all, **continuous** radio watch and an ice **patrol**. The **outbreak** of war left the **treaty** **dormant**; the patrol began **regardless**.

In **hindsight**, the **paradox** is **stark**. The liner sank in under three hours; the **outdated** rules that let her sail with seats for half those aboard had stayed **afloat** for 18 years. Sinking the **conviction** that **compliance** **guaranteed** safety took some 1,500 deaths.
