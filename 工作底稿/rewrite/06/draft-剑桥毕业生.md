# EN: The Myth of the Unsinkable

On 10 April 1912 the **opulent** **liner** Titanic left Southampton for New York on her **maiden** voyage. At about 11.40pm on 14 April she **collided** with an **iceberg**, and by about 2.20am she had **vanished**. The death **toll** **surpassed** 1,500. **Folklore** blames the **catastrophe** on **arrogance**: a shipbuilding **periodical** had **dubbed** her "practically unsinkable".

Yet the Titanic was **compliant**. In 1894 the Board of Trade, which **regulated** British shipping, had **pegged** lifeboat **quotas** to **gross** tonnage, on a **scale** topping out at 16 boats above 10,000 tons. At about 46,000 tons, she carried 20, **exceeding** the **requirement**. They could **accommodate** 1,178, about half the 2,200 aboard: **sufficient** in law, **inadequate** at sea.

The silence nearby was equally **lawful**. The Californian, a steamer **stationary** in the ice, had one **wireless** **operator**, Cyril Evans, on the Marconi company's **payroll**. Around 11.30pm he switched off and went to bed. **Hindsight** calls him **negligent**, yet no rule **obliged** him to listen overnight, so the Titanic's **distress** calls went **unheard** there.

From April to July 1912 a US Senate **investigation** and a British inquiry under Lord Mersey, a judge, heard **testimony**. In August Congress made a **round-the-clock** radio watch **compulsory**. In London on 20 January 1914, **delegates** of 13 countries signed the first Safety of Life at Sea **convention**, **mandating** lifeboats for all, **uninterrupted** radio watch and an ice **patrol**. The First World War **stalled** the **treaty**, not the patrol.

Rules can **lag** behind the ships they **oversee**. The Titanic sank in under three hours; the **comforting** **premise** that **obsolete** **provisions** **guaranteed** safety outlasted her by nearly two years.
