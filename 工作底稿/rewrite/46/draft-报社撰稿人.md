# EN: The Horse That Could Count

In Berlin, von Osten, a **mathematics** teacher and **trainer**, owned a **stallion** called Hans. The animal answered with its hoof, one blow per unit. Sums, **subtraction** and dates seemed easy to **calculate**. **Admirers** were **stunned** by such **intelligent** **demonstrations**, and the **brilliance** inspired **awe**. The New York Times reported on Hans in 1904.

A **commission** of 13 members met to **investigate**. It included a **veterinarian**, a **circus** manager, a **cavalry** officer, several schoolteachers and the director of the Berlin **zoological** garden. Members looked for **trickery**, **fraud**, **deception** or **collusion**. In September 1904 they reported none. The horse had, in effect, been **certified** as **authentic**.

In 1907 the **psychologist** Pfungst worked **systematically**. He isolated horse and **questioner**, swapped askers, fitted **blinders** and used people ignorant of the answer, changing one **variable** each time. **Accuracy** hinged on sight. Hans replied **correctly** 50 times in 56 to a visible, knowing questioner. Facing an **invisible** one, he answered **incorrectly** almost always, a **ratio** of 2 right in 35.

The **cues** came from the askers. Near the answer, **tension** grew. At the right tap it eased, and the questioner **straightened** or gave a small upward **jerk** of the head. Each **motion** was **unintentional** and **subconscious**, and von Osten noticed no **gesture**. Nobody meant to **deceive**. The owner was **sincere**, and the signals came **innocently**.

The Clever Hans effect, a **phenomenon** of **expectancy** and **bias**, shaped **behavioural** **psychology**, animal **cognition** and **double-blind** designs. After von Osten died in June 1909, Hans passed to an Elberfeld **jeweller** convinced horses could reason. Pfungst had already played the animal, **sensitive** to the cues of **attentive** people.
