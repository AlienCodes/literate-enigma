# EN: The Horse That Could Count

Wilhelm von Osten, a **mathematics** teacher and **trainer** in Berlin, owned a **stallion**, Hans. Asked to **calculate** a sum, attempt **subtraction** or name a date, Hans tapped out the answer with a hoof. Such **aptitude** suggested **brilliance**, and public **demonstrations** made him seem **intelligent**. The New York Times reported the **stunning** act in 1904.

A **commission** of thirteen was formed to **investigate**. It included a **veterinarian**, a **circus** manager, a **cavalry** officer, several schoolteachers and the director of the Berlin **zoological** garden. Its members looked for **trickery**, **fraud** and **collusion**. In September 1904 they found no **deception**, in effect **certifying** the act as **authentic**.

In 1907 the **psychologist** Oskar Pfungst tested the act **systematically**, altering one **variable** after another. He kept onlookers away, swapped **questioners**, fitted **blinders** and sometimes used askers **ignorant** of the answer. When Hans could see the questioner, he answered **correctly** 50 times in 56. With that person **invisible**, his **accuracy** fell to a **ratio** of two in 35.

The **cues** came from the questioners. Each would **lean** forward as tapping began, and **tension** rose as the taps neared the answer. At the right tap it eased, and the asker would **straighten** or **jerk** the head upward. Every **gesture** and **motion** was **subconscious**, made **innocently** by people who had not set out to **deceive**.

The Clever Hans effect, in which **expectancy** shapes **behavioural** results, is a **phenomenon** of **bias** that **double-blind** designs in **psychology** and animal **cognition** guard against. Hans later passed to a **jeweller** convinced horses could reason. In the laboratory, Pfungst took the animal's place and proved equally **sensitive** to the cues of **attentive** volunteers.
