# EN: The Horse That Could Count

Wilhelm von Osten, a **mathematics** teacher and amateur **trainer** in Berlin, owned a **stallion**, Hans. Asked to **calculate** a sum, give a date or attempt **subtraction**, the seemingly **intelligent** animal **rapped** out answers with a hoof. Such **calculating** **brilliance** won **admirers** at every **demonstration**, and The New York Times reported on Hans in 1904.

A **commission** of thirteen was formed to **investigate**. It comprised a **veterinarian**, a **circus** manager, a **cavalry** officer, several schoolteachers and the director of the Berlin **zoological** garden. It was to judge whether the act was **authentic** or a **fraud** resting on **deception** or **collusion**. In September 1904 it **certified** the act free of **trickery**.

In 1907 the **psychologist** Oskar Pfungst tested the act **systematically**. He separated Hans and the **questioner** from onlookers, used other **questioners**, fitted **blinders** and changed each **variable**, even the asker's knowledge of the answer. Seeing the asker, Hans answered **correctly** 50 times in 56. With the asker **invisible**, **accuracy** fell to a **ratio** of two in 35.

As the taps neared the answer, each **questioner** showed rising **tension**. At the correct tap the asker relaxed and would **straighten** or give a slight upward **jerk** of the head. These **cues** were involuntary, and von Osten, a **sincere** man, did not **deceive** anyone. He made every **gesture** **innocently**, unaware of each **motion**.

The Clever Hans effect is a **phenomenon** where **expectancy** shapes **behavioural** responses, a **bias** behind **double-blind** designs in **psychology**. After von Osten died in June 1909, Hans passed to an Elberfeld **jeweller** convinced horses could reason. Playing the animal, Pfungst had read **inadvertently** offered **cues** from **attentive** volunteers.
