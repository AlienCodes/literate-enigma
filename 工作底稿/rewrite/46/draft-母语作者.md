# EN: The Horse That Could Count

Von Osten taught **mathematics** in Berlin and was an amateur **trainer**. His **stallion**, Hans, would **calculate** in **demonstrations**, tapping out sums, dates and more with a hoof. Such **calculating** suggested an **intelligent** animal of real **proficiency**, and the **stunning** displays won **admirers**. In 1904 the New York Times reported on his **brilliance**.

A **commission** of thirteen was formed to **investigate**. It included a **veterinarian**, a **circus** manager, a **cavalry** officer, several schoolteachers and the director of the Berlin **zoological** garden. Its **assessors** looked for **trickery**, **fraud** and **collusion**. In September 1904 they concluded that no **deception** was involved, in effect **certifying** the act as **authentic**.

In 1907 the **psychologist** Oskar Pfungst tested Hans **systematically**, changing one **variable** at a time. He removed onlookers, used other **questioners**, fitted **blinders** and sometimes chose a **questioner** ignorant of the answer. In sight, the **ratio** was 50 of 56. With the questioner **invisible**, Hans's **accuracy** fell to 2 of 35, and the rest came out **incorrectly**.

The **cues** lay with the questioners. As the taps neared the answer, their **tension** rose. At the right tap they relaxed, **straightened** or gave a slight **upward** **jerk** of the head. Each **gesture** was involuntary, and the **sincere** owner, unaware of the **motion**, did not **deceive** anyone. Hans was reading people who signalled **inadvertently**.

The **phenomenon**, now the Clever Hans effect, shows **expectancy** shaping **behavioural** results. Such **bias** explains **double-blind** designs in **psychology** and animal **cognition**. Von Osten died in June 1909, and Hans passed to a **jeweller** sure horses could reason. Pfungst played the part, and **attentive** volunteers fed him the same **cues**.
