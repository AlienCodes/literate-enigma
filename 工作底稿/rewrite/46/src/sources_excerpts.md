# 第46篇 来源摘要（检索摘要，不得直接引用原文）
- S1 Wikipedia "Clever Hans": owner Wilhelm von Osten, a gymnasium mathematics teacher, amateur horse trainer; Hans tapped answers with his hoof (arithmetic, dates, etc.); NYT reported on Hans in 1904. Von Osten exhibited Hans publicly across Germany (free of charge); performances were held in von Osten's Berlin courtyard (susannaforrest.blog 2016: 'von Osten's courtyard in 1904'). Carl Stumpf formed a 13-person "Hans Commission" (a veterinarian, a circus manager, a cavalry officer, several schoolteachers, the director of the Berlin zoological garden); in September 1904 it concluded no tricks were involved.
- S2 Wikipedia/Pfungst 1907: psychologist Oskar Pfungst isolated horse and questioner from spectators, used questioners other than the owner, used blinders to vary whether Hans could see the questioner, varied whether the questioner knew the answer. Horse could answer correctly even when von Osten did not ask. Results: could see questioner → 89% (50 of 56) correct; could not see → 6% (2 of 35). Hans was right only when the questioner knew the answer and Hans could see him.
- S3 Mechanism (Wikipedia/IPM/whole-dog-journal): as taps approached the answer, the questioner's posture and face showed rising tension, which relaxed at the final correct tap; questioner leaned forward slightly at the start and stood taller / slight upward head-jerk when answer reached; cues were involuntary, the trainer unaware.
- S4 Pfungst lab tests: he played the horse; human subjects stood on his right and concentrated on a number; he tapped answers with his right hand, reading their cues.
- S5 Legacy: "Clever Hans effect" — observer-expectancy artefact; reason for double-blind designs; important in animal cognition studies.
- S6 von Osten died June 1909; Hans passed to Karl Krall, an Elberfeld jeweller, who remained convinced horses could reason.
