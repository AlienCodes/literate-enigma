# EN: The Game That Escaped the Soviet Union

In June 1984 Alexey Pajitnov, a 29-year-old Moscow engineer, could not stop playing his own program. His **primitive** Elektronika 60 had a **purely** **textual** display, so its falling blocks were **crude-looking** characters. The **geometric** **pastime** proved **hypnotic**, even **addictive**. Its name, Tetris, joined the Greek tetra-, for each piece's four **tiles**, with tennis.

The **obsession** was his, but the **intellectual** property was not. A **salaried** state worker, Pajitnov had no **entitlement** to the program, whose **proprietor** was his **employer**. **Unofficial** copies, **duplicated** on floppy disks, travelled **freely** across Moscow and Eastern Europe.

In 1986 Robert Stein, an **enterprising** British software **trader**, **stumbled upon** Tetris in Hungary. In **utter** **ignorance** of Elorg, the **state-run** **bureau** that **regulated** software exports, he **hawked** rights he did not yet **legitimately** hold. They passed **onward** through a **chaotic** chain of Western companies, including those of the media **magnate** Robert Maxwell and Henk Rogers.

In February 1989 Rogers, a **Dutch-born** **publisher**, reached Moscow on a tourist **visa** and turned up at Elorg **unannounced**. Stein and Maxwell's son Kevin arrived that same week, leaving Nikolai Belikov, Elorg's chief, **negotiating** with **simultaneous** **bidders**. **Crucially**, Pajitnov **grew fond of** the **genial** Rogers. Nintendo won handheld, then global **console**, rights.

Nintendo **bundled** Tetris with its **newly** launched, **pocket-sized** Game Boy, and that version's **phenomenal** sales passed 35 million.

Pajitnov saw no **remuneration** for years. **Earnings** arrived only after his **prior** contract ran out in 1995 and he took up **residence** in America. In 1996 he and Rogers set up The Tetris Company to **oversee** the **copyright**.

The blocks once owned **solely** by a state now counted their creator among their **guardians**.
