## 英文

In June 1984 Alexey Pajitnov, a 29-year-old Moscow **coder**, grew **engrossed** in a **diversion** he had built. His **primitive** Elektronika 60 had a **purely** **textual** display, so every **geometric** piece of four **tiles** was assembled from characters. The result proved **addictive**. He called it Tetris, a **fusion** of the Greek tetra- and tennis, his favourite **pastime**.

Under the **communist** system, the state, not its **salaried** **designer**, was the **proprietor** of this **intellectual** property, and Pajitnov held no **entitlement** to it. Copies **mushroomed** **freely** on floppy **disks** across the Eastern **bloc**.

In 1986 Robert Stein, an **enterprising** British software **vendor**, **stumbled** upon Tetris in Hungary. In his **ignorance** of Elorg, the state **bureau** **regulating** Soviet software exports, he **brazenly** **hawked** rights he did not yet **legitimately** hold. The rights were **shuffled** **onward** through a **chaotic** **muddle** of **overlapping** deals among Western firms, one owned by the **magnate** Robert Maxwell.

In February 1989 Henk Rogers, a **resourceful** Dutch-born **publisher**, reached Moscow on a tourist **visa** and found his way to Elorg. Stein and Kevin Maxwell, the magnate's son, were in Moscow that same **hectic** week of **negotiations**, each a **bidder**. Pajitnov felt a **fondness** for Rogers, and Nintendo **scooped** the handheld rights, then worldwide **console** rights.

Nintendo **bundled** Tetris with its **pocket-sized** Game Boy, and that version shipped over 35 million copies. Pajitnov **reaped** none of the **spoils** for years. His **earnings** began only after his arrangement's **expiry** around 1995. In 1996 he and Rogers founded The Tetris Company to manage the rights.

The man whose puzzle once spread for nothing later helped decide who could sell it.
