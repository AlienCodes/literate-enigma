# EN: The Robber Who Thought Lemon Juice Made Him Invisible

## 英文

In broad **daylight** on 6 January 1995, McArthur Wheeler and a **confederate** **robbed** two Pittsburgh-area banks at gunpoint, with no **disguise** **whatsoever**. Their only **camouflage** was lemon juice, **rubbed** on their faces in the **utter** **certainty** that it would **foil** the cameras.

The **rationale** had a **kernel** of truth. Lemon juice is a **time-honoured** invisible ink, and Wheeler took the phrase **literally**. A Polaroid **self-portrait** taken **beforehand** came out **mysteriously** **devoid** of its subject. That was **reassurance** enough.

On 19 April a Crime Stoppers slot on Pittsburgh's eleven o'clock news showed **viewers** the **damning** images, and police arrested Wheeler **shortly** after midnight. **Confronted** with the tapes, he protested in **sheer** **disbelief** that he had worn the juice.

**Stumbling** on the story in the 1996 World Almanac, the Cornell psychologist David Dunning **pondered** whether **incompetence**, unlike lemon juice, could **cloak** itself. In 1999 he and his graduate student Justin Kruger published four studies **quizzing** Cornell **undergraduates** on humour, grammar and **logical** reasoning.

**Participants** in the bottom quarter scored around the 12th percentile. Their **flattering** **self-assessments** put them near the 62nd, **whereas** high **achievers** showed **undue** **modesty**. In the authors' account, the skills behind **proficiency** are the very skills needed to judge it, a **dual** burden leaving the **unskilled** blind to their **deficiency**.

**Detractors** of the Dunning-Kruger effect attribute part of the **disparity** to a statistical **illusion**, **namely** **regression** to the mean. Studies designed to avoid it **nevertheless** **confirm** a smaller but **tangible** effect. In 2023 Kruger and Dunning **garnered** the Grawemeyer Award in Psychology. It takes no **know-how** to fault a plan built on **citrus**, and rather more to fault our own.
