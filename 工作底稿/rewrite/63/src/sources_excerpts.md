# 第63篇（柠檬汁劫匪与达克效应）来源摘录（WebSearch，2026-10-03）
S1 Wikipedia "1995 Greater Pittsburgh bank robberies"; officer.com: On 6 January 1995, McArthur Wheeler and Clifton Earl Johnson robbed two Greater Pittsburgh banks at gunpoint, in daylight, with no disguise. They had rubbed lemon juice on their faces, believing it would make them invisible to security cameras (lemon juice can serve as "invisible ink"). Wheeler had tested the idea by taking a Polaroid photograph of himself; he did not appear in the picture, so he trusted the method.
S2 Same: Surveillance images were broadcast in a Pittsburgh Crime Stoppers segment on the 11 p.m. news on 19 April 1995; Wheeler was arrested just after midnight (20 April). Shown the footage, he protested in disbelief that he had worn the lemon juice. Johnson had been arrested a few days after the robberies.
S3 Psychological Science (APS) / Cornell; Dunning interviews: David Dunning, a social psychologist at Cornell University, read about the case (in the 1996 World Almanac) and wondered whether incompetence also hides itself — whether people who lack a skill also lack the ability to see that they lack it.
S4 Kruger & Dunning 1999, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, "Unskilled and Unaware of It": with graduate student Justin Kruger, four studies tested Cornell students on humour, grammar and logical reasoning. Those in the bottom quartile scored around the 12th percentile but estimated themselves around the 62nd. Top performers tended to underestimate their standing. Explanation: a "dual burden" — the skills needed to perform well are the same skills needed to judge performance, so the unskilled cannot recognise their own errors.
S5 Later debate (BPS Psychologist; Frontiers 2022): critics argue part of the pattern is a statistical artefact (regression to the mean); the original paper acknowledged this in part; studies designed to avoid it find a smaller but real effect. Kruger and Dunning received the 2023 Grawemeyer Award in Psychology for the work.
