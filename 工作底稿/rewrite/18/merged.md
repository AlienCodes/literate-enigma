# EN: The Web Beneath the Woods

One of ecology's most **romantic** ideas took root in plastic bags. Canadian researchers **enclosed** paper birch and Douglas fir seedlings in them, then **bathed** the seedlings in carbon dioxide **laced** with traceable carbon. The **tracer** **shuttled** both ways, **apparently** via **fungi** linking the trees' roots, yet the firs **reaped** a **slight** net gain, larger in deeper **shade**.

Most **terrestrial** plants **harbour** such fungi, whose **filaments** **forage** for water and **nutrients** such as **nitrogen**, **swapping** them for sugars. One fungus can **knit** two or more plants into a shared network. The journal Nature **unveiled** the study on 7 August 1997, its cover offering a **catchy** **coinage**: the "wood-wide web".

The **metaphor** proved **irresistible**. Between 2015 and 2021 a forester's **bestseller**, an **acclaimed** novel and an ecologist's **memoir** told readers that trees **converse**, warn one another and, as "mother trees", **nurture** their **kin**. Yet the 1997 trees were **mere** seedlings, not **elderly** mothers.

In February 2023 three researchers **re-examined** the field evidence. They judged it too **sparse**, **inconsistent** or **ambiguous** to **demonstrate** that shared networks are **widespread**. In 26 field studies, networks helped and **hindered** seedlings about equally often, with **neutral** effects most **prevalent**. No **peer-reviewed** field study showed **mature** trees **favouring** their own offspring. **Unsupported** **assertions** in later papers had doubled over 25 years, with **citations** **skewed** towards positive results.

None of this **disproves** the networks' existence. The critics **concede** that carbon and nutrients can **migrate** between trees underground. It is evidence **unearthed** in real forests, not the **allure** of a family **saga**, that must settle how much the networks **convey**, and whether it matters.
