# EN: The Web Beneath the Woods

On 7 August 1997 the journal Nature published a **landmark** Canadian field experiment. Paper birch and Douglas fir seedlings were **enclosed** in bags and fed carbon dioxide **laced** with a rare, traceable form of carbon. The **tracer** revealed a **reciprocal** **transfer**, but the firs **acquired** a **slight** **surplus**, which grew the more **deeply** they were **shaded**.

The carbon was **apparently** **conveyed** by **fungi** **inhabiting** the roots of most **terrestrial** plants. Fungal **filaments** **forage** for water and **nutrients**, taking sugars as **payment**. One fungus can **knit** **multiple** plants into a shared network, which Nature's cover **heralded** as the "wood-wide web".

The **metaphor** proved **irresistible**, and **nuance** a **casualty**. Between 2015 and 2021, **bestsellers** and even an **acclaimed** novel **assured** readers that trees **converse** and warn one another, and that "mother trees" **nurture** their young. Yet the 1997 trees were seedlings, not **mature** mothers.

In February 2023 a sister journal of Nature carried a **sobering** **critique** by three researchers. Claims that shared networks are **widespread**, they found, rested on **sparse**, **inconsistent** field data. In 26 field studies, networks helped and **hindered** seedlings about equally often, with **neutral** effects most **prevalent**. No **peer-reviewed** field study showed trees **favouring** their own offspring. **Publications** citing early studies **betrayed** a **bias** towards positive results; their **unsupported** **assertions** doubled over 25 years.

None of this **disproves** the networks' existence. The critics **readily** **concede** that carbon and nutrients can **shuttle** between trees underground. It is **fieldwork**, not the **allure** of a **tender** tale, that must settle how much the networks carry, and whether that matters.
