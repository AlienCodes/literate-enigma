# EN: The Web Beneath the Woods

On 7 August 1997 the journal Nature **unveiled** a **landmark** finding from a Canadian forest. Paper birch and Douglas fir seedlings were **bathed** in carbon dioxide **enriched** with traceable carbon. The **tracer** flowed both ways, **apparently** **ferried** by fungi linking their roots, yet the firs **reaped** a **slight** net gain, larger in deeper **shade**. Nature's cover offered a **catchy** **coinage**: the "wood-wide web".

Such **fungi**, called mycorrhizal, grow on and inside the roots of most **terrestrial** plants. Their **filaments** **forage** for water and **nutrients** such as **nitrogen**, **swapping** them for the plants' sugars. A fungus **inhabiting** two or more plants **knits** them into a shared network.

The **metaphor** proved **irresistible**. Between 2015 and 2021 a German forester's book, an **acclaimed** novel and a Canadian ecologist's **memoir** became **bestsellers**. **Collectively**, they told readers that trees **converse**, warn one another and, as "mother trees", **nurture** their own seedlings. Yet the 1997 trees had been **mere** seedlings, not **veteran** "mothers".

In February 2023 three researchers **reassessed** the evidence. Data on the networks' **prevalence**, they found, were **inconsistent**, **ambiguous** or **sparse**. In 26 field studies, networks helped and **hindered** seedlings about equally often. No **peer-reviewed** field study had **demonstrated** that **mature** trees **favour** their own young. **Unsupported** claims in later papers had doubled over 25 years, with **citations** **skewed** towards positive results.

**Nonetheless**, the critics **conceded** that carbon and nutrients can **migrate** between trees underground. The **controversy** concerns the **extent** and **significance** of what shared networks **convey**. It is **thorough** **fieldwork**, not the **allure** of a family **saga**, that must settle it.
