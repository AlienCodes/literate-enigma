# EN: The Web Beneath the Woods

Ecology's most **romantic** idea took root in plastic bags. In a Canadian forest, researchers **enclosed** birch and fir seedlings in them and **pumped** in carbon dioxide **laced** with a traceable form of carbon. The **tracer** **shuttled** both ways, **apparently** via **fungi** **entwined** with the trees' roots, but the firs ran a **slight** **surplus**, **particularly** when heavily **shaded**.

Most **terrestrial** plants **harbour** such fungi, whose **filaments** **forage** for water and **nutrients**, **notably** **nitrogen**, in return for sugars. One fungus can **weave** two or more trees into a shared network. The journal Nature **unveiled** the study on 7 August 1997, its cover offering a **catchy** **coinage**: the "wood-wide web".

The **notion** travelled much further than the carbon. Between 2015 and 2021 a forester's **bestseller** and an ecologist's **memoir** **depicted** trees that **converse** and warn one another, and "mother trees" that **nurture** their **kin**. Yet the 1997 trees were seedlings, not **elderly** mothers.

In February 2023 three researchers **re-examined** the field evidence in a sister journal of Nature, finding it too **sparse**, **inconsistent** or **ambiguous** to **demonstrate** that shared networks are **widespread**. In 26 such studies, networks helped and **hindered** seedlings about equally often; **neutral** effects **predominated**. No **peer-reviewed** field study showed **mature** trees **favouring** their offspring. **Unsupported** **assertions** citing the early studies doubled over 25 years, **skewed** towards positive results.

**Nonetheless**, the critics **concede** that carbon and nutrients can **migrate** between trees underground. It is evidence **unearthed** in real forests, not the **charm** of a family **saga**, that must **resolve** the **controversy** over how much shared networks **convey**, and why.
