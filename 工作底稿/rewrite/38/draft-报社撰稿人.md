# EN: The Planet That Was Demoted

On 18 February 1930, comparing **photographic** plates at the Lowell **Observatory**, the **astronomer** Clyde Tombaugh caught a moving dot, a find **heralded** on 13 March 1930. Its name, the **proposal** of an eleven-year-old English girl, Venetia Burney, recalled the **deity** of the **underworld**. For 76 years Pluto was a **fixture** of the **planetary** **roster**.

In January 2005 astronomers found a body, later named Eris, that at first seemed larger than Pluto. NASA briefly called it the tenth planet. The **contender** **provoked** an **awkward** **dilemma**. Astronomers had relied on **vague** **precedent** rather than a **precise** **definition**, and the **embarrassment** could no longer be **ignored**.

On 24 August 2006, the International Astronomical Union, **convened** in Prague, voted on a **motion**. A planet, it **specified**, must **revolve** around the Sun, be nearly **spherical**, and have cleared the **neighbourhood** around its orbit. Pluto failed the third **criterion** and was **reclassified** as a **dwarf** planet, leaving a **remainder** of eight.

The **ballot** was **valid** but **attracted** an **outcry**. Only 424 astronomers, those still in Prague on the last day, **participated**, out of a **membership** of some 10,000. Hundreds signed a **petition**. The American **Dialect** Society named "plutoed", to **devalue**, its word of 2006. **Lawmakers** in New Mexico and Illinois passed resolutions **affirming** Pluto as a planet.

Amid the **controversy**, an **irony** was already in **transit**. New Horizons had been launched on 19 January 2006, seven months before the vote, carrying a **pinch** of Tombaugh's ashes. On 14 July 2015 its first flyby **transmitted** **images** of a **sprawling** **heart-shaped** plain, named Tombaugh Regio. His ashes **glided** **silently** over the heart that bears his name.
