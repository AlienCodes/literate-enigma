# EN: The Secret of Rome's Undying Concrete

Nearly 1,900 years after its **completion** in about AD 125–128, the Pantheon's **graceful** **dome** still **crowns** the **vast** **chamber** beneath it, **defying** the centuries. **Stretching** about 43 metres across, it remains **unrivalled** as the world's largest unreinforced concrete dome, an **astonishing** **testament** to the skill of its builders.

Modern concrete has steel bars **embedded** in it, leaving it **susceptible** to an **insidious** **decay**. Once the steel begins to **rust**, it **swells** and **ruptures** the surrounding concrete; many such structures are often designed for around 50 years. Ancient concrete enjoys no **superiority** in strength over its modern **counterpart**. Its **merit** is **durability**.

Yet one feature of the ancient material once looked like a **shortcoming**. **Sprinkled** through it are small white **chunks** of lime, known as lime clasts. These were long **ascribed** to **sloppy** mixing or **deficient** raw **ingredients**, the kind of **defect** a more **conscientious** builder would surely have **eradicated**.

A study published in Science Advances in January 2023 suggested an **alternative**: the clasts were a **by-product** of "hot mixing" with quicklime, a **reactive** lime that heats in water. When water **seeps** into cracks, the clasts **dissolve** and the calcium recrystallises as calcium carbonate, **plugging** the **crevice**. Within weeks, water **ceased** to **trickle** through cracked hot-mixed samples, yet **persisted** through quicklime-free ones.

The **implications** **extend** beyond **archaeology**. **Manufacturing** cement accounts for about 8 per cent of global carbon dioxide **emissions**, according to a Chatham House report published in June 2018. Longer-lasting buildings could **trim** **renovation**, and emissions with it. The white **lumps** once **blamed** on **negligent** builders have **prompted** a **rethink**.
