# EN: The Secret of Rome's Undying Concrete

Nearly 1,900 years after its **completion**, in about AD 125–128, the Pantheon's **dome** still **arches** over its **temple** with no steel **skeleton**. Some 43 metres across, it remains the world's largest concrete **vault** built without **reinforcement**. It is an **astonishing** **feat** of **architecture**, and a **stubborn** **reproach** to modern engineering.

Today's concrete is usually **strengthened** with steel, which **conceals** a **weakness**. When the bars **rust**, they **swell** and **crack** the concrete around them, and many structures are often designed for around 50 years. Roman concrete is not stronger than its modern **successor**; its **superiority** lies in **longevity**, not **compressive** **muscle**.

For decades, small white **chunks** of lime **dotted** through ancient walls **puzzled** **archaeologists**. These lime clasts were long put down to **sloppy** mixing or **inferior** raw materials, the **residue** of **indifferent** builders. Few thought such apparent **defects** **merited** close **inspection**, let alone a place in any **formula**.

A study published in Science Advances in January 2023 suggested that builders used quicklime, a **reactive** form of lime that gives off heat when it meets water. On this **interpretation**, the clasts were **leftovers** of this "hot mixing". When water **seeps** into a **fracture**, the clasts **dissolve** and the calcium recrystallises as calcium carbonate, **plugging** the gap. In tests, cracked hot-mixed blocks **ceased** to **leak** within weeks; quicklime-free ones kept **trickling**.

The **stakes** are **considerable**. Cement accounts for about 8 per cent of global carbon dioxide **emissions**, according to a Chatham House report published in June 2018. Longer-lasting buildings could **trim** that **proportion**. The white **lumps** once **scorned** as **clumsy** work were, in effect, a **latent** repair **kit**.
