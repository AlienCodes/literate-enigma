# EN: The Secret of Rome's Undying Concrete

The Pantheon's **colossal** **dome** was completed in about AD 125–128, nearly 1,900 years ago. With a **diameter** of about 43 metres, it remains the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world, a **massive** **vault** with no steel inside. Its **remarkable** **endurance** makes the **monument** both a **marvel** and a **stubborn** **puzzle**.

Modern reinforced concrete is more **vulnerable**: its **embedded** steel bars are **prone** to **corrosion**, and as they **rust**, they **swell** and **fracture** the concrete around them. Many such buildings are designed for a **lifespan** of around 50 years. Roman concrete is not stronger; its **merit** is **substantially** greater **durability**.

The concrete's **ostensible** weak point lay in its **mixture**. It is **dotted** with small white **chunks** of lime, known as lime clasts, often a few millimetres wide. For generations, experts put these **lumps** down to **sloppy** mixing or **inferior** raw materials and treated them as **defects**, **unworthy** of **scholarly** attention.

A study published in Science Advances in January 2023 **contradicted** that **assumption**. It suggested the clasts came from "hot mixing" with quicklime, a **reactive** lime that heats up in water. In wet cracks, the clasts **dissolve**, and **calcium** recrystallises as calcium carbonate, a **mineral** **plugging** the **gap**. In tests, hot-mixed samples stopped water **seeping** through within weeks; without quicklime, the **leak** **persisted**.

The **implication** reaches beyond **archaeology**. Cement **manufacture** accounts for about 8 per cent of global carbon dioxide **emissions**, according to a Chatham House report published in June 2018. Longer-lasting materials could **trim** that **proportion** by making **demolition** and **renovation** rarer. The lumps once **ridiculed** as **amateurish** proved a **safeguard** against cracks.
