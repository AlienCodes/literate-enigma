# EN: Eleven Days Without Sleep

On 28 December 1963 Randy Gardner, a 17-year-old science **enthusiast** at a San Diego high school, began a **voluntary** **endeavour** in sleep **deprivation** for a local fair. His **objective** was **straightforward**, to stay **conscious** as long as his **stamina** allowed. Two friends kept him awake and **diligently** recorded his **alertness**.

William Dement, a sleep scientist at Stanford, read about the **audacious** attempt in the **regional** press. **Intrigued**, he drove to San Diego and helped a Navy researcher monitor the boy **closely**. Their **scrupulous** **supervision** offered a **scholarly** chance to **investigate** how **mounting** **fatigue** **impairs** human **cognition**.

The **consequences** soon showed. Gardner grew **moody**, struggled with **concentration** and suffered an **impairment** of **short-term** memory. He became **suspicious** of his **companions**, and his **grip** on what was real **loosened** as he began to hallucinate. On the fourth day he held the **bewildering** **delusion** that he was a professional American football player.

Gardner stopped on 8 January 1964, after 264.4 hours, about 11 days and 25 minutes. He then slept **soundly** for about 14 hours and woke apparently **refreshed**. A 1965 paper on the case helped **highlight** microsleeps, **involuntary** **lapses** into sleep lasting a few seconds. He may have had some, a **reminder** that **tempers** any **boast** of **flawless** **endurance**.

In 1997 Guinness World Records **discontinued** monitoring such **quests**, citing health **perils**, and others have since **unofficially** **professed** longer **stints** awake. In a 2017 interview Gardner described about a decade of severe **insomnia**, which he himself **pinned** on the experiment. The teenager who **bounced back** after one long **slumber** later found it hard to **drift off**.
