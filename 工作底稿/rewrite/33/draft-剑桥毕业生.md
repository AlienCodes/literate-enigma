# EN: Eleven Days Without Sleep

On 28 December 1963 Randy Gardner, a 17-year-old high-school student in San Diego, set out to **forgo** **slumber** for a local science fair. Two **companions** worked **diligently** to **rouse** him whenever his **alertness** **waned** and to keep **tabs** on his **wellbeing**. The **endeavour** would go on to **garner** first place.

Press **coverage** of the attempt **intrigued** William Dement, a sleep researcher at Stanford. He drove to San Diego and **assisted** John J. Ross, a Navy researcher, in **supervising** the **lad**. Together the two **investigators** watched, day after day, how **fatigue** and **exhaustion** **sapped** the boy's **stamina**.

As the **deprivation** **mounted**, Gardner grew **moody**, lost **concentration** and found his **short-term** memory **impaired**. He also became **suspicious** of those around him, and **paranoia** soon **accompanied** hallucinations that **blurred** his **grasp** of what was real. On the fourth day he was **gripped** by the **delusion** that he was a professional American football player.

Gardner **terminated** his **endurance** **quest** on 8 January 1964, after 264.4 hours, about 11 days and 25 minutes. He then slept for about 14 hours and woke apparently recovered. A 1965 **scholarly** paper **probing** the case helped **illuminate** microsleeps, **lapses** into sleep lasting a few seconds. He may have **undergone** such **bouts** **unawares**.

In 1997 Guinness World Records **discontinued** monitoring such attempts over the health **perils**, and **contenders** have since **professed** longer **stints**. In 2017 Gardner told an **interviewer** of severe **insomnia** lasting about a decade, which he himself blamed on the experiment. The teenager who **bounced** back after one **nap** later found **dozing** off a **torment**.
