# EN: Eleven Days Without Sleep

On 28 December 1963 Randy Gardner, a 17-year-old **high-school** student in San Diego, set out to **forgo** **slumber** as his **submission** to a local science fair. Two **companions** **diligently** kept him awake and logged his **mood** and **alertness**. They **rejoiced** when the **endeavour** **garnered** first place.

William Dement, a sleep researcher at Stanford, read press **coverage** of the attempt and grew **curious**. He drove to San Diego, where he **assisted** a Navy researcher, John J. Ross, in monitoring the **sleepless** **lad**. Together they kept an **attentive** eye on him, noting each **fluctuation** in his state as the **deprivation** **progressively** **deepened**.

The **consequences** soon **mounted**. Gardner grew **moody** and **irritable**, while his **concentration** and **short-term** memory began to **deteriorate**. He became **suspicious**, at times **paranoid**, and began to **perceive** things that were not there. On the fourth day he **mistakenly** believed he was a professional American football player, a **bizarre** **delusion**.

Gardner **terminated** the **wearying** attempt on 8 January 1964 after 264.4 hours, about 11 days and 25 minutes. He then slept for about 14 hours and woke apparently **refreshed**. Researchers **probed** the case in a 1965 **scholarly** paper, and it helped **highlight** the **phenomenon** of microsleeps, **momentary**, **involuntary** **lapses** into sleep lasting a few seconds, which he may have had.

In 1997 Guinness World Records stopped **overseeing** such attempts because of the **perils** to **wellbeing**. In a 2017 interview Gardner said severe **insomnia** had **tormented** him for about a decade, and he himself **pinned** it on the experiment. The teenager who had **bounced** back after one long **lie-in** grew into a man who could **barely** **doze**.
