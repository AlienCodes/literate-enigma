# EN: The Virus That Saved a Life

In late 2015 Tom Patterson, a 69-year-old psychiatry professor at UC San Diego, fell ill on holiday in Egypt. The bacterium Acinetobacter baumannii turned **drug-proof** against every **antibiotic**. Flown home by 12 December, he sank when a **surgical** drain slipped. Bacteria **spilled** through his **abdomen** and **besieged** his bloodstream, leaving him **unresponsive** in a **coma**.

The **weapon** that reached him was a **century-old** idea. In 1917, at the Institut Pasteur, Félix d'Hérelle studied a dysentery patient's samples and found a virus that **preys** on **harmful** **germs**. He named it **bacteriophage**, **literally** a bacteria-**eater**, and proposed it as an **antidote** to infection. Twort had reported a similar agent in 1915.

Postwar, antibiotics like penicillin became medicine's **cornerstone**, and the West dropped phage therapy as a **quaint** **precursor**. It survived in Georgia. George Eliava founded a Tbilisi institute in 1923, where d'Hérelle worked from 1933 to 1935. Eliava was executed in 1937. Phage stayed a **standby**, sold **over the counter** like a **cocktail**.

His **spouse** Steffanie Strathdee sought phage researchers. Texas A&M and US Navy teams, which had collected phages from **sewage**, found matches. The **lab-grown**, **rediscovered** phages were **infused** by **vein**. In March 2016 he received them, a US first, and woke three days later. He was **discharged** on 12 August and returned to work.

Antibiotic resistance is a growing danger that has driven a **resurgence** of interest in phages, almost a **renaissance**. A therapy the West had **forsaken** looked a **miracle**. Patterson's **awakening** amid an **escalating** hunt **stirred** respect for d'Hérelle's idea. The phages that **roused** him had been drawn from sewage, and they shamed an age that had dismissed them.
